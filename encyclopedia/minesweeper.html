Minesweeper

A logic game of clearing a field without stepping on a mine.

📜 History🏛️ Historical context⏳ Timeline📖 How to play🎭 Anecdotes💡 Fun facts

📜 History

Minesweeper’s forerunners are computer games of the 1960s and 1970s, such as Cube, and the game Mined-Out (1983) for the ZX Spectrum. The most famous version was programmed by Robert Donner and Curt Johnson for Microsoft.

It was included in Windows 3.1 in 1992 and for decades was installed on hundreds of millions of computers, like Solitaire.

Behind its simple appearance lies deep mathematics: in 2000 it was proved that deciding whether a minesweeper board is consistent is an “NP-complete” problem, one of the hardest in computer science.

🏛️ Historical context

Minesweeper descends from mainframe games of the 1960s and 1970s, such as Cube and Relentless Logic. The Microsoft version, included in Windows 3.1 in 1992, was programmed by Robert Donner and Curt Johnson.

Like Solitaire, it had a practical purpose: teaching people to use the mouse, in this case the left and right clicks. It became one of the most played games in offices around the world.

📖 How to play

  • The field hides mines.
  • Each number shows how many mines are around it.
  • You mark mines with flags.
  • You win when you uncover every square without a mine.

🎭 Anecdotes

In 2001 a campaign called the “International Campaign to Ban Windows Minesweeper” argued that the game trivialised landmines. Microsoft later included an alternative version with flowers instead of mines.

There are minesweeper championships with world records: the best players solve the expert level in under 40 seconds.

💡 Fun facts: did you know…?

  • Microsoft included Minesweeper in Windows so that people could practise using the right mouse button.
  • The expert level has 99 mines on a 30 × 16 grid.
  • Minesweeper has been proved mathematically to be an NP-complete problem.
  • In the Windows version the first click is never a mine.
