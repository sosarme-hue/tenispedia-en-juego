Backgammon

A race game with dice and 15 pieces per player, heir to Persian and Roman games.

📜 History🏛️ Historical context⏳ Timeline📖 How to play🎭 Anecdotes💡 Fun facts

📜 History

Backgammon’s ancestors are very old: the Persian nard and the Roman tabula, mentioned in connection with Emperor Zeno in the 5th century, were already played with dice and fifteen pieces on 24 points. In medieval Spain it was called the game of tables (tablas), and it appears in the Book of Games commissioned by Alfonso X the Wise in 1283.

The name backgammon appears in England in the 17th century. In 1743 Edmond Hoyle published its rules, and in the 1920s the doubling cube was added in New York, letting players raise the stakes and giving the game its modern form.

Today it is very popular in Greece (tavli), Turkey (tavla) and the Middle East, and world championships are held every year in Monte Carlo.

🏛️ Historical context

In imperial Rome, tabula and its forerunner, duodecim scripta, were tavern and gambling games. The emperors Claudius and Nero were great fans; Claudius was said to have written a book about the game and to have had a board fitted in his carriage so he could play while travelling.

In medieval Europe, tables shared the bad reputation of dice: the Church banned them several times because of gambling and fights. Even so, they were as common as chess among the nobility, and many luxury boards had chess on one side and tables on the other.

📖 How to play

  • Each player moves their 15 pieces round 24 points in the opposite direction to their opponent.
  • Two dice are thrown; each number is a move. With doubles you move four times.
  • A single piece alone on a point can be hit and must re-enter the board.
  • The first to bear all their pieces off the board wins.

🎭 Anecdotes

The poet Agathias recorded a disastrous game by Emperor Zeno around the year 480: one throw of the dice left so many of his pieces alone that he lost everything. It is one of the first games of a board game described in detail.

In the 1960s and 1970s backgammon became a craze in the United States and Europe: it was played in discos and fashionable clubs, and the Russian prince Alexis Obolensky, who organised tournaments, was called “the father of modern backgammon”.

💡 Fun facts: did you know…?

  • Alfonso X’s Book of Games (1283) describes fifteen variants of tables games.
  • One theory says backgammon comes from Middle English back and gamen, “back game”, because of the pieces that have to come back in.
  • In Iran it is called nard, and a Persian legend says the vizier Bozorgmehr invented it as a reply to Indian chess.
  • The dice used with Viking and Roman boards looked very much like today’s.
