Senet

One of the oldest known board games, played in Egypt for almost three thousand years.

📜 History🏛️ Historical context⏳ Timeline📖 How to play🎭 Anecdotes💡 Fun facts

📜 History

Senet already appears in Egyptian tombs of the Predynastic period and the First Dynasty, around 3100 BC. Its name means “passing” or “crossing”. It was played on a board of 30 squares arranged in three rows of ten, with pieces that moved according to the throw of sticks or knucklebones used as dice.

Over time the game took on a religious meaning: moving across the board was linked to the soul’s journey through the afterlife, and so senet boards were buried with the dead. Several were found in Tutankhamun’s tomb, and the tomb of Nefertari has a famous painting of the queen playing.

The original rules have not survived. The ones used today are reconstructions proposed by 20th-century historians such as Timothy Kendall and R. C. Bell, based on the boards and inscriptions.

🏛️ Historical context

The Egypt in which senet was born was that of the unification of Upper and Lower Egypt and the first pharaohs. It was a very hierarchical society, where the leisure of nobles and officials included hunting, music, banquets and board games. The richest boards were made of ebony, ivory or imported woods, but workers played too: boards have been found scratched into stone slabs and on temple ramps.

In the New Kingdom (1550–1070 BC) the game became steeped in religion. The Book of the Dead, the collection of spells that went with the deceased, mentions playing senet in the afterlife, and the last squares of the board are decorated with symbols of gods and of the waters of the Nile, representing trials on the soul’s journey to Osiris.

⏳ Timeline

c. 3100 BC

First depictions of senet boards in Predynastic tombs.

c. 2600 BC

Paintings of nobles playing in Old Kingdom mastabas, such as that of Hesy-Ra at Saqqara.

c. 1323 BC

Tutankhamun is buried with four senet boards, one with a drawer to keep the pieces.

c. 1250 BC

Queen Nefertari is painted in her tomb playing alone, a scene read as a game against an invisible opponent: fate.

1st century

The game disappears as Roman games spread through Egypt.

20th century

Kendall and Bell propose the rules used today.

📖 How to play

  • Each player has between 5 and 7 pieces that travel round the board in an S shape.
  • You move according to the throw of four two-sided sticks.
  • Some of the last squares have special symbols that help or punish.
  • The first to bear all their pieces off the board wins.

🎭 Anecdotes

Many senet boards were double-sided boxes: one side had the 30 squares of senet and the other a 20-square board for another game, the “game of twenty squares”, related to the Royal Game of Ur. In a way, they were the first compendiums of games.

An Egyptian text known as the Turin Papyrus includes a satirical scene in which a lion and a gazelle play senet. Animals took human roles in these vignettes, and the picture has been seen as one of the oldest caricatures in history.

💡 Fun facts: did you know…?

  • Four senet boards were found in Tutankhamun’s tomb, one of them made of ebony and ivory.
  • The pieces usually came in two different shapes, some like cones and others like spools, rather than in two colours.
  • Square 15 was called the “house of rebirth” and square 26 the “house of beauty”, according to inscriptions on some boards.
  • There were no cube-shaped dice in Egypt at that time: players threw four flat sticks or knucklebones (lamb ankle bones).
