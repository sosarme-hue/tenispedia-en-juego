Hide and seek

One player counts with eyes closed while the others hide.

📜 History🏛️ Historical context⏳ Timeline📖 How to play🎭 Anecdotes💡 Fun facts

📜 History

In the 2nd century the Greek writer Julius Pollux describes a game called apodidraskinda that is almost identical to hide and seek: one child closes their eyes while the others hide, and then goes to look for them.

In Spain it is called escondite, escondite inglés (when you have to freeze) or tula in some variants. It is played in almost every culture in the world.

There is even a hide and seek world championship, held in Italy since 2010.

🏛️ Historical context

In the 2nd century the Greek writer Julius Pollux describes a game called apodidraskinda, in which one player covered their eyes and the others hid. It is the first description of hide and seek that we know of.

Hide and seek is found in every country and period, and it is one of the games most studied by psychologists: it helps young children understand that things go on existing even when we can’t see them.

📖 How to play

  • One player is “it” and counts out loud.
  • The others hide.
  • Whoever is “it” must find them and get back to “home” first.
  • You can “free” the others by reaching home without being seen.

🎭 Anecdotes

The famous painting Children’s Games, painted in 1560 by Pieter Bruegel the Elder, shows more than 80 children’s games of the time, including hide and seek. It can be seen today in the Kunsthistorisches Museum in Vienna.

Italy hosts a hide and seek world championship, Nascondino, in which teams hide in a village for a set time.

💡 Fun facts: did you know…?

  • In many places, the last player to be found can shout “for me and all my friends!” and free the whole group.
  • The Spanish phrase “por mí y por todos mis compañeros” (for me and all my friends) frees the whole team.
  • In Spanish it is called escondite, from esconder, “to hide”.
  • Whether you count to 10 or to 100 depends on the group.
