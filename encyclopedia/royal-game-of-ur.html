Royal Game of Ur

A Sumerian race game whose rules could be reconstructed thanks to a Babylonian clay tablet.

📜 History🏛️ Historical context⏳ Timeline📖 How to play🎭 Anecdotes💡 Fun facts

📜 History

In the 1920s the British archaeologist Leonard Woolley found several boards decorated with shell, lapis lazuli and red limestone in the Royal Cemetery of the Sumerian city of Ur, dating from around 2600 BC. One of them is now on display in the British Museum.

The game spread across the whole Near East for more than two thousand years and reached India, where a variant was still played in the city of Cochin until the 20th century.

The rules are partly known thanks to a cuneiform tablet from 177 BC written by the Babylonian scribe Itti-Marduk-balāṭu, which the British Museum curator Irving Finkel translated in the 1980s.

🏛️ Historical context

Ur was one of the great city-states of Sumer, near the mouth of the Euphrates. Around 2600 BC its kings were buried surrounded by jewellery, musical instruments, chariots and servants, in tombs that Woolley excavated between 1922 and 1934 and that caused a sensation in the press of the time, almost as much as Tutankhamun’s tomb.

The game was not just for kings. Boards of twenty squares have been found almost everywhere in the Near East, from Iran to Cyprus and from Syria to Egypt, and were even carved by Assyrian soldiers into the paving of the palace gate at Khorsabad, showing that they passed their guard duty playing.

📖 How to play

  • Each player has 7 pieces that must travel along the 20-square board.
  • Four pyramid-shaped dice are thrown; each marked tip counts 1.
  • If you land on an opponent’s piece in the central lane, you send it back to the start.
  • Rosette squares give another throw and protect the piece.

🎭 Anecdotes

The Babylonian tablet that explains the game also uses it to tell the future: each square had an omen, such as “you will find a friend” or “you will be powerful like a lion”, depending on where the piece landed.

In India, in the Jewish community of Cochin, an almost identical game of twenty squares was played until the middle of the 20th century. When that community emigrated to Israel, the game stopped being played, ending a tradition more than four thousand years old.

💡 Fun facts: did you know…?

  • In 2017 Irving Finkel played a filmed game against YouTuber Tom Scott to make the game better known.
  • The dice were tetrahedrons, four-sided pyramids with two marked tips.
  • The five rosettes on the board are among the most recognisable decorations in Sumerian art.
  • Finkel discovered that the Assyrian soldier who carved a board at Khorsabad did so beneath the feet of a giant winged stone bull.
