Mikado (pick-up sticks)

A game of skill that consists of removing sticks from a pile without moving the others.

📜 History🏛️ Historical context⏳ Timeline📖 How to play🎭 Anecdotes💡 Fun facts

📜 History

Games of removing sticks from a pile have forerunners in Asia and among the indigenous peoples of the Americas, who used sticks for divination. In China, bamboo rods were used to consult the I Ching, the “Book of Changes”.

In Europe it became popular in the 19th century under the name jonchets in France and mikado in Germany and Austria. The name mikado, the title of the emperor of Japan, comes from the most valuable stick, which was blue and bore that name.

In Spain it is known as mikado or palillos chinos (“Chinese sticks”), and in the United States as pick-up sticks.

🏛️ Historical context

In 19th-century Europe, the fashion for orientalism and “Japonisme” meant that many objects and games were given Asian names. Gilbert and Sullivan’s comic opera The Mikado (1885) increased the popularity of the name.

It was a parlour game for family afternoons, and it was made with sticks of wood, bone or ivory.

📖 How to play

  • You hold all the sticks and let them fall in a pile.
  • Taking turns, you remove a stick without moving any other.
  • If another stick moves, your turn passes.
  • Each stick is worth points according to its colour, and whoever scores most wins.

🎭 Anecdotes

In France the game was called jonchets, and in the 18th century the sticks were made of ivory or carved bone, sometimes in the shape of tools, weapons or animals.

Some people play using two sticks to lift the others, like chopsticks, although in the classic game a special stick is used.

💡 Fun facts: did you know…?

  • The most valuable stick in the classic game, the mikado, is blue and there is only one.
  • A classic set has 41 sticks.
  • You can use one stick to help lift another.
  • Mikado is a good test of a steady hand and patience.
