Xiangqi (Chinese chess)

Chinese chess, with a river across the middle of the board and pieces placed on the intersections.

📜 History🏛️ Historical context⏳ Timeline📖 How to play🎭 Anecdotes💡 Fun facts

📜 History

Xiangqi is a relative of chess that probably also comes from Indian chaturanga, although some Chinese historians argue for a local origin. Its present form was fixed during the Song dynasty, between the 10th and 13th centuries.

The pieces are discs with Chinese characters and are placed on the intersections, not in the squares. The board has a river in the middle and a 3 × 3 palace on each side, which the general cannot leave.

By number of players it is one of the most played games in the world, especially in China, Taiwan and Vietnam, where it is common to see games in parks and streets.

🏛️ Historical context

Under the Song dynasty, China went through an urban revolution: huge cities, printing, paper money and a lively cultural life in theatres and teahouses. In that setting xiangqi became a popular game across all social classes, while go remained the game of scholars.

The pieces reflect the Chinese imperial army: the general with his advisors in the palace, the elephants defending the territory, the horses, the chariots, the soldiers and the cannon, a piece that could only exist in a country that already knew gunpowder.

📖 How to play

  • Each side has a general, advisors, elephants, horses, chariots, cannons and soldiers.
  • The cannon captures by jumping over exactly one piece.
  • Elephants cannot cross the river.
  • Whoever checkmates the opposing general wins.

🎭 Anecdotes

The pieces of each side have different characters even when they do the same job: the red general is called shuai and the black one jiang, and the elephants and soldiers are also written differently depending on their colour.

In Southeast Asia, Vietnam has a tradition of “human” xiangqi, in which the pieces are costumed people moving on a large board in the town square during Lunar New Year festivities.

💡 Fun facts: did you know…?

  • The two generals may not “face each other” on the same file with no pieces between them.
  • The river in the middle usually bears the words “Chu River, Han border”, in memory of the war between those two kingdoms.
  • Xiangqi has 90 playing points compared with the 64 squares of chess.
  • Soldiers gain a sideways move once they cross the river.
