Carcassonne

A tile-laying game of building cities, roads and monasteries in the landscape of southern France.

📜 History🏛️ Historical context⏳ Timeline📖 How to play🎭 Anecdotes💡 Fun facts

📜 History

Carcassonne was designed by the German Klaus-Jürgen Wrede and published in 2000 by Hans im Glück. Wrede was inspired by a trip to the walled medieval city of Carcassonne in the south of France.

In 2001 it won the Spiel des Jahres, the German game of the year award, and it became one of the great classics of modern board games, along with Catan. More than 10 million copies have been sold.

Its mechanic of drawing a tile and placing it next to the others is so simple that almost anyone can play it, and it has given rise to dozens of expansions.

🏛️ Historical context

In the late 1990s, after the success of Catan, German board games were enjoying a golden age. Publishers were looking for quick, attractive, easy-to-explain games for the whole family.

The city of Carcassonne, with its double walls and 52 towers, was restored in the 19th century by the architect Viollet-le-Duc and has been a World Heritage Site since 1997.

📖 How to play

  • Each turn you draw a tile and place it next to those already laid, matching roads, cities and fields.
  • You can place a “meeple” (little figure) on the tile to claim a road, a city or a monastery.
  • When a feature is completed, the points are counted.
  • Whoever has most points when the tiles run out wins.

🎭 Anecdotes

The term “meeple”, a blend of my and people, was coined by the American player Alison Hansel in 2000 to refer to the Carcassonne figures. Today it even appears in dictionaries.

Many Carcassonne expansions add curious elements: dragons, princesses, a river, catapults or a tower.

💡 Fun facts: did you know…?

  • The word “meeple”, used today for all board game figures, became popular thanks to Carcassonne.
  • The basic game has 72 tiles.
  • Every game creates a different landscape.
  • The real city of Carcassonne has 52 towers and two rings of walls.
