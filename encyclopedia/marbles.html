Marbles

Little balls flicked with a finger to hit others or knock them into a hole.

📜 History🏛️ Historical context⏳ Timeline📖 How to play🎭 Anecdotes💡 Fun facts

📜 History

Little stone and clay balls used for games have been found in Egyptian tombs and at sites in the Indus Valley. Roman children played with nuts, and the Latin expression relinquere nuces (“to leave the nuts behind”) meant to grow up.

Ceramic marbles were mass-produced in Germany in the 19th century, and in 1846 a German glassmaker invented special shears to make them from glass. In the 20th century American glass marble factories arrived.

In Spain they have many names depending on the region, such as bolas, boliches, pitos or caniques, and there are many different games: the gua (hole), the triangle and the foot.

🏛️ Historical context

The oldest stone and clay marbles, more than 4,000 years old, have been found in Egypt and the Indus Valley. Roman children played with walnuts and hazelnuts, and the poet Ovid describes several games with them in his poem Nux.

In 19th-century Germany a special pair of shears was invented for making glass marbles, which became much cheaper. In the 1930s American factories produced them by the million on machines.

📖 How to play

  • You flick the marble with your thumb.
  • In gua, you must get your marble into a hole in the ground.
  • By hitting an opponent’s marble you can win it.
  • Whoever wins the most marbles or reaches the hole first wins.

🎭 Anecdotes

In the English village of Tinsley Green a world marbles championship has been held for almost a century. According to local legend, in the 16th century two young men competed there for a girl’s hand in a game of marbles.

In Spain marbles have dozens of names depending on the region: bolinches, boliches, pitos, mecas, chivas, bolinchas, gua…

💡 Fun facts: did you know…?

  • A world marbles championship has been held in the United Kingdom since 1932.
  • The gua is the hole in the ground that you have to get the marbles into.
  • Marbles with coloured spirals are called “lecheras” (milkmaids) in some parts of Spain.
  • Bigger marbles, the “gordas”, are worth more in swaps.
