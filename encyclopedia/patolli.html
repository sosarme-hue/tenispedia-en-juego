Patolli

A Mesoamerican race game with marked beans as dice, much loved by the Aztecs.

📜 History🏛️ Historical context⏳ Timeline📖 How to play🎭 Anecdotes💡 Fun facts

📜 History

Patolli is a race game on a cross- or X-shaped board with 52 squares. It was played throughout Mesoamerica, from Teotihuacan to the Maya, and boards carved into the floors and stone benches of many ancient cities have been found.

In Aztec times it was a passion. Spanish chroniclers such as Friar Diego Durán and Friar Bernardino de Sahagún told how people bet blankets, jewels, quetzal feathers and even their own freedom. Before playing, they called on Macuilxóchitl, the god of games.

After the Spanish conquest the friars banned it because of its link with the old gods, and boards and beans were even burned in public. Even so, the game survived for a while in some communities.

🏛️ Historical context

Mesoamerican cultures had a cyclical view of time, with interlocking calendars of 260 and 365 days. Many scholars believe the patolli board represented that time and that playing was also a way of relating to the gods.

The Aztecs were great players and gamblers: besides patolli they had the ballgame, ullamaliztli, on which great riches were also wagered.

📖 How to play

  • It is played on a cross-shaped board of 52 squares.
  • The dice are five beans with a dot on one side.
  • You move according to the dots that come up, and some squares reward or punish.
  • The first to complete the track with all their stones wins, and objects are staked on each game.

🎭 Anecdotes

According to Friar Diego Durán, players carried their beans and stones in a little basket and treated them almost as sacred objects: they spoke to them and offered them incense before the game for luck.

The chroniclers say that some players lost everything and ended up selling themselves into slavery to pay their gambling debts.

💡 Fun facts: did you know…?

  • The 52 squares match the number of years in the Aztec “century”, the cycle after which the calendar started again.
  • The dice were beans marked with a hole or a white dot.
  • Coloured stones served as pieces.
  • A similar game, India’s pachisi, has no connection with it: it is a case of independent invention.
