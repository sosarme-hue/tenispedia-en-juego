Cuatrola

A four-player version of tresillo, the most elegant card game of 18th- and 19th-century Spain.

📜 History🏛️ Historical context⏳ Timeline📖 How to play🎭 Anecdotes💡 Fun facts

📜 History

Cuatrola comes from tresillo, which in turn descends from the “game of the man” (juego del hombre), a 17th-century Spanish card game. Hombre became fashionable at the European courts: in France it was called l’hombre and in England ombre, and during the 18th century it was the most prestigious card game in Europe.

Tresillo is the three-player version, and cuatrola adapted it for four. In it one player “makes the game” and chooses trumps, and can ask a partner for help in exchange for sharing the winnings.

Today cuatrola survives mainly in Asturias, León and Galicia, where there are still clubs and casinos that play it and organise tournaments.

🏛️ Historical context

In the 18th century, playing hombre or tresillo well was a sign of good breeding, like dancing or conversing gracefully. It was played in the drawing rooms of the nobility and bourgeoisie all over Europe.

In the 19th century tresillo was the game par excellence of Spanish social clubs. It appears in many novels of the period, in which gentlemen spend their afternoons at the village club among tricks and arguments.

📖 How to play

  • It is played with the 40-card Spanish deck, dealt among the four players.
  • Players bid to be the one who makes the game. The winner of the bidding chooses the trump suit.
  • The player can call a partner, who helps them during that hand.
  • There are three special cards called matadores: the espadilla (ace of swords), the malilla and the basto (ace of clubs).
  • Whoever makes the game must win more tricks than each of their opponents to collect.

🎭 Anecdotes

In the poem The Rape of the Lock (1714 version), the Englishman Alexander Pope narrates a game of ombre in full detail as if it were an epic battle, with the Spanish cards turned into warriors.

Many of the game’s terms passed into other languages untranslated. In English, for example, people said “spadille” for espadilla and “basto” for the ace of clubs.

💡 Fun facts: did you know…?

  • The game of hombre was so called because the player who chose trumps and faced the others was “the man”.
  • In the black suits (swords and clubs), the order of the cards changes compared with the red suits (coins and cups).
  • Tresillo and cuatrola have a very rich vocabulary of their own: “solo”, “bola”, “codillo”, “puesta”…
  • The Spanish expression “dar codillo” comes from these games: it is when an opponent wins more tricks than the player making the game.
