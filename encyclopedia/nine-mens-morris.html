Nine men’s morris

A strategy game in which you form lines of three pieces, “mills”, to capture your opponent’s pieces.

📜 History🏛️ Historical context⏳ Timeline📖 How to play🎭 Anecdotes💡 Fun facts

📜 History

Nine men’s morris is played on a board of three squares one inside the other, joined by lines, with 24 points. The oldest securely dated boards are Roman; boards have also been found on the Viking ship from Gokstad, in Norway, from the 9th century.

In the Middle Ages it was one of the most popular games in Europe. Alfonso X describes it in his Book of Games as “alquerque of nine”, and in medieval England it was called nine men’s morris. Boards are carved into the benches of the cathedrals of Canterbury, Gloucester and Westminster.

Today it is still widely played in Germany (Mühle), the Netherlands and northern Europe, and it is part of almost every compendium of games.

🏛️ Historical context

In the Middle Ages board games like nine men’s morris could be played almost anywhere: you only had to carve the lines in a stone or draw them in the earth. That is why they appear in cloisters, castles, ships and even on church steps, where pilgrims waited.

Shakespeare mentions it in A Midsummer Night’s Dream, where a character complains that mud has filled up the board marked out on the village green.

📖 How to play

  • Each player has nine pieces, placed in turn on empty points.
  • When you form a line of three of your own pieces, a “mill”, you remove one of your opponent’s pieces.
  • Once all are placed, pieces move to an empty neighbouring point.
  • You lose if you are left with two pieces or cannot move.

🎭 Anecdotes

In rural England villagers cut the morris board into the turf on a large scale and used stones as pieces. That is why A Midsummer Night’s Dream says “the nine men’s morris is filled up with mud”.

In Germany it is so popular that many parks and squares have giant versions, with pieces you move by hand.

💡 Fun facts: did you know…?

  • In 1993 the computer scientist Ralph Gasser proved that, with perfect play, nine men’s morris ends in a draw.
  • There are variants with three, six, nine and twelve pieces per player.
  • The board has 24 points.
  • Under some rules, when a player has three pieces left they can “fly” to any point.
