Brisca

A simple, quick trick-taking game with the Spanish deck, in which the ace and the three are worth the most.

📜 History🏛️ Historical context⏳ Timeline📖 How to play🎭 Anecdotes💡 Fun facts

📜 History

Brisca comes from a 17th-century French game called brusquembille, which passed to Italy as briscola and to Spain as brisca. It is one of the most widespread card games in Mediterranean countries and Latin America.

Its simplicity has made it the first card game many children learn in Spain, and also a classic of village social clubs and retirement homes.

Tute and guiñote belong to the same family, with declarations and slightly more complex rules.

🏛️ Historical context

Brisca spread around the Mediterranean in the 18th century, in Bourbon Italy and Spain, and was the game of taverns and neighbours’ gatherings. Its simplicity and speed made it very popular in the countryside, where it was played on winter afternoons.

It belongs to the large family of trick-taking games with trumps: one suit ranks above the others and cards have point values, just as in tute, its close relative.

📖 How to play

  • 3 cards are dealt and the trump card is turned up.
  • Each player plays one card; the highest card or a trump wins the trick.
  • Values: ace 11, three 10, king 4, knight 3, jack 2.
  • Whoever scores more than 60 of the 120 points wins.

🎭 Anecdotes

In Italy, especially in the south, briscola is almost a national game, and there are proverbs that mention it. In Cuba and Puerto Rico it is played with the Spanish deck and with signal rules similar to those of mus.

The most valuable cards are the ace and the three: a three can be worth more than a king, which surprises anyone playing with the Spanish deck for the first time.

💡 Fun facts: did you know…?

  • In Italy briscola is played with permitted signals between partners, as in mus.
  • There are 120 points in total; to win you need more than 60.
  • The card shown at the start sets the trump suit.
  • It can be played by two, three, or four in pairs.
