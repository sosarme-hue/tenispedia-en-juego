Shogi (Japanese chess)

Japanese chess, in which captured pieces can come back into play on your side.

📜 History🏛️ Historical context⏳ Timeline📖 How to play🎭 Anecdotes💡 Fun facts

📜 History

Shogi is also descended from chaturanga and probably reached Japan by way of Southeast Asia. The oldest pieces found, at the Kōfuku-ji temple in Nara, date from the 11th century.

Its present form, on a 9 × 9 board, was established in the 16th century. The novelty that makes it unique is the “drop” rule: the pieces you capture become yours and you can later place them on the board.

In the Edo period the shogunate protected the families of shogi masters, and today there is a professional league with a large following in Japan.

🏛️ Historical context

Early shogi coexisted in Japan with enormous versions: dai shogi had a 15 × 15 board and taikyoku shogi, 36 × 36, had 402 pieces per side. They were display games more than games actually played.

The long, peaceful Edo period (1603–1868) favoured the arts and games. Shogi masters received a salary from the shogunate and each year played a ceremonial game before him, the oshogi. This tradition created families of masters, such as the Ōhashi and the Itō, who passed the title down.

📖 How to play

  • The pieces are wedge-shaped and point towards the opponent; they have no colour.
  • Captured pieces can be dropped back on the board instead of making a move.
  • On entering the opponent’s zone, many pieces can promote.
  • Whoever checkmates the king wins.

🎭 Anecdotes

In 2017 Sōta Fujii, aged just 14, won 29 official games in a row, a record that caused a real “Fujii effect” in Japan: sales of shogi sets rose and even the snacks he ate during games sold out in the shops.

The rule of reusing captured pieces is sometimes explained by Japanese history: in wars between feudal lords, it was common for defeated soldiers to switch to the winning side.

💡 Fun facts: did you know…?

  • Because pieces change sides, a shogi game almost never ends in a draw for lack of material.
  • The pieces have their names written in Japanese characters; when they promote they are turned over to show another character, often in red.
  • You may not give checkmate with a pawn dropped from your hand.
  • Professional shogi games can last two days.
