Chess

The king of strategy games: two armies of 16 pieces and the aim of giving checkmate.

📜 History🏛️ Historical context⏳ Timeline📖 How to play🎭 Anecdotes💡 Fun facts

📜 History

The ancestor of chess is chaturanga, played in northern India around the 6th century. Its name means “four limbs”, after the four divisions of the army: infantry, cavalry, elephants and chariots. From India it passed to Persia, where it was called chatrang, and then the Arabs spread it as shatranj.

It reached Europe through al-Andalus and Sicily around the year 1000. Some of the first treatises were written in Spain: the Book of Games of Alfonso X (1283) and, above all, that of Luis Ramírez de Lucena (1497). At the end of the 15th century, in the Iberian Peninsula itself, the queen and the bishop gained their long moves and modern, faster chess was born.

International tournaments began in the 19th century, and in 1886 the first official world championship was played, won by Wilhelm Steinitz. In 1997 IBM’s computer Deep Blue beat the champion Garry Kasparov, a historic moment.

🏛️ Historical context

Chaturanga was born in the India of the Gupta dynasty, a golden age for mathematics, astronomy and literature. Its pieces reflected the army of the time: war elephants were a decisive weapon and chariots were still used in battle.

In medieval Europe, chess became the game of the nobility and of knights. The pieces changed to represent feudal society: the Arab vizier became the queen, the elephant became the bishop in English (and alfil in Spanish, from the Arabic al-fil, “the elephant”), and the chariot became the rook or castle. Moralists wrote books that used the pieces to explain how each social class should behave.

In the Cold War, chess became a political symbol. The Soviet Union dominated the world championship for decades, and the victory of the American Bobby Fischer over Boris Spassky in Reykjavík in 1972 was seen as a clash between the two superpowers.

📖 How to play

  • Each player has a king, a queen, two rooks, two bishops, two knights and eight pawns.
  • White moves first. Each piece has its own way of moving.
  • You capture a piece by moving onto its square.
  • Whoever gives checkmate wins: the opposing king is attacked and cannot escape.

🎭 Anecdotes

A very famous legend tells that the inventor of chess asked the king of India for a reward of one grain of wheat on the first square, two on the second, four on the third and so on up to the 64th. The king laughed and agreed, until his mathematicians worked out that more than 18 quintillion grains were needed, more wheat than exists in the world.

The Lewis chessmen, a set of 78 pieces of walrus ivory carved in Norway around the 12th century, were found in 1831 on a beach on the Isle of Lewis in Scotland. Their rooks are warriors biting their shields, like Viking berserkers, and today they are among the treasures of the British Museum.

In 1770 the Hungarian inventor Wolfgang von Kempelen presented the Turk in Vienna, an automaton that played chess and beat almost everyone. For 80 years it toured Europe and America and played against Napoleon and Benjamin Franklin. The secret: a strong human player was hidden inside the cabinet.

💡 Fun facts: did you know…?

  • The word “checkmate” comes from the Persian shāh māt, meaning “the king is helpless”.
  • The number of possible chess games, known as the Shannon number, is estimated at 10 to the power of 120.
  • The first printed chess book in Spain, Lucena’s, was published in Salamanca in 1497.
  • The longest possible official game could have almost 6,000 moves because of the 50-move rule.
  • Chess clocks have been used in tournaments since the second half of the 19th century.
