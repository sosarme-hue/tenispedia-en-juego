Code-breaking games

Deduction games in which you have to guess a secret combination of colours or numbers.

📜 History🏛️ Historical context⏳ Timeline📖 How to play🎭 Anecdotes💡 Fun facts

📜 History

The pencil-and-paper game bulls and cows, about guessing four-digit numbers, was already played in the 19th century.

In 1970 the Israeli Mordecai Meirowitz created a version with coloured pegs, published by the British company Invicta Plastics under the name Mastermind, a registered trademark that sold millions in the 1970s.

In 1977 the computer scientist Donald Knuth proved that any combination of 4 colours out of 6 can be guessed in five tries or fewer.

🏛️ Historical context

The game of cracking a colour code with clues has a forerunner in a pencil-and-paper game called bulls and cows, played with numbers. In 1970 the Israeli Mordecai Meirowitz, a postmaster and telecommunications expert, created the version with coloured pegs.

It was turned down by several toy companies until the British firm Invicta Plastics marketed it in 1971. In the 1970s it won the game of the year award in the United Kingdom and more than 50 million copies were sold.

📖 How to play

  • One player hides a secret combination.
  • The other proposes combinations.
  • They are told how many are right and in the right place, and how many are right but in the wrong place.
  • Whoever cracks it in the fewest tries wins.

🎭 Anecdotes

The cover of the original box showed a bearded man in a suit and an Asian woman. The models were two people from the Leeds area, where the factory was; the picture became so famous that it turned into a 1970s icon.

In 1977 the computer scientist Donald Knuth published a study proving that any code in the classic game of 4 positions and 6 colours can be cracked in at most five tries.

💡 Fun facts: did you know…?

  • In bulls and cows, a “bull” is a correct digit in the right place and a “cow” a digit that is there but in another position.
  • There are 1,296 possible codes in the classic game of 4 positions and 6 colours.
  • Black pegs show the right colour in the right place; white pegs, the right colour in the wrong place.
  • In computer science it is used as an example of search problems.
