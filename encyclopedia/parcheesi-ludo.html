Parcheesi (ludo)

A race game with dice and four pieces per player; in Spain, parchís is the great family classic.

📜 History🏛️ Historical context⏳ Timeline📖 How to play🎭 Anecdotes💡 Fun facts

📜 History

Parchís comes from pachisi, the national game of India, whose name means “twenty-five”, the highest throw. In the 16th century the Mughal emperor Akbar the Great played it at the palace of Fatehpur Sikri on a giant marble board, using people dressed in colours as pieces.

The British brought it to Europe in the 19th century, where it was simplified with an ordinary die: in England it was patented as Ludo in 1896, in Germany Mensch ärgere Dich nicht was created (1914), and in Spain it became popular as parchís at the beginning of the 20th century.

Spanish parchís has its own rules, such as blockades, safe squares, and bonuses of 20 and 10 squares for capturing and for reaching home.

🏛️ Historical context

Akbar’s Mughal India (1556–1605) was a cosmopolitan empire, with a court that blended Persian, Hindu and Muslim traditions. Pachisi and its relative chaupar were played by all social classes, on cross-shaped cloth boards with cowrie shells as dice.

In Victorian England, interest in the “exotic” side of the colonies led many Indian games to be adapted and sold. The industrial revolution made it possible to print cheap cardboard boards, and race games became the family pastime of Sunday afternoons.

📖 How to play

  • Each player has 4 pieces in their base.
  • You come out with a 5. With a 6 you throw again.
  • If you land on an opponent’s piece outside a safe square, you capture it and move 20.
  • The first to get all 4 pieces home wins.

🎭 Anecdotes

At the palace of Fatehpur Sikri, near Agra, you can still see a courtyard whose floor is laid out as a giant pachisi board. According to tradition, Akbar and his guests played from a dais while young people in coloured clothes acted as pieces.

Mensch ärgere Dich nicht (“Don’t get angry, man”) became popular in Germany during the First World War because its creator gave thousands of copies to military hospitals to entertain wounded soldiers.

💡 Fun facts: did you know…?

  • Akbar’s pachisi was played with people as pieces in a palace courtyard.
  • In the original pachisi six or seven cowrie shells were thrown and those landing mouth up were counted.
  • Spanish parchís has 68 squares on the shared track.
  • In Latin America it is also called parqués, and in Colombia it has its own rules.
