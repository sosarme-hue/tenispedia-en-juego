Ludus latrunculorum

The Romans’ “game of little soldiers”, a capture strategy game played on a grid.

📜 History🏛️ Historical context⏳ Timeline📖 How to play🎭 Anecdotes💡 Fun facts

📜 History

Ludus latrunculorum or latrunculi, the “game of soldiers” or “of mercenaries”, was the great strategy game of ancient Rome. It was played on grids of varying sizes, such as 8 × 8 or 7 × 8, with pieces of glass, bone or stone.

Latin authors mention it often: Varro, Ovid and Martial refer to it, and an anonymous poem, the Laus Pisonis, praises a certain Piso for being a great player. It probably comes from a Greek game called petteia, already mentioned by Plato.

The complete rules have not survived. Modern reconstructions assume that pieces moved like the rook in chess and captured by trapping an enemy piece between two of their own.

🏛️ Historical context

In Rome, gambling games of chance were frowned upon and sometimes banned, but strategy games like latrunculi were considered fitting for educated people. Soldiers took it all over the Empire, from Hispania to Syria.

Boards were carved into the steps of forums and basilicas: in the Roman Forum and the Basilica Julia you can still see game boards cut into the marble by citizens who were waiting.

📖 How to play

  • Each player has about 16 pieces on a grid.
  • Pieces move in straight lines.
  • An enemy piece is captured if it is trapped between two of your pieces.
  • Whoever captures the most pieces or blocks the opponent wins.

🎭 Anecdotes

The philosopher Seneca tells that a man called Julius Canus, sentenced to death by the emperor Caligula, was playing latrunculi when they came for him. Before leaving he counted the pieces and told his opponent: “Don’t claim after my death that you won.”

In the grave of a man buried at Stanway, England, in the 1st century, a game board was found with its pieces set out, as if the game had been left unfinished.

💡 Fun facts: did you know…?

  • Latrunculi boards carved by Roman soldiers have been found on Hadrian’s Wall in Britain.
  • The pieces were called calculi, the origin of the word “calculate”.
  • The word latro originally meant “mercenary”.
  • There were portable wooden boards for travelling.
