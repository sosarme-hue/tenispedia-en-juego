Guiñote

The great card game of Aragon: played in pairs, with trumps, declarations of twenty and forty and plenty of signals between partners.

📜 History🏛️ Historical context⏳ Timeline📖 How to play🎭 Anecdotes💡 Fun facts

📜 History

Guiñote is a close relative of tute and brisca, and like them it belongs to the large European family of trick-taking games with trumps, in which cards are worth points. It is played with the 40-card Spanish deck.

It is the most popular card game in Aragon, and it is also widely played in La Rioja, Navarre and Soria. In many Aragonese villages there are championships during the local festivities, and the guiñote tables at pensioners’ clubs are always busy in the afternoons.

Its name comes from guiño, “wink”: partners pass each other information with facial signals, such as winking, biting their lip or raising their eyebrows, to say which cards they hold without their opponents noticing.

🏛️ Historical context

In 20th-century rural Aragon, guiñote was the main entertainment of village cafés and social clubs, especially in winter, when there was little work in the fields. A game of guiñote was also a way of getting together and talking.

Today it has become a symbol of Aragonese identity. Tournaments are organised with hundreds of pairs, and there are also versions for playing online.

📖 How to play

  • It is played in pairs, with partners sitting opposite each other. Each player receives six cards.
  • The last card of the pack is placed face up and sets the trump suit, which beats the others.
  • The cards are worth: ace 11 points, three 10, king 4, jack 3 and knight 2. The rest are worth nothing. In guiñote the jack beats the knight.
  • Whoever holds the king and knight of the same suit can declare twenty, or forty if they are trumps.
  • When the pack runs out you must follow suit and win the trick if you can (the “arrastre”). The pair that passes 100 points wins the game.

🎭 Anecdotes

Veteran guiñote pairs understand each other with a single gesture. That is why, in many tournaments, the referees make sure no exaggerated signals are made, although discreet ones are part of the game.

An Aragonese saying goes that “guiñote is played with your face”: anyone who cannot keep a straight face or read their partner’s gestures will find it very hard to win.

💡 Fun facts: did you know…?

  • Guiñote signals are so important that in many places they are learned almost at the same time as the rules: winking, poking out the tip of your tongue or pursing your lips indicate particular cards, and each district has its own code.
  • Winning the last trick gives ten extra points, the “diez de últimas”.
  • If neither pair reaches 101 points in the first hand, another is played, called “de vueltas”.
  • In guiñote you can swap the seven of trumps for the face-up card.
