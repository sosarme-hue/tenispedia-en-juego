Hnefatafl (Viking game)

The Vikings’ board game: a king and his defenders try to escape from an army that surrounds them.

📜 History🏛️ Historical context⏳ Timeline📖 How to play🎭 Anecdotes💡 Fun facts

📜 History

Hnefatafl (“king’s table” or “fist table”) belongs to the family of tafl games, played in Scandinavia, Iceland, Ireland and the British Isles from the 4th century until the arrival of chess around the 11th century.

It is an asymmetric game: one side, with a king in the centre, must get the king to a corner of the board; the other, with twice as many pieces, tries to surround and capture him.

The exact rules were lost. In 1732 the Swedish naturalist Carl Linnaeus described in his travel diary of Lapland a variant called tablut that the Sami still played, and the modern rules were reconstructed from it.

🏛️ Historical context

In Viking society board games were a symbol of status and intelligence. The sagas say that a good warrior had to know how to play tafl, and some poems list it among a nobleman’s skills.

Boards and pieces have been found in warriors’ graves, sometimes alongside their weapons, as at Birka (Sweden) and in Orkney, showing that the game went with the dead into the afterlife.

📖 How to play

  • The king starts in the centre with his defenders; the attackers start on the edges.
  • All pieces move in straight lines like the rook in chess.
  • A piece is captured by trapping it between two enemy pieces.
  • The king wins if he reaches a corner; the attackers win if they surround him on all four sides.

🎭 Anecdotes

Linnaeus wrote his description of tablut in Latin, and a 19th-century translator got it wrong, producing absurd rules that were played for years until the original text was reviewed.

At the Ballinderry site in Ireland a 10th-century wooden board was found with holes for pegs, so it could be played on a ship or a journey without the pieces moving.

💡 Fun facts: did you know…?

  • The Icelandic sagas tell of games of tafl between heroes, some of which end in a fight.
  • The attackers have about 24 pieces and the defenders 12 plus the king.
  • Tafl means “table” or “board” in Old Norse.
  • Today there are international tafl championships.
