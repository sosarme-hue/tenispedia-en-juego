The Spanish deck

The deck of coins, cups, swords and clubs, heir to the first playing cards that reached Europe.

📜 History🏛️ Historical context⏳ Timeline📖 How to play🎭 Anecdotes💡 Fun facts

📜 History

Playing cards reached Europe from the Islamic world, probably through the Mamluks of Egypt, at the end of the 14th century. In 1371 the word naip appears in a Catalan dictionary, one of the first European mentions, and in 1377 they were banned in Florence.

The suits of the Spanish deck (oros, copas, espadas and bastos: coins, cups, swords and clubs) come directly from the Mamluk suits: coins, cups, scimitars and polo sticks. They are called Latin suits and are also used in Italy.

The Spanish deck has 40 cards (48 with eights and nines) and no queens: the court cards are the jack (sota), the knight (caballo) and the king. It is used to play mus, brisca, tute, chinchón and cinquillo.

🏛️ Historical context

Playing cards reached Europe at the end of the 14th century, probably from the Mamluk sultanate of Egypt, whose cards had suits of cups, coins, swords and polo sticks. The Iberian Peninsula and Italy were among the first places to adopt them, which is why their decks keep those four suits.

Cards quickly became both a business and a problem. Kings banned them many times because of gambling, but in the end they chose to tax them: for centuries card making was a Crown monopoly, controlled by the Royal Treasury, and every deck had to carry an official stamp.

📖 How to play

  • Four suits: coins, cups, swords and clubs.
  • Cards from 1 (ace) to 7, plus court cards: jack (10), knight (11) and king (12).
  • The 48-card version includes eights and nines.
  • The breaks in the border show the suit.

🎭 Anecdotes

Heraclio Fournier’s cards use a design commissioned in 1877 from the painter Augusto Ríus. The pattern became so popular that today, when we think of the Spanish deck, we almost always picture those figures, with the ace of coins bearing the maker’s name.

The Fournier Playing Card Museum in Vitoria holds one of the largest collections in the world, with decks from every continent and period, some from the 15th century.

💡 Fun facts: did you know…?

  • The gaps in the card’s border let you recognise the suit without seeing the whole card: one for coins, two for cups, three for swords and four for clubs.
  • The word naipe may come from the Arabic nā’ib, “deputy”, the name of a court card in Mamluk decks.
  • The Spanish deck has 40 or 48 cards: the 40-card version has no eights or nines.
  • The sota represents a page or squire, which is why he is shown standing.
