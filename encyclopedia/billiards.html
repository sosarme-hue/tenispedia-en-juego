Billiards

A game of striking balls with a cue on a table covered in green cloth.

📜 History🏛️ Historical context⏳ Timeline📖 How to play🎭 Anecdotes💡 Fun facts

📜 History

Billiards was born as an indoor version of croquet in northern Europe in the 15th century: the table’s green cloth imitated grass. King Louis XI of France had a billiard table around 1470.

At first the ball was struck with a mace; the cue took over in the 18th century and the leather tip was invented around 1820, which made it possible to put spin on the ball.

Today there are many forms: carom (without pockets, very popular in Spain), pool, and snooker, born in British India in 1875.

🏛️ Historical context

Billiards arose in northern Europe in the 15th century as a table version of croquet, a lawn game with balls and mallets. That is why billiard cloths are green, in memory of the grass.

In the 17th and 18th centuries billiards became fashionable at courts and in palaces, and in the 19th century in cafés and clubs. The tipped cue, which replaced the mace, and the leather tip invented by the Frenchman François Mingaud around 1807 made spin shots possible.

📖 How to play

  • You strike the white ball with the cue.
  • In pool you must pot the balls in the pockets.
  • In carom your ball must touch the other two.
  • Whoever completes their objective first wins.

🎭 Anecdotes

In 1863 an American manufacturer offered 10,000 dollars to anyone who could invent a material to replace the ivory in billiard balls, which was very expensive. The inventor John Wesley Hyatt created celluloid, one of the first plastics, although the first balls sometimes exploded when they collided.

It is said that Louis XIV was a great billiards fan and that his doctor recommended it to him as exercise to help his digestion.

💡 Fun facts: did you know…?

  • Mozart was a great billiards fan and had a table at home.
  • There are many forms: carom, pool, snooker, French billiards…
  • Snooker was invented in India in 1875 by British officers.
  • The cloth is made of high-quality wool, often merino.
