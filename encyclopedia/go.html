Go

A Chinese strategy game of surrounding territory with black and white stones. One of the deepest games in the world.

📜 History🏛️ Historical context⏳ Timeline📖 How to play🎭 Anecdotes💡 Fun facts

📜 History

Go, called wéiqí in China and baduk in Korea, is the oldest board game still played with rules almost the same as the original ones. Legends credit it to Emperor Yao, around 2000 BC, but the first reliable written references date from the 4th century BC, in texts such as the Analects of Confucius and the work of Mencius.

It was one of the four arts of the Chinese gentleman, together with calligraphy, painting and music. It reached Japan in the 7th century, where professional schools funded by the shogunate were created in the Edo period.

In 2016 the program AlphaGo, by the company DeepMind, beat South Korea’s Lee Sedol, one of the best players in the world. It was a milestone for artificial intelligence, because it was thought that go would take decades to be mastered by a machine.

🏛️ Historical context

In the China of the Warring States (5th–3rd centuries BC), go was seen as an exercise in military strategy and character: you had to give way on one side to win on another, an idea that fitted well with Confucian and Taoist thought. The oldest surviving board, with 17 × 17 lines, was found in a Han dynasty tomb.

In Japan, go was a pastime of the Heian court (9th–12th centuries) and appears in works such as The Tale of Genji. Later, in 1612, the Tokugawa shogunate created an official post, the godokoro, and four houses or schools (Honinbō, Inoue, Yasui and Hayashi) that trained professional players and competed in games before the shogun.

📖 How to play

  • It is played on a board of 19 × 19 lines (or 13 × 13 or 9 × 9 for learning).
  • Players take turns to place a stone on an empty intersection.
  • Stones that are surrounded with no liberties are captured.
  • Whoever controls more territory at the end wins.

🎭 Anecdotes

A Chinese legend tells of a woodcutter who stopped to watch two old men playing go on a mountain. When they finished the game, the handle of his axe had rotted away: a hundred years had passed. The story is used to say that go makes you lose track of time.

In 1846 the young Honinbō Shūsaku played the so-called “ear-reddening game”: a doctor watching noticed that the ears of his opponent, Gennan Inseki, turned red after one of Shūsaku’s moves, a sign that he knew he was lost.

Move 37 in the second game between AlphaGo and Lee Sedol was so unexpected that commentators thought it was a mistake. It turned out to be brilliant, and Lee Sedol’s move 78 in the fourth game was the only one with which a human beat it in that match.

💡 Fun facts: did you know…?

  • There are more possible positions on a go board than atoms in the observable universe.
  • Traditional Japanese go stones are made of black slate and white clamshell.
  • In Korean go is called baduk, and it is so popular that there are TV channels devoted to it.
  • Black plays first, and to make up for it White receives some bonus points called komi.
