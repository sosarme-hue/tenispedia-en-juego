Leapfrog (pídola)

The game of vaulting over a crouching friend, with your hands on their back, known in English as leapfrog.

📜 History🏛️ Historical context⏳ Timeline📖 How to play🎭 Anecdotes💡 Fun facts

📜 History

Leapfrog is one of the oldest and most widespread jumping games in Europe. One player crouches, hands on knees and head tucked in, and the others jump over them by pushing off their back with their hands.

In the famous painting Children’s Games (1560) by the Flemish painter Pieter Bruegel the Elder, which shows more than eighty children’s games, there are children jumping over one another.

There are many versions: in some, each jumper crouches on reaching the end of the line and a chain forms that keeps moving; in others, rhymes are sung while jumping and the crouching player gradually gets higher.

🏛️ Historical context

In the Middle Ages and the Renaissance, children played jumping, racing and chasing games in streets and squares, many of which have come down to us almost unchanged.

Leapfrog was also practised in school gymnastics in the 19th and 20th centuries as an exercise to strengthen the legs, before vaulting horses became common in gyms.

📖 How to play

  • One player crouches with hands on knees, back straight and head down.
  • The others take a run-up and jump over them, pushing off their back with their hands.
  • Whoever fails to jump or knocks over the crouching player becomes the next one to crouch.
  • In some versions, the crouching player rises a little each round to make the jump harder.
  • In the chain version, each jumper crouches when they finish and the line keeps moving forward.

🎭 Anecdotes

The gym vaulting horse, the apparatus you vault over with your hands, is in a way leapfrog without a person: it replaces the crouching friend.

In English the verb “to leapfrog” means to overtake someone by skipping steps, and it is used in economics and technology.

💡 Fun facts: did you know…?

  • In the British army, the expression leapfrog was used to describe units advancing by overtaking one another in turn.
  • The Spanish word pídola has an uncertain origin. In some regions people say píndola or pídula.
  • In French it is called saute-mouton, “sheep-jump”, and in English leapfrog.
  • It is one of the few games played the same way across almost all of Europe.
