📻 The games of 1960s Spain

In 1960s Spain, most children spent their afternoons playing in the street, in the village square or on the waste ground of their neighbourhood. Toys were few and many were made at home, but there was no shortage of imagination. Meanwhile, the grown-ups filled the bars with card and domino games, and on Sunday afternoons the football pools results were heard on the radio.

🏃 The street as a playground

With little traffic in many streets, children played hide-and-seek, the handkerchief game, hopscotch, which many called “el truque” or “la charranca”, and skipping, accompanied by songs passed from one girl to another.

The more daring boys played churro, media manga, mangotero and leapfrog, games of jumping on friends’ backs that often ended with a grazed knee. Blind man’s buff was the game for parties and birthdays.

🔵 Pocket treasures

Marbles were almost a currency: they were won, lost, swapped and kept in cloth bags. Glass ones with coloured patterns inside were the most valuable. Bottle caps became cyclists or footballers with a paper cut-out and a little plasticine, and whole cycling tours were organised on the pavement.

The wooden spinning top, with its metal tip and its string, had its seasons, just like marbles and trading cards. The most skilful could make it spin on the palm of their hand.

🏠 At home with the family

At home people played parcheesi and the game of the goose, which often came on the same board, one on each side. On winter afternoons the cards came out for brisca, cinquillo or seven and a half, and at Christmas for julepe with chickpeas.

Television had just arrived in many homes and was beginning to change habits, but it was still common for the family to gather round the table with a board game or a deck of cards.

🍺 The grown-ups’ games

In bars and social clubs, the grown-ups played mus, tute, dominoes and chinchón, with arguments that could be heard from the street. Table football, invented in Spain by Alejandro Finisterre, was spreading through bars and amusement arcades.

And every weekend, millions of people filled in the quiniela (football pools) hoping to get all fourteen results right and change their lives. Listening to the results on the radio with the coupon in hand was a Sunday ritual.

The games in this article

🔮
Antiquity

Marbles

Little balls flicked with a finger to hit others or knock them into a hole.

🍾
20th century

Bottle caps (chapas)

A street game with bottle caps flicked with a finger in races or football matches drawn on the ground.

🌀
c. 3500 BC

Spinning top

A wooden top with a metal tip that spins when you throw it with a string.

🪢
Antiquity

Skipping rope

Skipping, alone or in a group, with songs that keep the rhythm: a playground game of every age.

🦶
Antiquity or Middle Ages

Hopscotch

A game of hopping on one foot through squares drawn on the ground.

🏃
traditional

The handkerchief (el pañuelo)

Two teams, a handkerchief in the middle and a number: whoever grabs it has to get back to their side without being caught.

🐎
traditional

Churro, media manga, mangotero

One team plays the donkey and the other jumps on top. Then you have to guess: churro, media manga or mangotero?

🦘
Middle Ages

Leapfrog (pídola)

The game of vaulting over a crouching friend, with your hands on their back, known in English as leapfrog.

🐔
Antiquity

Blind man’s buff

A blindfolded player tries to catch the others and guess who they are. Goya painted it, and it was already played in ancient Greece.

🎲
16th century

Parcheesi (ludo)

A race game with dice and four pieces per player; in Spain, parchís is the great family classic.

🪿
16th century

Game of the Goose

A 63-square spiral track with geese, bridges, a well and a skull. Pure chance and tradition.

🤫
18th century

Mus

Spain’s most popular partnership card game, full of signals, all-in bets (órdagos) and bluffs.

⚽
1920s–1930s

Table football

Football on a table, with figures fixed to rods that you spin.

📝
1946

La quiniela (football pools)

The game of predicting football results with a 1, an X or a 2, a Spanish tradition since 1946.
