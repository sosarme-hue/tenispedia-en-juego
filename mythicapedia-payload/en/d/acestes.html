hero of Roman myth, king of Sicily

Acestes

Acestes is a hero of Roman myth, a Sicilian king of Trojan descent and the founder of Segesta. His role as host of Aeneas in Sicily makes him a notable figure in Virgil's Aeneid.

The myth

According to Roman tradition, Acestes (also Egestes, a name said to mean "pleasing goat") was the son of the Sicilian river-god Crinisus and a Dardanian or Trojan woman called Egesta or Segesta. The commentator Servius explains that her father sent her to Sicily so she would not be devoured by the monsters plaguing Troy, a punishment for the city's refusal to reward Poseidon and Apollo for building its walls. Once on the island, Crinisus, appearing as a bear or a dog, fathered Acestes, who became king of the region and was remembered as the founder of Segesta.

In the Aeneid, Acestes welcomes Aeneas when he reaches Sicily on his way to Italy. There the funeral games for Anchises, Aeneas's father, are held. In a contest of skill, Acestes shoots an arrow that bursts into flame in the sky, a sign from Jupiter that the poet reads as a favourable omen. When Aeneas departs, Acestes gives him wine as a farewell gift. Companions who do not wish to travel further stay behind and, with his people, found the city of Acesta, that is, Segesta.

Dionysius of Halicarnassus tells a different and far less supernatural story. A Trojan noble sailed with the daughters of a man slain by Laomedon, who had been sent away with merchants, and married one of them in Sicily. Their son Aegestus later joined the Trojan War, returned to Sicily and founded a city bearing his name. Many scholars see this version as a rationalised reading of the older legend.

Legacy

The name of Acestes is linked to the Sicilian city of Segesta, which tradition credited to him or to his people.

Ancient sources: Virgil's Aeneid, with the commentary of Servius, and the Roman Antiquities of Dionysius of Halicarnassus.

Trivia

Flaming arrow. The arrow that burns in the air is read as a sign from Jupiter favouring Acestes, who is honoured by the prodigy during the games.
Two different origins. Servius presents him as the son of a river-god who takes animal form; Dionysius of Halicarnassus, by contrast, tells a story with no divine intervention.
