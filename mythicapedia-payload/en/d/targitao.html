mythic first king and ancestor of the Scythians

Targitaos

Targitaos is, according to Scythian tradition, the first king and ancestor of this steppe people north of the Black Sea. The Greeks identified him with Heracles and, at Olbia, with Achilles Pontarkhes.

The myth

According to Herodotus, Targitaos was the son of Zeus and a daughter of the Borysthenes river, today the Dnieper. He had three sons, Lipoxais, Arpoxais and the youngest, Colaxais. During the reign of the three brothers, four golden objects fell from the sky: a plough, a yoke, a sagaris and a cup.

In another version recorded by the historian, Heracles stole the cattle of Geryon and crossed Scythia driving a chariot pulled by mares. Some authors gave Papaios, the sky father, a role close to that of Zeus, and the mother of the line was a snake-legged goddess.

Worship and religion

In the Hylaea region, near the Borysthenes, a sixth-century BC inscription records an open-air shrine with altars to Targitaos, called Heracles there, to the Mother of the Gods and to the river god. At Olbia, the Greeks worshipped Achilles Pontarkhes, linked with Targitaos, and dedications by priests and archons suggest a state cult.

Legacy

Herodotus recorded his genealogy, and Greeks linked Achilles with Scythia. Alcaeus of Mytilene called Achilles the lord of Scythia.

Ancient sources: Herodotus (Histories, Book IV) recounts the Scythian genealogy; Eumelus and Alcaeus of Mytilene connect Achilles with this land.

Trivia

Objects fallen from the sky. According to the myth, a plough, a yoke, a sagaris and a golden cup fell in Scythia during the reign of the three brothers.
Heracles on the steppe. The Greeks equated Targitaos with Heracles, and in one version the hero drives a chariot pulled by mares instead of walking.
