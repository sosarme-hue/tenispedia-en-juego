Norse god and giant of wisdom

Mímir

Mímir is a giant and god in Norse mythology, famed for his knowledge and keeper of a well of wisdom beneath one of the roots of Yggdrasil. He matters chiefly because, after his death, his severed head kept counselling Odin.

The myth

According to the myth, Mímir guarded the well that bears his name, Mímisbrunnr, where wisdom flowed to anyone who could pay the price. The sources place it in the realm of the frost giants, beneath one of the three roots of the world ash Yggdrasil. The Völuspá says he drinks mead there each morning, and the Prose Edda has him use the Gjallarhorn to draw water from its depths.

Odin wanted to drink from that well, and Mímir demanded one of his eyes in exchange, which the god gave. Later came the war between the Æsir and the Vanir. In Snorri Sturluson's account, both sides made a truce and swapped hostages: the Æsir sent Mímir to Vanaheimr in exchange for Kvasir. Convinced they had been cheated, the Vanir beheaded him and sent his head to Asgard. Odin embalmed it with herbs and spoke charms over it, so that the head could speak to him and reveal secrets from other worlds.

At Ragnarök, according to the Völuspá, Odin rides to the well to seek counsel, and Mímir's head advises him. Snorri also presents him as a counsellor to Hœnir, though Hœnir would only answer 'let others decide' whenever Mímir was not at his side. Scholars have proposed that he was the brother of Bestla, Odin's mother, which would make him Odin's maternal uncle.

Worship and religion

The sources describe no temples, festivals or rituals devoted to Mímir. His figure appears mainly in the stories and in the poetic names of the cosmic ash and its well, and his veneration has not survived as a living practice.

Legacy

Mímir appears in the video games God of War (2018) and God of War Ragnarök (2022), where he is voiced by Scottish actor Alastair Duncan. His name comes from a Proto-Indo-European root linked to thinking and remembering, the same root behind the idea of memory; scholars render it as 'the rememberer, the wise one'.

Ancient sources: The Völuspá, Sigrdrífumál and Fjölsvinnsmál of the Poetic Edda; Gylfaginning and Skáldskaparmál of the Prose Edda, and Ynglinga saga in Heimskringla.

Trivia

A name meaning memory. His name is linked to a Proto-Indo-European root meaning to think or remember, the same family as the English word memory.
Another name for Yggdrasil. In the poems, the ash Yggdrasil is also called Mímameiðr, the tree of Mímir.
