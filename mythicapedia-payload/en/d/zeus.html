Zeus

Zeus is the chief of the gods of ancient Greece and king of Mount Olympus, a god of the sky, thunder and lightning. He embodies the cosmic and political order of the Greeks, and his name comes from the same Indo-European root as the ancient god of the daytime sky.

The myth

According to Hesiod's Theogony, Zeus was the son of the Titans Cronus and Rhea. Cronus had overthrown his own father, Uranus, and swallowed his children at birth, fearing a prophecy that one of them would dethrone him. Desperate, Rhea turned to her parents, Gaia and Uranus, for a plan. When she gave birth to Zeus in Crete, she handed the newborn to Gaia and offered Cronus a stone wrapped in swaddling clothes, which he swallowed without noticing the trick.

The place of his birth varies by author. Hesiod places it at Lyctus, the poet Eumelus locates it in Lydia, Callimachus in Arcadia, and Apollodorus in a cave on Mount Dicte. Diodorus Siculus also mentions Mount Ida. Pausanias remarked that it would be impossible to count all the people who claimed Zeus had been born or raised among them.

According to Apollodorus, during his infancy the nymphs Adrasteia and Ida nursed him on the milk of the goat Amalthea, while the Kouretes beat their spears on their shields so that Cronus could not hear the baby crying. Other versions give Themis or the sacred bees of the cave the role of nurses. Antoninus Liberalis tells how a band of thieves who tried to steal honey from that cave were turned into birds when they saw the infant's swaddling clothes.

As an adult, Zeus married Hera, but his many love affairs produced numerous gods and heroes. The sources do not always agree on the genealogies: the Iliad makes Zeus and Dione the parents of Aphrodite, while the Theogony names Metis as his first wife and the mother of Athena.

Worship and religion

Zeus was worshipped across the Greek world, and four major regional traditions stand out: the Zeus Lykaios of Arcadia, the Pelasgian Zeus of Dodona, the Zeus of Mount Dicte in Crete, and the Panhellenic Zeus of Olympia, patron of the Olympic Games. His sacrifices usually consisted of goats, bulls and cows, and the eagle, the oak and mountain peaks were sacred to him. His epithets reflect facets of his authority: Xenios protected guests, Horkios watched over oaths, and Agoraios punished dishonest traders in the marketplace. Phidias's statue of Olympian Zeus fixed the usual image of the god as a king armed with the thunderbolt.

Legacy

The name of Zeus survives in the Latin Iuppiter, the source of Jupiter, whom the Romans identified with him. Zeus and Jupiter share the Indo-European root dyew-, which also gives the Spanish words for 'god' and 'day', though by different etymological routes. The thunderbolt and the eagle remain his most recognisable emblems, and the sceptre, a trait inherited from the Ancient Near East, appears in his artistic representations.

Ancient sources: Hesiod's Theogony, Homer's Iliad, the Rigveda with the god Dyaus Pita, Pausanias, Apollodorus, Diodorus Siculus, and Plato's Cratylus.

Trivia

A etymology from Plato. In the Cratylus, Plato proposes a folk etymology linking the name of Zeus to life, although modern scholars do not accept it.
A goat that nursed a god. The goat Amalthea appears in the childhood story of Zeus as his nurse, and the epithet Aigiochos has been linked to this legend.
