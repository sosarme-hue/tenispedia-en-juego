culture hero who founded Pallantium in Roman mythology

Evander

Evander is a culture hero of Roman mythology, an Arcadian who led his people to Latium and founded Pallantium on the future Palatine Hill. He was credited with bringing Greek religion, music, farming and the alphabet to ancient Italy, and his story gave Rome an illustrious mythical past.

The myth

According to tradition, Evander was the son of Hermes (Mercury) and a nymph whom Dionysius of Halicarnassus identifies with Carmenta. Other authors name his mother Timandra and his father Equemus of Tegea. Accounts differ on why he left Greece: some say it was his own choice, others that he was exiled after the death of his father or mother, and some mention civil unrest in Arcadia.

In Italy he was welcomed by King Faunus but had to fight the giant Erulus, king of Praeneste, whom he defeated after three battles thanks to a protection granted by the goddess Feronia. Once established, he founded Pallantium, named after his grandfather. He taught the natives the worship of Greek gods, the lyre, farming and herding, and writing, which myth credits to him and his mother.

In Virgil's Aeneid, Evander is a wise old king who helps Aeneas against Turnus. His son Pallas dies in the fighting, and the father's grief becomes one of the poem's most moving moments.

Worship and religion

After his death Evander was deified, and an altar was built to him on the Aventine Hill, near the Porta Trigemina, where annual sacrifices were offered. His mother Carmenta had a similar altar at the foot of the Capitol. According to Pausanias, an Arcadian temple with his statue stood in Pallantium. He also erected the Great Altar of Hercules in the Forum Boarium.

Legacy

The Evander myth allowed leading Roman families, such as the gens Fabia, to claim divine origins and descent from Arcadia. He appears in literature through the Aeneid and in other classical authors such as Strabo.

Ancient sources: Virgil presents him in the Aeneid, Dionysius of Halicarnassus and Pausanias discuss him in detail, and Ovid and Strabo also refer to him.
