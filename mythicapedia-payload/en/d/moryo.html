collective spirit of Japanese folklore

Mōryō

Mōryō is a collective term for spirits of mountains, rivers, trees and rocks, as well as mononoke, kappa and other yōkai. Their origin lies in China, from where they passed to Japan with their own traits.

The myth

According to Chinese tradition, mōryō were spirits of nature. The Huainanzi describes them as having the shape of a three-year-old child, dark red in color, with red eyes, long ears and beautiful hair.

The Compendium of Materia Medica adds that they feed on the innards of the dead and, underground, eat their brains. They fear tigers and oak: it is said they die if an oak branch is pressed against their necks.

In Japan they are often confused with the kasha, the yōkai that steals corpses. In the Mimibukuro essay from the Edo period, a retainer of an official named Shibata declares he is a mōryō and resigns, because it is his turn to steal a corpse in a certain village. The next day he vanishes, and at a funeral in that village dark clouds cover the place; when they clear, the body has disappeared from the coffin.

Ancient sources: The Huainanzi and the Compendium of Materia Medica, from the Chinese tradition, and the Mimibukuro essay by Negishi Shizumori, from Edo-period Japan.

Trivia

Clouds at the funeral. In the Mimibukuro story, dark clouds cover a funeral and, when they clear, the corpse is gone from the coffin.
A servant who resigns. The servant tells his master he is not human but a mōryō and resigns because it is his turn to steal a corpse. The next day he has vanished.
