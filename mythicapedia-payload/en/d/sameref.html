Egyptian god of death

Sameref

Sameref (‘His beloved son’) is an Egyptian god of death associated with the pharaohs of the Third Dynasty. Over time his role came to resemble that of Horus, son of Osiris.

The myth

According to the funerary texts, Sameref was understood as the divine embodiment of the deceased king, who, like Horus, loves his own father. In the Middle Kingdom he was believed to count the hearts of the dead, and early in the New Kingdom he appeared in Horus form before the Ennead of Heliopolis to lead the dead before the great council of deities.

In the Late Period he was linked to Qebehsenuf, one of the four sons of Horus who guarded the intestines of the deceased, and in the Graeco-Roman era to Imhotep, Anubis and Horus, all described as beloved sons of their father. He could occasionally, though rarely, be called a son of Ra.

Worship and religion

His name appears in the Pyramid Texts within the expanded opening-of-the-mouth ceremony for the deceased, listed among the priests and officiants who performed the rite alongside the son of the deceased.

Ancient sources: The Pyramid Texts and the Book of the Dead (a speech by the priest Sameref in the papyrus of Ani).

Trivia

Leopard skin. He was shown wearing a priestly cloak of leopard or cheetah skin and the side lock of youth. From the New Kingdom he was theologically drawn close to Iunmutef.
Names of priests. The Pyramid Texts mention several officiants with similar names, such as Sem, Smer and Am-as, alongside Sameref.
