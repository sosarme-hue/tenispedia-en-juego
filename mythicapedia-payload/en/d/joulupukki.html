Finnish Father Christmas, the "Christmas goat"

Joulupukki

Joulupukki, the "Christmas goat", is Finland's version of Father Christmas. Its figure grew out of old goat-costumed mummers and winter spirits, and today it is the central character of Christmas in Finland.

The myth

In its earliest form, the Christmas Goat was a fearsome creature. Men dressed in animal skins and goat horns went from house to house demanding leftover Christmas food and frightening those they met. According to tradition, these dark spirits came to take rather than to give. How the creature became the kindly Father Christmas is unclear; it was probably a slow blend of folk beliefs and customs, and from 1927 radio programmes helped shape the modern image: red robes, a walking stick, reindeer and a home at Korvatunturi.

In the most widespread version of the story, Joulupukki lives at Korvatunturi near the Russian border, while his workshop is in Rovaniemi. He travels by sleigh pulled by reindeer. He is often said to have a wife, Joulumuori ("Mistress Christmas"), remembered mainly for her oat-and-rice porridge, though tradition says little more about her.

Worship and religion

On Christmas Eve, the father of the family, or a neighbour or relative, dresses as Joulupukki and enters through the front door. He asks "Onkos täällä kilttejä lapsia?" ("Are there any good children here?"), and the children, dressed as tonttus, answer with an enthusiastic "Siiii!" before he hands out the presents. Unlike the chimney visits of Santa Claus, this in-person visit still survives in Finland.

Legacy

The song "Rudolph the Red-Nosed Reindeer", in its Finnish translation, has made Rudolph the lead reindeer of Joulupukki's sleigh team. Joulupukki is also a prominent character in Rare Exports, Jalmari Helander's film based on his award-winning short films. Each year, his office in Rovaniemi receives letters from children all over the world.

Trivia

A name with history. The Finnish word "pukki" comes from the Swedish "bock", meaning deer or billy goat.
Letters from around the world. According to the Finnish broadcaster Yleisradio, his Rovaniemi office received about 700,000 letters from children around the world in 2006.
