Aztec god of the night sky, obsidian and conflict

Tezcatlipoca

Tezcatlipoca was a central deity of Aztec religion, linked to the night sky, hurricanes, obsidian and conflict. He was counted among the four sons of the primordial couple Ometecuhtli and Omecihuatl, and his name, often rendered as "smoking mirror", refers to the obsidian mirrors used in rituals and divination.

The myth

According to Nahua traditions, Tezcatlipoca and Quetzalcoatl created the world from a primordial ocean inhabited only by the earth monster Cipactli. Tezcatlipoca offered his foot as bait; the monster bit it off, and the two gods then tore its body apart to form the earth, its eyes becoming lakes and its skin the fields.

The sources give differing genealogies. In the Historia de los toltecas por sus pinturas, Tezcatlipoca is the second son of Tonacatecuhtli and Tonacacihuatl, the most powerful of the four and able to know every thought. Among the Toltecs he appears as a transforming force who descended from heaven on a spider's thread to undo Quetzalcoatl's work. This duality, with black Tezcatlipoca set against white Quetzalcoatl, shapes much of his myth.

Worship and religion

His main festival was Toxcatl, which like most Aztec festivals involved human sacrifice. His nagual was the jaguar, and in the ritual calendar he ruled the trecena 1 Ocelotl. According to the sources, he demanded human offerings to repair the harm done to the earth monster. Among the Nahua of the Huasteca he survives in tradition as Tlacatecolotl.

Legacy

Sahagún recorded as many as 360 ways of naming him, reflecting his shifting character. Epithets include "the one who creates himself" and "the one who understands people". His image, with the smoking mirror and a substituted foot, recurs in codices such as the Borgia and Fejérváry-Mayer.

Ancient sources: Bernardino de Sahagún in the Florentine Codex, the Historia de los toltecas por sus pinturas, and the Borgia and Fejérváry-Mayer codices.

Trivia

Smoking mirror. His name is usually translated as "smoking mirror", referring to the obsidian mirrors of Mesoamerica.
The lost foot. He is often shown with an object in place of his right foot, a reference to the creation myth in which he lost it to Cipactli.
