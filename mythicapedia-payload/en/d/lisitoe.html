Oceanid of Greek mythology

Lysithoe

Lysithoe is an Oceanid of Greek mythology, daughter of the Titans Oceanus and Tethys, who according to some traditions was one of Zeus's lovers and mother of the first Heracles. Her name also appears as Lysithea, a spelling that has caused confusion between different figures.

The myth

According to tradition, Lysithoe was one of the Oceanids, the daughters of the Titans Oceanus and Tethys. Zeus took her as one of his many lovers, and from that union came the first Heracles, one of the seven that ancient authors describe. Cicero mentions this idea when discussing several Herculeses and several Jupiters, noting that the Heracles son of Lysithoe was the one who clashed with Apollo.

In that episode, Pseudo-Apollodorus and Hyginus tell how Heracles, stricken with a grave illness as punishment for killing Iphitus, consulted the Oracle of Delphi. The Pythia gave no answer, so he tried to plunder the temple and carry off the tripod, which brought him into conflict with Apollo. Zeus hurled a thunderbolt between them to stop the fight and ordered Apollo to answer him, whether he wished to or not.

The name poses a problem. The Pseudo-Clementines speak of Lysithea, daughter of a certain Evenus, who bore Helenus to Zeus. Many scholars think this is a corruption of Lysithoe, since those texts survive in poor condition, though it may also be a lost variant. Manuscripts often confuse the two spellings.

Legacy

The name Lysithea was given to one of Jupiter's small moons, about 39 kilometres across, which orbits roughly 11.7 million kilometres from the planet and completes a revolution in 259 days.

Ancient sources: Cicero, who discusses the various Herculeses, Pseudo-Apollodorus, Hyginus and the Pseudo-Clementines mention her.

Trivia

Another name, another woman. According to the English reference, Lysithea is also another name for Semele, daughter of King Cadmus of Thebes and mother of Dionysus.
A moon with her name. One of Jupiter's small moons bears the name Lysithea, although the mythological figure and the celestial body have no direct connection.
