Hindu god of wisdom and remover of obstacles

Ganesha

Ganesha is one of the most popular deities in Hinduism, instantly recognizable by his elephant head. He is revered as the remover of obstacles and the patron of the arts, letters and learning, and he is invoked at the start of rites and ceremonies.

The myth

According to tradition, Ganesha is the son of Shiva and Parvati, and Hindu texts place him within Shiva's family. There are at least three accounts of his birth and of how he received his elephant head. In the best-known one, Parvati creates him from clay to guard the door while she bathes. Shiva, who is not recognized by the boy, is stopped at the entrance and, in a fit of anger, beheads him. Seeing Parvati's grief, Shiva promises to restore the child with the head of the first living being to pass the door, and that creature is an elephant.

Another version holds that Shiva left home while Parvati was pregnant and, on his return, quarrelled with the boy, who died without Shiva realizing he was his own son. Shiva again promises to replace the head, this time with that of the first infant not lying in its mother's lap, and once more the elephant is chosen. A third, less familiar tradition says that Ganesha was punished by Shiva after playing a mischievous trick on his father. Other episodes explain further details of his appearance, such as his broken tusk.

Hindu texts also credit him with writing down the Mahabharata, which the sage Vyasa is said to have dictated to him. His stout body and his rat vehicle complete his familiar image.

Worship and religion

Ganesha is worshipped throughout India and is also honoured by Jains and Buddhists, while his cult extends across South and Southeast Asia, where he has other names. Devotees pray to him before beginning any ceremony. During the festival of Ganesh Chaturthi, his images are carried in procession and immersed in water at the end of the celebrations. His devotees are called Ganapatyas. Worship includes chants such as the Ganesha Sahasranama, which lists his thousand names, and prayers such as the Ganesha Chalisa.

Legacy

His figure is very common in Indian art, from medieval sculpture to contemporary painting. In India he is the patron of new beginnings, students and scribes. His name in its Tamil form, Pillaiyar, is also widespread in southern India.

Ancient sources: Ganesha appears in Hindu texts, and the main scriptures devoted to him are the Ganapati Atharvashirsha, the Ganesha Purana and the Mudgala Purana.
