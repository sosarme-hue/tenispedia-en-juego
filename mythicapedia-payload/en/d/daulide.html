Daulis

Daulis is a nymph of Greek mythology, daughter of the river Cephisus. According to tradition, the Phocian city that bears her name took its name from her.

The myth

According to Homer, the city's name could come from the nymph Daulis, daughter of the river-god Cephisus, or from the wooded landscape, since the ancients called such shaggy places dasea or daula.

Daulis is tied to one of the grimmest stories of the region: in Daulis, the women of the household of Tereus, the Thracian king, served him his own son. This act is regarded as the first pollution of the dining table among men, and the legend says Tereus was transformed into a hoopoe.

Worship and religion

The nymph Daulis had no cult of her own according to the sources. In Daulis there was a sanctuary of Athena with an ancient image, and an even older wooden image that the Daulians said Procne brought from Athens. In the territory, at a place called Tronis, stood a shrine of the founder hero, to whom the Phocians made daily offerings, pouring the blood into the grave.

Legacy

Daulis's name lives on in the Phocian city that bears it, and the city appears in the tradition as the setting of Tereus's story, on the road Oedipus did not take.

Ancient sources: Homer is cited for the naming of the city after the nymph Daulis, and Pausanias's account of Phocis records the local traditions.

Trivia

Absent swallows. According to the Phocians, swallows in their territory neither nest nor lay eggs, because even Philomela, once a bird, feared Tereus and stayed away from his country.
Tereus's hoopoe. The hoopoe, into which the legend turns Tereus, is a bird slightly larger than a quail, with head feathers that rise into a crest.
