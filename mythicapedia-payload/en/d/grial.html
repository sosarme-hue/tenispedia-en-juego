sacred cup of Arthurian legend

Holy Grail

The Grail is a cup, dish or stone with miraculous powers that King Arthur's knights seek in medieval romance. Its origins are debated, and it became the central motif of Arthurian quest stories.

The myth

The earliest surviving mention appears in Perceval, the Story of the Grail, an unfinished romance by Chrétien de Troyes written around 1180 to 1191. Perceval visits the castle of the Fisher King, where a procession passes: a young man carries a bleeding lance, two boys carry candelabra, and a girl bears an elaborate graal, a serving dish. Perceval stays silent, as he had been warned, and wakes alone the next morning. Later he learns that the questions he failed to ask would have healed the king and restored the Wasteland. For Chrétien the grail was still a broad, deep dish holding a single communion wafer, not yet a holy cup.

Soon after, Robert de Boron presented the grail as the vessel of the Last Supper, which Joseph of Arimathea used to catch Christ's blood on the cross. This merged the object with the legend of the Holy Chalice and fed into the vast Lancelot-Grail cycle, where the knight Galahad achieves it. In Wolfram von Eschenbach's Parzival it becomes a stone linked to the fall of the angels. In the continuations of Chrétien's poem, the grail is finally taken up to Heaven after Perceval's death.

Worship and religion

In Aragon and Valencia the grail was identified with the cup of the Last Supper. Local belief held that Saint Lawrence saved it from Emperor Valerian and brought it to Spain. Romanesque reliefs in the cathedral of Jaca show Pope Sixtus II handing the chalice to Lawrence. In 1437 the relic was permanently deposited in the cathedral of Valencia, where it is kept today. Other sources placed the cup in Jerusalem or Genoa.

Legacy

The Grail is one of the most enduring images of medieval legend, appearing in novels, films, video games and countless retellings. The phrase 'holy grail' is now used for any highly prized and elusive goal.

Ancient sources: Chrétien de Troyes' Perceval, the Story of the Grail, with its continuations, and Wolfram von Eschenbach's Parzival.

Trivia

A name with many theories. The word may come from Latin gradalis, a serving dish. In the 15th century the English writer John Hardyng invented an etymology linking it to royal blood.
Onyx and silver cups. Early accounts described the Jerusalem chalice differently: as an onyx cup in De locis sanctis and as a silver chalice with two handles in Adomnán's account.
