boar avatar of the god Vishnu

Varaha

Varaha is the boar-formed avatar of the Hindu god Vishnu. He is usually listed third among the ten principal avatars, and his story of a god who rescues the Earth from the primordial waters is one of the oldest in Hinduism.

The myth

In the classic legend, the demon Hiranyaksha, whose name means golden-eyed, steals the earth goddess Bhumi, also called Prithvi, and hides her in the depths of the cosmic ocean. Vishnu takes the form of a boar to find her, guided by his sense of smell. After a battle that the texts put at a thousand years, he kills the demon. He then lifts the Earth on his tusks and restores her to her place at the centre of the universe, and he marries her there.

Vedic texts tell the story differently. In the Shatapatha Brahmana, Prajapati becomes a boar called Emusha, plunges into the waters and brings up a piece of earth the size of a hand. In the Taittiriya Brahmana, the Lord of creation finds mud beneath a lotus leaf, spreads it over the water, and the Earth appears, named Bhumi, meaning that which spread out. The Rigveda contains other boar episodes, such as Vishnu, helped by Indra, stealing a hundred buffaloes, according to Macdonell's reading.

Over time, the Puranas, such as the Brahmanda and the Garuda, merged these versions and made Varaha an incarnation of Vishnu. The story is understood as the resurrection of the Earth after the dissolution of a kalpa, the stage that opens a new cosmic cycle.

Worship and religion

Varaha is worshipped in many temples across northern and southern India. At Tirumala-Tirupati, pilgrims first honour the Boar Lord, who removes offences, and then Venkateswara. The temple at Thiruvidandai, near Chennai, is well known for weddings, and couples come from all over India. Many shrines house images that devotees regard as self-manifested, swayambhu murti, and the worship remains alive among believers.

Legacy

His image appears in religious art with four arms, holding a discus, conch, mace, sword, lotus or a blessing gesture, and sometimes lifting the Earth on his tusks. The Varaha Purana is narrated by the god himself. In the Ramayana, Rama is praised as the single-tusked boar, an allusion that links Varaha with Vishnu.

Ancient sources: Rigveda, Taittiriya Samhita, Shatapatha Brahmana, Ramayana, Mahabharata and the Puranas, such as the Brahmanda and the Garuda.

Trivia

A name with several readings. Varaha comes from the Sanskrit word for boar, but the Rigveda also uses it for rain clouds. The grammarian Yaska, around 300 BCE, linked it to a root meaning to tear up roots.
The single-tusked boar. In the Ramayana, Rama is given the title of single-tusked boar, showing how the figure of Varaha came to be associated with Vishnu.
