Mesopotamian goddess of writing and grain

Nisaba

Nisaba was the Mesopotamian goddess of writing and grain, one of the oldest Sumerian deities attested in writing. Worshiped especially by scribes and regarded as a source of wisdom, she remained prominent through many periods of Mesopotamian history.

The myth

In the Sumerian tradition, Nisaba belonged to the circle of Enlil and was married to Haya, a deity of relatively low rank possibly associated with seals. Their daughter was Sud, goddess of Shuruppak. In the myth Enlil and Sud, Enlil must seek Nisaba's permission to marry Sud, with help from his attendant Nuska, a story that shows how close mother and daughter were.

Her origin varies between sources. She is described as the firstborn daughter of Enlil, as his mother-in-law, or possibly as his twin sister, and her mother is usually said to be Urash. Other texts make her a daughter of Enki or of An. The origin of her name remains unknown.

After the Old Babylonian period her prominence declined with the rise of Nabu, the new scribe god, though she never fully vanished from Mesopotamian religion.

Worship and religion

Her main cult center was Eresh, and she was also worshiped in Uruk and Umma. Scribes honored her in particular, and numerous Sumerian texts end with the doxology 'praise to Nisaba'. In late periods she appears mostly as the deification of grain. Her cult does not survive today.

Ancient sources: God lists such as An = Anum place her among the relatives and servants of Enlil. She appears in the myth Enlil and Sud and in The Curse of Agade, which lists her among the most prominent deities.

Trivia

Closing formula. Many Sumerian texts end with the formula 'praise to Nisaba', a sign of her status among scribes.
A disputed name. The origin of her name is unknown. The reading Nidaba, defended by Miguel Civil, is now considered implausible.
