Yule Cat

The Yule Cat is a huge, vicious cat from Icelandic folklore. According to tradition, it lurks in the snowy countryside during the Christmas season and punishes anyone who does not receive new clothes before Christmas Eve. It is considered the pet of the ogress Grýla and her sons, the Yule Lads.

The myth

According to the story, the cat devours people who have not received new clothing before Christmas Eve. In other versions, it is content to steal their food, especially the extra Christmas portion given to the residents of a farm.

The figure served as a threat and an incentive for farm workers who had to finish processing the wool gathered in autumn before the holidays. Those who worked received new clothes as a reward; those who did not take part were left exposed to the cat.

Over time it became linked to Grýla and her sons. In some later tales the cat is so unruly that only Stúfur, the smallest of the Yule Lads, can control it, riding it across the countryside.

Legacy

The cat became popular in 1932, when Jóhannes úr Kötlum devoted a poem to it in his collection about the coming of Christmas, and the piece became a fixture of Icelandic festivities and holiday decorations. Ingibjörg Þorbergs wrote a song about it around 1970. In 2008 an article in the English-language online paper The Reykjavík Grapevine helped it gain international fame, and in 2018 Reykjavík set up a five-metre iron sculpture of the cat in its central square.

Trivia

An extra meal. Some versions say the cat does not eat people but steals the extra portion of Christmas food set aside for the residents of the farm.
A very small rider. In some later tales, Stúfur, the smallest of the Yule Lads, is the only one able to control the cat and ride it across the countryside.
