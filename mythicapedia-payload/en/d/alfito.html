Greek nursery bogey

Alphito

Alphito is a frightening female figure of ancient Greece, a relative of the bogeyman, with whom nursemaids scared children into behaving. She matters because she is one of the few child-related spirits documented in that world.

The myth

The earliest mention comes from Plutarch's Moralia, which records the nursery tales that nursemaids and women told to discipline little children. Alphito appears as a pale old woman with white hair, and her name is linked to flour or barley meal (alphiton), though that connection is not entirely certain.

Since the nineteenth century, the folklorist Wilhelm Mannhardt saw her as a "corn mother". Robert Graves, in The White Goddess, proposed that she was once a barley goddess of Argos, later degraded into a bogey for children. It is a highly speculative thesis: no ancient source links her to leprosy or to the cliffs of Nonacris.

Legacy

She gained prominence mainly through Graves's work, which used her as an example of his White Goddess theory. Recent scholarship groups her with spirits such as Acco, Gello and Mormo, which threatened reproduction and child-rearing.

Ancient sources: Plutarch's Moralia is the earliest reference; the lexicographer Hesychius mentions her only indirectly.

Trivia

Divination with flour. The term alphitomanteia refers to a form of divination using flour or barley meal, linked to her name.
The White Goddess. Robert Graves took Alphito as one of the basis for his book The White Goddess, a study of poetic myth.
