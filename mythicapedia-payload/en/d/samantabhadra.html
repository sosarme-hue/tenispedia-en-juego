Buddhist bodhisattva of practice and meditation

Samantabhadra

Samantabhadra, known as the Bodhisattva of Universal Virtue, is a great bodhisattva in Mahayana Buddhism associated with practice and meditation. He forms the Shakyamuni Triad with Shakyamuni Buddha and Mañjuśrī, and in China he is known as Puxian.

The myth

According to Buddhist tradition, Samantabhadra is the patron of the Lotus Sutra. The Avatamsaka Sutra presents him as the source of the ten great vows that form the basis of the bodhisattva path. In the final chapter, the Gandavyuha, the young seeker Sudhana meets him, receives confirmation of his awakening, and merges with the bodhisattva. Samantabhadra then recites the verses of the Bhadracaripranidhana, the Vows of Good Conduct, which were widely loved in India, East Asia and the Himalayas.

The ten vows include paying homage to all buddhas, making offerings, confessing one's faults, rejoicing in others' merits, and dedicating all merit to sentient beings so that they may reach buddhahood.

Worship and religion

In Japan, where he is called Fugen, he is an important deity for the Tendai and Shingon schools. In China he is venerated as the patron of the monasteries on Mount Emei, one of the four famous Buddhist sacred mountains. He is often shown riding a six-tusked white elephant, in triads beside Shakyamuni or Vairocana, and sometimes with feminine features similar to those of Guanyin. His veneration remains alive in monasteries and Buddhist liturgy.

Legacy

His figure entered Chinese literature through the sixteenth-century novel Fengshen Yanyi, where he appears as a Daoist immortal who later joins Buddhism. Tang-era statues of him, such as those at Longmen, are among the most reproduced images of East Asian Buddhist art.

Ancient sources: Mahayana Buddhist texts such as the Avatamsaka Sutra, the Lotus Sutra and the Gandavyuha, as well as the Chinese novel Fengshen Yanyi.

Trivia

A six-tusked elephant. His mount is a six-tusked white elephant. In the novel Fengshen Yanyi, this animal was originally an immortal defeated by Puxian in battle.
Two different Samantabhadras. In Tibetan Buddhism, the name Samantabhadra also refers to the Primordial Buddha, often shown in union with his consort. He is not the same being as the bodhisattva.
