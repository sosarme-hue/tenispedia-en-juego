king of the Vedic gods, lord of the sky, storm and thunderbolt

Indra

Indra is the king of the devas and lord of the heaven of Svarga in Vedic and Hindu tradition, linked to the sky, lightning, rain, rivers and war. He is the most frequently mentioned deity in the Rigveda, and his story bridges the ancient Vedic religion and later Hinduism, where his standing falls behind the Trimurti.

The myth

According to the central Vedic myth, Indra kills the asura Vritra, a monster that held back the waters, and so frees the rivers and brings rain and light to humankind. In another account his victory is over Vala, a cave-like demon on the mountain where the Panis had shut away the cows and the goddess Ushas. He is also a warrior who breaks the stone fortresses of the Dasius, and fighters on both sides of the battle of the Ten Kings invoke him. His drink is soma, whose consumption strengthens him for combat.

In the Vedic texts Indra is the son of Diaus Pitar, the Father Sky, husband of Prithivi, the Earth, and father of Agni, god of fire; he is also considered the brother of Surya, the Sun. According to later tradition, he rose to the throne of the devas after killing his father, whom he dragged down from the sky by one foot. He is married to Shachi, daughter of Puloman, whom he had killed, and is the father, by several wives, of Arjuna and other sons. The Maruts, storm gods, were born from a fetus that Diti had carried to avenge herself on him; Indra shattered it with his thunderbolt.

Another well-known episode tells how Indra seduced Ahalya, wife of the sage Gautama. When the ascetic discovered it, he cursed Indra and his body was covered in vulvas; after Indra's penance, the sage agreed to turn them into eyes. As a deva aware of karma, Indra fears losing his position: when the ascetic Vishvamitra piles up austerities to ascend, Indra sends the apsaras, such as Urvashi or Rambha, to seduce him. Even so, in later Hinduism he is surpassed by Brahma, Vishnu and Shiva. His mount is the elephant Airavata, which represents the cloud, and his weapon is the thunderbolt, the vajra.

Worship and religion

Indra was a central figure in Vedic hymns and sacrifices, where soma was offered to him and victory and rain were requested; in the Vedic period he was regarded as lord of the thirty-three gods. In later Hinduism his cult lost prominence to other deities, though he remains present in the tradition. In Buddhism he appears as Shakra (Taishakuten in Japan), ruler of the realm of the devas and often mocked as a figure who also suffers rebirth; in Jainism he is king of the superhumans of Svarga, who accompanies with Indrani the sacred moments in the life of a Tirthankara.

Legacy

His name survives in many Asian languages: in Chinese it appears as Yintuoluo or Dishitian, in Thai as Phra In, and in Japanese as Taishakuten. His resemblances to Zeus, Jupiter, Thor and Perun, all thunder gods, have fueled comparisons among Indo-European mythologies since the nineteenth century. His name also appears in a Hittite treaty from around 1400 BCE and among the gods of the Mitanni, showing his early spread. At Bangkok's Wat Arun he is shown riding Airavata on the central tower of the temple.

Ancient sources: The Rigveda, where he is the most frequently mentioned deity; also the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad, the epics and the Puranas, and in Iran the Avestan texts, where he appears as a demon.

Trivia

Indra's eyes. According to the story, the curse of the sage Gautama covered Indra's body with vulvas, which were later turned into eyes. This is why he is shown with many eyes.
A birth in pieces. The Maruts were born from a fetus that Diti had carried for a century. Indra split it with his thunderbolt into seven or forty-nine parts, and each became a storm god.
A Hittite tablet. On the Boghaz-köi tablets, dated to about 1400 BCE, a treaty invokes Indra alongside Mitra, Varuna and the Nasatya, the same Vedic deities.
