bat god of Maya mythology

Camazotz

Camazotz is a bat god of Maya mythology whose name means “death bat” in K'iche'. His bat-headed figure was also adopted by the Zapotecs and the Mexica, and it reflects the bat's links with night, death and sacrifice.

The myth

In the Popol Vuh, the Camazotz are bat-like beings that live in the House of Bats in the underworld of Xibalba. There the Hero Twins, Hunahpu and Xbalanque, must spend the night during their trials. When Hunahpu sticks his head out to check whether dawn has come, one of the bats snatches it off.

Xbalanque gathers the animals, each bringing its favourite food. The tapir brings a squash, which is carved into a new head for his brother. The twins can then play the final ballgame, which leads to the defeat of Xibalba.

Worship and religion

According to these sources, the cult emerged around 100 BCE among the Zapotecs of Oaxaca, who portrayed a figure with a human body and a bat's head. Among the Nahuas the god was linked to healing, but also to the power to cut the silver cord joining body and soul, and their masters invoked him to ask for their students' health. The sources do not say whether his cult survives today.

Legacy

His name has travelled well beyond Maya tradition. Madeleine L'Engle used it for a dark planet controlled by a telepathic brain in the 1962 novel A Wrinkle in Time. The fifth season of Stranger Things uses it to name the evil dimension where its characters are trapped. It also appears as a god of bats and evil in the Greyhawk setting of Dungeons & Dragons, and as a bat-like Titan in the graphic novel Kingdom Kong. In 2014, a Batman bust made by the Yucatán design studio Kimbal drew on Maya motifs and references to Camazotz.

Ancient sources: The Popol Vuh, a Late Post-Classic Maya text, places him among the bat-like beings of the House of Bats.

Trivia

A planet with the same name. The novel A Wrinkle in Time describes Camazotz as a dark planet ruled by a telepathic mind called IT.
A Batman with Maya motifs. In 2014, for Batman's 75th anniversary, a bust of the character designed in Yucatán included references to Camazotz. It was shown at the Mexican Design Museum in 2015.
A disputed iconography. The researchers Brady and Coltman question whether classic Maya bat imagery really represents Camazotz.
