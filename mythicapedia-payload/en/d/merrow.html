sea creature of Irish folklore

Merrow

The merrow is the Irish folklore mermaid or merman, a sea being who can cross to dry land with the help of a magical cap. The name comes from the Irish murúch, but the figure took its modern shape mainly through 19th-century tales collected by Croker.

The myth

According to 19th-century tales, the merrow is a woman with a fish's tail below the waist, green hair and a modest, affectionate nature, who can fall in love with a human. The best-known story is that of the Lady of Gollerus, who marries a Kerry man; when he hides her red cap, she cannot return to the sea. Merrows also lure young men beneath the waves, where they live in an enchanted state, and their music can be heard from the deep ocean.

Male merrows are described as ugly and crude. The male character Coomara, in 'The Soul Cages', turned out to be a fabrication by Keightley based on a German tale.

Legacy

Yeats borrowed heavily from Croker's material, and his work helped spread the figure of the merrow. The Brothers Grimm translated the second volume of Croker's Fairy Legends into German soon after its publication in 1828.

Ancient sources: The Book of Invasions mentions a siren-like creature, though the term merrow is modern.

Trivia

An invented tale. 'The Soul Cages', with its male merrow, turned out to be fiction based on a German tale, although Keightley claimed similar stories circulated in Cork and Wicklow.
Sea cows. Yeats claimed merrows came ashore as small hornless cows, an idea some researchers link to sea cows said to graze where a merrow lived.
