Norse poem on the origin of the social classes

Rígsþula

Rígsþula, or 'The Lay of Ríg', is a poem from the Poetic Edda in which a god named Ríg wanders the world and fathers the three social classes of humanity: thralls, free farmers and nobles. His line ends with Konr the Young, the first true king.

The myth

According to the poem, Ríg walks the shore and is sheltered by Ái and Edda, who give him poor food. Their son Þræll becomes the ancestor of the thralls. Next Ríg visits Afi and Amma, whose son Karl, ruddy and neat, fathers the free farmers.

Last, Ríg is hosted by Faðir and Móðir, whose son Jarl, fair-haired, is claimed by Ríg as heir and becomes a lord. Jarl's youngest son Konr the Young gives his name to the Norse word for king. The poem breaks off as a crow urges Konr to seek the halls of Dan and Danþír.

Ancient sources: It survives incomplete in the 14th-century Codex Wormianus, after Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda, and belongs to the Poetic Edda.

Trivia

Origin of the word for king. The poem places in Konr the Young the origin of the Norse word konungr, meaning king.
