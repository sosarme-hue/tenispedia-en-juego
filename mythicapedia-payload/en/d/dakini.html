goddess of Hindu and Buddhist tradition

Dakini

The dakini is a female figure in Hindu and Buddhist traditions whose meaning shifts with the era and school: from flesh-eating demonesses to enlightened deities and guides in Tantra. The word is often glossed as "sky-goer", which explains her links with freedom and dance.

The myth

In the earliest Hindu texts, dakinis appear as demonesses who fed on flesh and the vital essence of humans. Some Purāṇas place them in the retinue of the goddess Kālī and of Vīrabhadra, who marches against Daksha, and the Bhāgavata Purāṇa mentions them among the beings that the gopis sought to keep away from Krishna through protective rites.

In Hindu Tantra, Ḍākinī is the name of a goddess linked to the chakras or to the constituent elements of the body. The Kubjikāmata and Rudrayāmala Tantras associate her with the mūlādhāra, the root chakra, within a series of six goddesses matched to the chakras and to parts of the body.

In Buddhism, the Laṅkāvatāra Sūtra speaks of dākas and ḍākinīs as the offspring of a carnivorous king. In East Asian tradition, the buddha Vairocana, in the form of Mahākāla, subdues them and forces them to stop eating humans, granting them instead the vital essence of the dead. In Tibetan Buddhism, by contrast, dakinis are fierce, enlightened energies who test aspirants and lead them toward the pure land.

Worship and religion

In Tibetan Buddhism, the dakini has a role in tantric practice, helping initiates through invocations and consummation rites, and is classed as inner, outer and secret. In Japan, within Shingon, three dakinis appear in the Womb Realm mandala, beside Yama.

Legacy

The term has entered Western culture through Tibetan Buddhism and writings on tantra, where it serves as a symbol of feminine enlightened energy. In Japan, the dakinis merged into the deity Dakiniten, who became associated with the goddess Inari and the fox (kitsune).

Ancient sources: Hindu Purāṇas (Shiva, Brahmāṇḍa and Bhāgavata Purāṇas), the Laṅkāvatāra Sūtra and the Kubjikāmata and Rudrayāmala Tantras.

Trivia

A black crown. According to tradition, a dakini gave the black crown to the third Karmapa, Rangjung Dorje, when he was three years old. The crown became an emblem of his lineage.
Bodily correspondences. In some texts, dakinis are linked to parts of the body and to the elements, and ritual practitioners symbolically offer them different fluids and tissues.
