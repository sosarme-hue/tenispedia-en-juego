Greek goddess of health

Hygieia

Hygieia is the Greek goddess of health, and her name gave English the word “hygiene”. She belonged to the circle of her father Asclepius, the god of medicine, and over time she came to receive a cult of her own.

The myth

The myths say very little about her: Hygieia stars in no story of her own, and her role is closer to that of a companion figure. The most common version makes her a daughter of Asclepius and Epione, with four sisters, Panacea, Iaso, Aceso and Aegle, each linked to one aspect of healing. The Suda gives this parentage, but other writers considered her the wife of Asclepius, and the Orphics imagined her as a daughter of Eros and Peitho.

Her own identity grew gradually, since she was once mainly an epithet of Athena. Tradition tells that during the building work on the Acropolis, a craftsman fell from a height and was given up by the physicians. Pericles, discouraged, was shown in a dream by the goddess a remedy that cured the man, and in gratitude he set up the bronze statue of Athena Hygieia.

Worship and religion

Hygieia was mostly worshipped alongside her father, in sanctuaries such as those at Argos, Athens, Corinth, Epidaurus and Cos. Pausanias described, at the Asclepieion of Titane in Sicyon, statues of the goddess covered with women’s hair and Babylonian garments, offerings that some inscriptions also place at Paros. After the plagues of Athens, the Delphic oracle recognised her as a goddess in her own right, and her name appears in the Hippocratic oath beside Apollo Healer, Asclepius and Panacea. She was usually shown as a young woman feeding a coiled serpent and holding a cup. In modern Hellenic religion she is still invoked with a hymn and a libation of rosé wine.

Legacy

The word “hygiene” comes from her name, and the asteroid Hygiea bears it too. The cup of Hygieia, with its serpent, is today the common emblem of pharmacies. The Romans linked her to Salus and Valetudo, and her iconography passed to the Gallo-Roman goddess Sirona.

Ancient sources: Pausanias in his Description of Greece, Plutarch, the Suda, the Hippocratic oath, Aeschylus, Licymnius, Ariphron of Sicyon and the Orphic hymns.

Trivia

From goddess to word. The English word “hygiene” comes directly from the Greek name of the goddess, hygieia, meaning “health”.
A physicians’ oath. The Hippocratic oath, sworn by physicians, called on Hygieia together with Apollo Healer, Asclepius and Panacea as witnesses to their commitment.
Offerings of hair. At Titane in Sicyon, her statues were covered with women’s hair, an offering that Pausanias described in detail.
