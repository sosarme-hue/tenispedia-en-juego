Niobe

Niobe is, in Greek mythology, the daughter of Tantalus and wife of Amphion, king of Thebes. Her pride in boasting of her children before Leto brought a tragedy that turned her to stone, and her story has become one of the great symbols of grief over lost children in Western culture.

The myth

Niobe was the daughter of Tantalus and wife of Amphion, king of Thebes. According to the most widely accepted version, she bore him fourteen children, seven sons and seven daughters, whom she loved dearly. She boasted of her large brood and mocked Leto, who had only two children, Apollo and Artemis. Her pride went so far that she forbade honours to the goddess, claiming she deserved altars more than Leto. Leto asked her children for revenge: Apollo shot the sons down with arrows and Artemis the daughters. Only Amyclas, who had prayed to Leto, and Meliboea survived. Meliboea turned pale with terror and was afterwards called Chloris.

Niobe, overcome with weeping, stood motionless beside the corpses and ended up turned to stone, as she had asked Zeus. One version says a whirlwind carried her to Mount Sipylus in Lydia, where tears flow from a rock shaped like a woman. Another says she fled of her own will and that her tears formed the river Achelous. Amphion tried to avenge his children by attacking Apollo’s sanctuary at Delphi and was killed by the god. The children’s bodies lay unburied for nine days until the gods themselves gave them burial.

Genealogies vary between authors. Some name Dione as her mother, others Eurythemista or Euryanassa, and in one version Niobe is the daughter of Assaon and wife of Philottus, with an even darker ending. The number of children also changes with the source: Homer mentions twelve, Hesiod twenty, Herodotus five and Euripides fourteen.

Legacy

Niobe became a standard image of grief over lost children. Homer compares her to Priam mourning before Achilles, and Sophocles has Antigone recall her own loneliness by comparing it to Niobe’s. Aeschylus and Sophocles both wrote tragedies titled Niobe, now lost or surviving only in fragments. Shakespeare quotes her in Hamlet (“like Niobe, all tears”), Byron calls Rome “the Niobe of nations”, Oscar Wilde applied the same image to Ireland, and Faulkner evokes her in Absalom, Absalom!.

Ancient sources: Homer (Iliad), Sophocles (Antigone), Aeschylus, Ovid (Metamorphoses), Hyginus and Parthenius of Nicaea.

Trivia

Meliboea turned Chloris. According to Pausanias, Meliboea was renamed Chloris, meaning “pale green”, because fear left her face ashen.
The Weeping Rock. On Mount Sipylus there is a rock formation that resembles a woman’s face. Rainwater seeps through its porous limestone, so it appears to weep, and it is known as the Weeping Rock.
Rome, the Niobe of nations. In Childe Harold’s Pilgrimage, Lord Byron called Rome “the Niobe of nations” to lament the fall of its grandeur.
