Huldra

The huldra is a seductive creature of the forests in Scandinavian folklore, beautiful and human-like at first glance, but with an animal's tail and a back hollow as a rotten tree trunk. Her name comes from a root meaning "covered" or "secret", and she is linked to the German Holda and other female figures of European folklore.

The myth

According to tradition, the huldra appears in mist or rain, kind and finely dressed, hiding her tail beneath her skirt and her hollow back beneath her hair. She lures men into the woods for sex. If a man satisfies her, she may let him live and even bless him with her power; if not, he dies. Even so, the man becomes obsessed, returns every day and eventually is worn out.

To protect yourself, carry iron or a handful of salt, which repel fairies. If already enchanted, tying sprigs of matacabras and vandelrot to yourself is said to make her lose interest, and seeing the rotten hollow in her back breaks the spell. Huldras also kidnap children, leaving their own offspring in their place. They may marry mortals, but cannot enter a church: if they do, they lose their tail and their beauty becomes eternal ugliness.

Legacy

The huldra remains present in popular culture. She is one of the Watchers in the mobile game Year Walk and stars in the first chapter of its sequel, Year Walk: Bedtime Stories for Awful Children. Neil Gaiman draws on huldra legends in his novella The Monarch of the Glen, and the Norwegian film Thale (2012) stars a huldra. The Danish bog Huldremose also takes its name from her, and is famous for the discovery of a bog body.

Trivia

A bog with her name. The Danish bog Huldremose, on Djursland, takes its name from these creatures and is famous for the discovery of the Huldremose Woman, a body preserved in the peat from about 55 BC.
Protected charcoal burners. According to some traditions, huldras watched over charcoal kilns while the charcoal burners slept. The burners left them provisions in return.
