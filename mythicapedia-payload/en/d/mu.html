supposed lost continent of the Pacific

Mu

Mu is a lost continent that, according to esoteric theories of the 19th and 20th centuries, supposedly existed in the Pacific Ocean and gave rise to great ancient civilizations. It has no scientific basis and geology rejects it, yet it remains a recurring motif in pseudoscientific literature.

The myth

The idea of Mu first emerged in the 19th century with the Anglo-American antiquarian Augustus Le Plongeon, who, after studying Maya ruins in Yucatán, claimed that an ancient land had once existed beyond the seas and that the civilizations of Egypt and Mesoamerica descended from it. His starting point was a mistranslation of the so-called Troano Codex by Brasseur de Bourbourg, in which a word read as "Mu" supposedly referred to a land swallowed by a catastrophe. Le Plongeon identified that land with Atlantis and placed it in the Atlantic.

The concept was later popularised by James Churchward in a series of books beginning with "Lost Continent of Mu, the Motherland of Man" (1926). Churchward placed Mu in the Pacific and described it as the cradle of an advanced civilization, the Naacal, with 64 million inhabitants and seven major cities, destroyed about 12,000 years ago in a single night. He claimed to base his work on clay tablets he had been given by a priest in India, though his translations are considered invented.

According to these authors, Mu gave rise to Egypt, Assyria, the Indus Valley, pre-Inca Peru and Easter Island, and even to legends such as that of Ten Ten and Cai Cai in Mapuche mythology, a reading that Mapuche sources do not support.

Legacy

Mu has been a recurring theme in pseudoscientific literature and fantasy; L. Sprague de Camp gave it a detailed analysis in "Lost Continents" (1954). It remains alive among occult and New Age groups, who still invoke it, citing findings such as Yonaguni.
