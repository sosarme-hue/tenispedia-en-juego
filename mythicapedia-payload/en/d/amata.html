queen of Latium in Roman mythology

Amata

Amata is, in Roman mythology, the queen consort of Latium, wife of Latinus and mother of Lavinia. Her opposition to her daughter's marriage to Aeneas makes her one of the tragic figures of Virgil's Aeneid.

The myth

According to Roman tradition, Amata was the wife of Latinus, king of the Latins, and mother of Lavinia, their only child to reach adulthood. Since Latinus had no surviving sons, he promised his kingdom to whoever married the princess. The strongest suitor was Turnus, heir of the Rutulians and favored by the queen as a son-in-law. An oracle, however, had declared that Lavinia must marry a foreigner.

When Aeneas, a Trojan and therefore a stranger, arrived, Latinus accepted him as a son-in-law, but Amata did not. Driven by a fury that, according to the stories, Juno and the Fury Alecto stirred in her, she tried to block the wedding and set off the conflict between the two sides. The war fills much of the seventh book of Virgil's Aeneid, and Amata is the figure who triggers it. Seeing that Turnus could not defeat the Trojans and that the union could not be prevented, the queen hanged herself in full view of her horrified daughter.

Legacy

Dante revisits her in Canto 17 of the Purgatorio, among the examples of the sin of wrath alongside Procne and Haman. There Lavinia reproaches her mother for the grief her suicide caused. Some scholars have linked this scene to Dante's own experience after his exile from Florence.

Ancient sources: Virgil's Aeneid presents her in Book VII, and Dante mentions her in the Purgatorio.
