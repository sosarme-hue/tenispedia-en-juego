Greek hero, son of Phylacus

Iphiclus

Iphiclus is a hero of Greek myth, son of King Phylacus of Phylace and famous for his extraordinary speed. His story, in which the seer Melampus helps him have children, shows the role of prophecy in Greek tales about family and inheritance.

The myth

According to tradition, Iphiclus was so swift that he could run over asphodel stalks and ears of wheat without breaking them. His father Phylacus asked the seer Melampus to find out why his son had no offspring. Melampus, who understood the language of animals, explained that the boy had fled in fear after seeing a bloodied knife with which Phylacus castrated rams. The blade had been driven into a sacred oak, whose bark eventually hid it. Following Melampus's advice, scraping the rust from the knife and having Iphiclus drink it for ten days would bring him a son, and so Podarces was born.

In another tale, Melampus stole Iphiclus's cattle and was caught. When he warned a servant that the roof was about to collapse, Iphiclus freed him and handed over the cows as well.

Ancient sources: Homer mentions him in the Iliad among the leaders of the Phylacians, and Hesiod gives a different genealogy, in which Podarces and Protesilaus are cousins.

Trivia

Prodigious runner. Accounts said Iphiclus could run over asphodels and wheat without harming the plants. His speed was proverbial in Greek tradition.
A knife in the oak. The knife that frightened the boy was driven into a sacred oak, and the tree's bark grew over it until it was later recovered.
