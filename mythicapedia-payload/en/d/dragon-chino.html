Chinese dragon

The Chinese dragon, known as long or loong, is a legendary creature of Chinese mythology, folklore and culture. Unlike the European dragon, it is usually a benevolent spiritual symbol linked to water, rain and good fortune, and for centuries it was the emblem of imperial power.

The myth

Traditionally, the Chinese dragon combines the features of several animals. The Han scholar Wang Fu recorded nine resemblances: stag's antlers, a camel's head, a demon's eyes, a snake's neck, carp scales, eagle claws, tiger soles, cow's ears and a belly like a clam's. Many images show a flaming pearl under its chin, associated with spiritual energy and wisdom.

The dragon flies without wings thanks to a structure on its head called chimu, without which it could not ascend to the sky. Its supernatural powers are almost unlimited: it can disguise itself as a silkworm, grow as large as the world or hide in water. It rules rivers, seas, waterfalls and storms, and is often shown as a king in human form with a dragon's head. Four Dragon Kings govern the four seas. Early dragons were said to be wise and just, but Buddhism introduced the idea of malevolent dragons that caused floods.

Tradition also links the dragon to the origins of the Chinese people. The legendary emperor Huang Di is said to have ascended to heaven as a dragon, which is why Chinese people sometimes call themselves the "Descendants of the Dragon".

Worship and religion

In antiquity, many communities, especially near rivers and seas, had temples to their local Dragon King. In droughts or floods, elders and officials led sacrifices and rites to appease the dragon and ask for rain or for it to stop. Worship of the Dragon Kings as rulers of water and weather persists in many regions and is part of traditions such as the Chinese New Year, although few people today believe it is a real animal.

Legacy

The dragon is a major emblem of Chinese culture. It appeared on the Qing dynasty flag, on the Hong Kong coat of arms under British rule and in the design of the Brand Hong Kong promotional symbol. It was a state symbol in Vietnam under the Nguyễn dynasty. In 2008 the Chinese government chose not to use the dragon as Olympic mascot because of its aggressive connotations abroad.

Ancient sources: Wang Fu of the Han dynasty recorded the nine resemblances of the dragon; the Guanzi and the Shuowen Jiezi describe its powers; the Tang dynasty text Youyang zazu records the legend of the chimu.

Trivia

Dragon bones. Ancient Chinese called fossil bones "dragon bones". Modern analysis suggests they are most often remains of Cenozoic mammals, such as the extinct horse Hipparion. They are still used in traditional Chinese medicine.
Birthmark. In some legends, an emperor is born with a dragon-shaped birthmark, and a peasant with such a mark overthrows the ruling dynasty and founds a new one.
