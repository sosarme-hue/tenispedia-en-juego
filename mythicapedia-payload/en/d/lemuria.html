Lemuria

Lemuria is a hypothetical continent that the British zoologist Philip Sclater proposed in 1864 to explain the distribution of lemurs across Madagascar, India and other lands around the Indian Ocean. Despite its scientific origin, the idea was abandoned and ended up as a lost continent in Western esoteric thought.

The myth

Sclater was not describing a mythical continent but a large landmass that, he argued, had once joined Madagascar and India before breaking apart. According to his proposal, remnants of that landmass survived in Madagascar and the Mascarene Islands, and the name he chose referred to the animals that had prompted the hypothesis. Shortly afterwards, in 1870, Ernst Haeckel suggested that Lemuria might have been the cradle of the first humans, as a way to explain the absence of missing links in the fossil record.

The shift towards mythology came with Theosophy. Helena Blavatsky made Lemuria part of her doctrine as the homeland of one of the successive "root races" of humanity, and in later works the continent sinks after a geological cataclysm. Over time the idea spread into stories about Mu, into claims by communities who saw themselves as descendants of its survivors, and into versions linking it with Kumari Kandam and Tamil nationalism. Modern geology, however, finds no sunken formation that could have linked India and Madagascar: continental drift explains their separation without any need for a drowned continent.

Legacy

Lemuria has left its mark mainly in esoteric and pseudoscientific literature. James Churchward developed it under the name Mu in books such as The Lost Continent of Mu (1926), and the theme also appears in the work of the Australian poet Bernard O'Dowd and in Rosa Campbell Praed's memoir My Australian Girlhood. In 1894, Frederick Spencer Oliver placed Lemurian survivors in a network of tunnels beneath Mount Shasta in the United States, an idea that several religious groups still maintain today through pilgrimages and annual festivals.

Trivia

A name taken from the lemurs. Sclater chose the name after the animals whose distribution he was trying to explain. The lemurs of his time included a broader group of primates than the one recognised today.
An earlier idea without a name. About two decades earlier, the French naturalist Étienne Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire had already proposed a land connection between India and Madagascar, though he gave it no name.
