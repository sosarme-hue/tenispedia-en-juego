culture hero and trickster of Polynesian mythology

Māui

Māui is the great culture hero and trickster of Polynesian mythology, whose stories are told across most of Polynesia, from New Zealand to Hawai'i, with variants reaching as far west as Vanuatu. Rather than a deity, he is a folk hero whose exploits explain the origin of islands, fire, and the length of the day.

The myth

According to tradition, Māui was a clever and mischievous young man who caught no fish and took his brothers' catches, until they stopped taking him out to sea. His mother, Hina in some versions and Taranga in others, advised him to seek his father for a magic fish-hook that would bring him endless fish. The versions differ, but in most he ends up joining the fishing trip despite his brothers' mockery.

Out at sea he baited the hook, with his own blood in the Māori account and with alae birds, sacred to his mother, in the Hawaiian one. When he pulled up the huge catch, it became dry land, and his brothers broke the line by disobeying his order not to look back. In New Zealand the fish became the North Island, Te Ika-a-Māui, and his canoe became the South Island; in Hawai'i the same episode produced several islands.

Other deeds follow the same spirit. Māui stole fire for humans from Mahuika, the fire goddess who lived in a cave in a burning mountain, and he slowed the sun with ropes and his grandmother's magic jawbone. His death is told as an attempt to win humans immortality: he entered the body of the goddess of death, Hine-nui-te-pō, intending to leave through her mouth while she slept, but she woke and crushed him.

Worship and religion

Māui was very rarely worshipped. He was a folk hero more than a god, and his stories were passed down mainly by word of mouth within communities.

Legacy

His figure remains alive in popular culture, above all through Disney's film Moana. In geography, his name lives on in the island of Maui in Hawai'i, and his great catch is remembered in New Zealand's North Island, known as Te Ika-a-Māui.

Ancient sources: Māui's stories were passed down orally; among the traditional texts that mention him is the Hawaiian chant Kumulipo, which includes his genealogy.

Trivia

A hook in the sky. According to Polynesian cosmogony, the gods placed Maui's magic hook in the sky, in the constellation Scorpius, so that humans would remember his deeds.
Māori names. In Māori he has names such as Māui-tikitiki, meaning Māui the top-knot, and Māui-pōtiki, meaning Māui the last born.
