snake god of ancient Egyptian mythology

Nehebkau

Nehebkau, whose name is often translated as "he who provides the ka", is a serpent god of ancient Egyptian mythology linked to the sun, kingship and the protection of the dead. He shifted from a feared demon to a benevolent guardian of the afterlife.

The myth

The oldest texts portray Nehebkau as a long, winding serpent who devoured the souls of the dead in the afterlife and who was regarded as an enemy of the sun god Ra. According to tradition, Ra designed his sun boats so that the wind could push them and let them escape the serpent's many coils. In one myth, Nehebkau swallowed seven cobras and afterwards became immune to any magic, fire or water.

Later, in the Coffin Texts, the god Atum presses his fingernail against a nerve in Nehebkau's spine and calms his chaotic nature. After this change, Nehebkau served Ra as a companion, helping to move the sun boat, and attended deceased kings by providing food and carrying messages.

As one of the forty-two assessors of Ma'at, he examined the purity of the dead and was responsible for protecting their neck and throat. Once the deceased were declared innocent, he gave them food, drink and ka, the life force that allowed them to endure in the afterlife. Accounts of his parentage vary: some make him a son of Serket, the scorpion goddess; others a son of the earth god Geb and the goddess Renenutet.

Worship and religion

His festival was widely celebrated during the Middle and New Kingdoms. He was invoked in rituals, amulets and protective spells, and his name appeared in magical spells linked to the sun. His cult is no longer practised.

Ancient sources: The Pyramid Texts, the Coffin Texts, the Book of Gates and the Book of the Dead, which mention him as a judge and protector of the afterlife.
