Bixi

Bixi is a figure from Chinese mythology, one of the nine sons of the Dragon King, shown as a dragon with a turtle's shell. His image became famous as the stone base of commemorative steles, and it can still be seen across much of East Asia.

The myth

In Chinese tradition, Bixi is one of the nine sons of the Dragon King, which is why he is depicted with a dragon's body and a turtle's shell. The sources that record this parentage give no further details of his deeds, so he is known mainly as a stone bearer.

His name is written in simplified Chinese as 赑屃, rendered in pinyin as Bìxì and in the older Wade-Giles system as Pi-hsi or Bi Xi.

Worship and religion

Bixi sculptures were used for centuries as plinths for commemorative steles and tablets, especially in the funerary complexes of later emperors and to mark important events such as an imperial visit. They also support the bases of bridges and archways. Sculptures are traditionally rubbed for good luck, a custom that can cause conservation problems.

Legacy

His image spread to Japan, Korea, Vietnam, Mongolia and the Russian Far East. The tradition of tortoise-borne steles continued through the Ming and Qing dynasties, and a Qing-era bixi given to Harvard University in 1936 still stands on its Cambridge campus.
