Greek god of the winds

Aeolus

Aeolus is the god of the winds in Greek mythology, best known for his encounter with Odysseus on his floating island. His story shaped the enduring image of a keeper who imprisons and releases the storms, an idea that reached modern art and language.

The myth

According to Homer, Aeolus, son of Hippotes, ruled the floating island of Aeolia, surrounded by a wall of bronze. Zeus had made him keeper of the winds, with power to still them or rouse them. He lived there with his wife and twelve children, six sons and six daughters, who were married to one another to keep the family in harmony.

In the Odyssey, Aeolus hosted Odysseus and his men for a month and, when they set off, gave him an oxhide bag holding all the winds except the west wind, which was to carry them home. When Ithaca came into view, Odysseus fell asleep, and his men, thinking the bag held gold and silver, opened it. The winds drove the ship back to the island. Aeolus, judging that the gods hated Odysseus, sent him away without further help.

In Apollonius of Rhodes's Argonautica, at Hera's request, Aeolus calms all the winds except the west wind to aid Jason and the Argonauts. In Virgil's Aeneid, he keeps the winds in a cave and unleashes them against Aeneas's fleet at Juno's request, in exchange for the nymph Deiopea. Neptune rebukes the winds and calms the waves, and Aeneas survives.

Accounts of his family vary. Euripides and Ovid make him the father of Canace and Macareus, while Diodorus Siculus gives him a wife, Cyane, and makes him a descendant of Ausonus. Some writers confused him with Aeolus, son of Hellen, eponym of the Aeolians.

Legacy

His name lives on in the word aeolian, as in aeolian harp, named after the wind. The story of the bag of winds became a common subject in Baroque painting, which often depicted the scene of Juno and Aeolus.

Ancient sources: Homer (Odyssey, Book X) and Virgil (Aeneid, Book I) are the main sources, alongside Apollonius of Rhodes, Apollodorus, Hyginus and Ovid.

Trivia

Children of the god. Ancient sources give different names for his children. A scholium lists Androcles, Crispus and Iocastus, while John Tzetzes offers another list.
A rationalized astrologer. Palaephatus explained the story rationally: Aeolus would have been an astronomer who predicted the winds from the rising of certain stars.
