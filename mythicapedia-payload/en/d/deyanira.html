Deianira

Deianira is a Calydonian princess in Greek mythology, the wife of Heracles and mother of several of his children. She is best known for the Shirt of Nessus, the poisoned gift that brought about the hero's death.

The myth

According to the most common tradition, she was the daughter of Oeneus, king of Calydon, and Althaea, although one version makes her the daughter of Dionysus and another makes her the daughter of Dexamenus, king of Olenus. Her brother was Meleager. The tradition tells that when her sisters mourned Meleager, Artemis turned them into birds, while Deianira and Gorge kept their human form thanks to Dionysus.

Her father had promised her to the river god Achelous, but Deianira, who according to Apollodorus drove a chariot and practiced the art of war, did not want that marriage. Heracles claimed her and defeated Achelous in a wrestling bout, tearing off one of his horns. With Heracles she had Hyllus, Glenus, Ctesippus and Onites, and, according to the tragic authors, a daughter, Macaria. Hyllus was the ancestor of the Heraclids, who led the Dorian invasion in the tradition.

The central episode is the Shirt of Nessus. While ferrying her across the river Euenus, the centaur Nessus tried to abduct her, and Heracles shot him with an arrow poisoned with the blood of the Lernaean Hydra. Dying, the centaur tricked Deianira into believing that his blood mixed with his semen would make a love potion. Years later, learning that Heracles had taken Iole as his new wife, Deianira soaked a tunic in the potion and sent it to him through the herald Lichas. The poison burned the hero, who died on a pyre. Deianira, in despair, took her own life, by hanging or by stabbing depending on the version.

Legacy

Sophocles made her the main character of Women of Trachis, and Giovanni Boccaccio devoted a biography to her in De mulieribus claris, one of the first collections focused exclusively on women. She also appears in some episodes of the television series Hercules: The Legendary Journeys, played by Tawny Kitaen.

Ancient sources: Sophocles' Women of Trachis, and Apollodorus, who describes her driving a chariot and practicing the art of war.

Trivia

A warrior princess. Apollodorus describes her driving a chariot and practicing the art of war, an unusual image for a princess of the period.
A saving daughter. According to the tragic authors, her daughter Macaria saved the Athenians from defeat by Eurystheus.
A Renaissance biography. Giovanni Boccaccio included her in De mulieribus claris, composed between 1361 and 1362.
