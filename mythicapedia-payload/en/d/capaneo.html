Greek warrior of the siege of Thebes

Capaneus

Capaneus is a figure of Greek myth, a son of Hipponous, known for his immense strength and his arrogance during the siege of Thebes. His punishment by a thunderbolt from Zeus made him one of the most cited examples of hubris punished by the gods.

The myth

According to the legend, Capaneus was a man of immense strength and size, an outstanding warrior, but also notorious for his arrogance. The sources do not agree on his parentage: some name his mother as Astynome, daughter of Talaus, others Laodice, daughter of Iphis. He married Evadne, with whom he had Sthenelus, though some say his wife was called Ianeira.

During the war of the Seven against Thebes, he stood at the city wall and declared that not even Zeus himself could stop him from entering. Aeschylus describes his shield, which shows a man carrying a torch and the words 'I will burn the city'. As he climbed a ladder, Zeus struck him down with a thunderbolt. Evadne, in despair, threw herself onto her husband's funeral pyre and died with him.

Legacy

Dante places him in Hell, in the seventh circle, among those violent against God: he lies under a rain of fire and keeps cursing Jove, as he calls Jupiter, even as the punishment grows ever harsher. Ezra Pound mentions him in Hugh Selwyn Mauberley to suggest daring and excessive self-confidence. In Chaucer's The Canterbury Tales, Theseus scolds a widow of Capaneus for weeping, accusing her of envying his honour.

Ancient sources: Aeschylus in Seven Against Thebes, Euripides in The Suppliants and The Phoenician Women, and the Roman poet Statius.

Trivia

A pioneering ladder. Vegetius remembers him as the first to use ladders in a siege, at the very moment Zeus struck him down.
A shield that speaks. In Aeschylus, his shield bears the inscription 'I will burn the city' beside the image of a man with a torch.
