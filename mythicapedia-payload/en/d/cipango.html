legendary island linked to Japan in Marco Polo's accounts

Cipango

Cipango, also written Zipangu, was the name Europeans and Chinese gave to Japan in the Late Middle Ages and early modern period. It became famous through Marco Polo, who described an island of fabulous wealth, and that image shaped Christopher Columbus's plans.

The myth

In Marco Polo's account, Cipango was a large island in the Sea of China, 1,500 miles from the coast, inhabited by idolaters who owed allegiance to no foreign monarch. Its ruler had a palace roofed in gold, and its pink pearls were valued as highly as white ones.

According to the same account, in 1269 the Great Khan Kublai sent a fleet to invade the island. A storm scattered the ships and many troops were left stranded; the lord of Cipango pursued them, but the Mongols seized his boats, took the capital and held out through a seven-month siege before surrendering.

Legacy

Paolo dal Pozzo Toscanelli, consulted by King Afonso V of Portugal, argued that the most direct way to reach Cathay and Cipango was to sail west. Columbus had access to a copy of that idea. On reaching the Bahamas he believed he was among the islands Polo had described, and he first identified Cuba as Cipango.

Trivia

A calculation error. Columbus confused the Arabic mile with the Italian one and calculated the distance from the Canaries to Cipango as roughly a quarter of the real figure.
An island named Juana. Europeans named Cuba Juana, in honor of the daughter of the Catholic Monarchs.
