Mesopotamian god of fertility and shepherds

Tammuz

Tammuz is the shepherd and fertility god of ancient Mesopotamia, known to the Sumerians as Dumuzid and the first consort of the goddess Inanna (Ishtar). His death in the summer and his return in the autumn were used to explain the cycle of the seasons, and his cult spread to the Levant and Greece, where he became Adonis.

The myth

According to Sumerian tradition, Dumuzid was a shepherd who became king of Bad-tibira, one of the antediluvian cities in the Sumerian King List, and, in another version, an early king of Uruk. He was the first husband of Inanna, who linked him to the fertility of the land and the herds. In the poem Inanna Prefers the Farmer, Dumuzid competes with the farmer Enkimdu for the goddess's hand.

The best-known story is Inanna's descent into the underworld. On her return she finds that Dumuzid has not mourned her death, and she hands him over to the galla demons, who drag him below in her place. Regretting her choice, she decrees that he will spend half the year underground and the other half with her, while his sister Geshtinanna takes his place. This explains the seasons. In the Epic of Gilgamesh, Tammuz is remembered as the love of Ishtar's youth, transformed into an allalu bird with a broken wing.

Worship and religion

His worship centred on public mourning during the midsummer month that bore his name, a lament led mostly by women. Shepherds asked him for milk, grain and cattle, and his cheerful side appeared in the date palm under the name Ama-ušumgal-ana, though that identification is disputed. Women also baked cakes for Ishtar, the Queen of Heaven. Kings of Uruk may have taken his role in a sacred marriage rite at the Akitu festival, although scholars now debate whether it involved a real sexual union. The sources say nothing about whether his cult survives today.

Legacy

His name gave the Babylonian calendar its month of Tammuz, and his Greek form, Adonis, gave its name to the Adonia festival. The Book of Ezekiel mentions him by name, describing women weeping for him. In the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, scholars saw him as the model of the dying-and-rising god, a view that the full text of Inanna's Descent, discovered in the mid-twentieth century, called into question.

Ancient sources: He appears in the Sumerian King List, the poem Inanna Prefers the Farmer, the story of Inanna's Descent into the Underworld, the Epic of Gilgamesh and the Book of Ezekiel.

Trivia

A month named after him. In Lagash, the month of Dumuzid was the sixth month of the year. The month and its festival passed to the Babylonians under the name Tammuz.
Summer gardens. In ancient Greece, during the Adonia festival, people planted small gardens of lettuce and fennel that sprouted in the sun and then withered, in memory of Adonis.
