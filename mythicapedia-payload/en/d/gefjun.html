Norse goddess of ploughing, virgins and fertility

Gefjon

Gefjon is a goddess of Norse mythology associated with ploughing, virgins and good fortune. Her best-known legend explains the origin of the Danish island of Zealand, and she is attested in several medieval sources alongside the Æsir and Vanir.

The myth

According to the medieval texts, Gefjon was a seeress who received into her hall all women who died as virgins, making them her attendants in the afterlife. For this reason she was seen as a guardian of virtue, though she was also linked to fertility.

The best-known episode tells how the Swedish king Gylfi promised her as much land as she could plough in a single night. Gefjon had fathered four sons with a giant; she turned them into oxen and yoked them to her plough. Ploughing deeply, she dragged a great piece of land into the sea, forming the island of Zealand, and a lake was left where it had been. Sources disagree on which lake it was: the Prose Edda names Mälaren, while other versions point to Vänern. She later married King Skjöld, son of Odin, and the couple lived at Lejre.

Ancient sources: She appears in the Poetic Edda (in the poem Lokasenna), in Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda, where the Gylfi episode is told, and in the Heimskringla.
