Greek god of death

Thanatos

Thanatos is the personification of death in Greek mythology, a feared figure whom the stories rarely show in person. He is the twin brother of Hypnos, Sleep, and his name survives in modern psychology and medicine.

The myth

According to Hesiod's Theogony, Thanatos was the son of Nyx, Night, and the brother of Hypnos, and he had no father. Homer, in the Iliad, shows the two brothers carrying out Zeus's order to take the body of his son Sarpedon back to Lycia, so that he could receive a proper burial.

The texts describe him as merciless and indiscriminate, with a heart of iron that cannot be moved by prayers and that holds on to whoever he seizes first. Yet tradition also gives him a gentler side: he brings calm, frees people from illness, and brings tyrants to an end.

The most famous episode involves Sisyphus, who managed to bind the god with his own chains, so that no mortal died while Thanatos was held captive, until Ares freed him and handed the captor over to him. Sisyphus later returned from the Underworld a second time and was condemned to an eternal punishment. In another story, Thanatos comes to take the soul of Alcestis, who had given her life for her husband, King Admetos. Heracles wrestles with him, defeats him, and returns the woman to her husband.

Worship and religion

The sources say little about temples dedicated to Thanatos. It is recorded that a rooster was customarily sacrificed in his honour, a ritual gesture that linked him to the farewell rites for the dead.

Legacy

In ancient pottery he appears as a winged man carrying a corpse alongside his brother Hypnos. Virgil places him among the beings that dwell at the entrance to the underworld, and Horace gives him black wings. In the twentieth century Sigmund Freud used the name for the death drive, opposed to Eros, the life drive. Today the term remains in psychology, thanatology and the word thanatophobia, the fear of death.

Ancient sources: Hesiod's Theogony, Homer's Iliad, Euripides' Alcestis, Virgil's Aeneid and the odes of Horace.

Trivia

A device with a Greek name. The doctor Jack Kevorkian named his euthanasia device the Thanatron, a reference to death.
A dysplasia with a death name. Thanatophoric dysplasia, a congenital bone disorder that is almost always fatal at birth, takes its name from the Greek thanatophoros, meaning death-bearing.
