Greek goddess of blindness and ruin

Ate

Ate is the Greek personification of moral blindness, the delusion that drives mortals, and sometimes gods, into reckless acts and ruin. She embodies the link between arrogance, error and tragic fate.

The myth

In Homer, Ate is the eldest daughter of Zeus, while Hesiod makes her a daughter of Eris, Strife, with no father named. She appears rarely in the stories and functions more as an abstraction that explains human folly than as a character with a full history.

In the Iliad, Agamemnon blames Ate for clouding his mind when he robbed Achilles of his war prize, Briseis. In his account, Hera tricked Zeus into swearing an oath that gave the birthright to Eurystheus instead of Heracles. Zeus, angered at being blinded, seized Ate by the hair and flung her from Olympus, so she came to dwell among men.

According to the mythographer Apollodorus, Ate fell in Phrygia, on a hill named after her, where Ilus later founded Ilion, that is, Troy. In Aeschylus's Oresteia she acts as an instrument of Zeus's justice, linked with Helen and Clytemnestra.

Legacy

Shakespeare invokes her in Julius Caesar as a figure of vengeance and threat. The Latin word nefas, for what offended divine law, gave Spanish the word nefasto, 'ill-omened'.

Ancient sources: Homer's Iliad, Hesiod's Theogony, Aeschylus's Oresteia and Apollodorus's Library.

Trivia

A hill named after her. Apollodorus places Ate's fall on a hill in Phrygia that bore her name, near the future site of Troy.
Feet that never touch the ground. In the Iliad, Agamemnon describes Ate walking over the heads of men rather than on the ground.
