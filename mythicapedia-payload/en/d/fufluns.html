Etruscan god of plants, wine and happiness

Fufluns

Fufluns (also Puphluns) was the Etruscan god of plant life, wine, happiness, health and growth. The Etruscans identified him with Dionysus, and his name gave rise to that of the town of Populonia.

The myth

According to the myth, Fufluns was the son of the sky god Tinia and Semla. His birth mirrors that of Dionysus: Semla was struck down by a lightning bolt from Tinia while pregnant, and Tinia sewed the infant into his thigh until the birth was complete. In art, Semla reappears beside an adult Fufluns, perhaps as a sign of resurrection or immortality.

A bronze mirror preserves another story: Fufluns falls in love with Areatha, the Etruscan Ariadne, who has been abandoned by Theseus after escaping the labyrinth of Minos, and the two eventually marry. The scene adds figures absent from the Greek version, such as Castur, Eiasun (Jason) and a small winged figure, Aminth, hinting at a conflict whose story is lost. He is usually shown as a beardless youth, accompanied by thyrsi, satyrs, maenads and other apotropaic symbols.

Worship and religion

He was worshipped at Populonia, the town that took its name from him. The Liver of Piacenza, a bronze model used in divination, lists him among the sixteen gods who ruled the astrological houses, in ninth place. The Romans adopted him, but he was soon merged with Dionysus and his rites were transformed by Dionysian frenzies. The sources do not document a living cult today.

Legacy

His name remains tied to the town of Populonia. His figure merged with Dionysus and with Liber, his Roman equivalent, so much of his image reaches us through Greek and Roman tradition.

Ancient sources: The Liver of Piacenza, whose inscriptions list him among the Etruscan astrological gods, and the bronze mirror that tells the story of Areatha.

Trivia

The Liver of Piacenza. This bronze liver model, used for divination, mentions Fufluns twice among the gods of the Etruscan astrological houses.
Pachies or Pacha. According to the sources, he was also called Fufluns Pachies or Pacha, an epithet that distinguishes him in some texts.
