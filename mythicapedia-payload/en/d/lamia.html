child-devouring creature of Greek mythology

Lamia

Lamia is a female creature from Greek mythology, first a monster that devoured children and later a night-haunting spirit and a seductress who lured young men in order to consume them. Her figure served as a bogeyman to frighten children and left a lasting mark on European literature.

The myth

In the oldest tradition, Lamia was a beautiful queen of ancient Libya, daughter of Belus and Libya. Zeus fell in love with her and had children with her, but Hera, jealous, killed them or took them away. Lamia, shattered by her loss, began to steal and devour the children of other mothers, and her beauty gave way to a monstrous appearance.

The accounts differ in their details. Some say Hera deprived her of sleep, and that Zeus, moved with pity, gave her the power to remove her eyes and put them back, along with the ability to change her shape. Others credit her with prophecy. Duris of Samos links her to a city that would bear her name, while Diodorus Siculus presents her as a cruel Libyan queen who ordered newborns snatched from their mothers, and who lost her sight through drinking wine.

Over time, the lamia became a seductive phantom. In Philostratus's account of Apollonius of Tyana, she appears as a beautiful woman who lures young men in order to feed on their blood and flesh, until the philosopher unmasks her during a wedding feast. She was also given serpentine traits, and some texts identify her with the empusae and the mormo.

Worship and religion

No cult dedicated to Lamia is documented. Her role was mainly folkloric: in antiquity nurses invoked her to make children behave, a function that made her a familiar bogeyman.

Legacy

John Keats's poem Lamia drew on the story of Apollonius of Tyana. In early modern times, Edward Topsell included her in his descriptions of fantastic animals, and a fourth-century mosaic in the cathedral of Pesaro shows her as a bird with a human head. Traditions about lamias survive in Neo-Hellenic, Basque, Galician, Cantabrian and Bulgarian folklore. It has been proposed that her name shares a root with the Latin lemures, the spirits of the dead.

Ancient sources: Aristophanes, Diodorus Siculus, Euripides, Plutarch, Horace, Philostratus in his Life of Apollonius of Tyana, and the Byzantine lexicon Suda.

Trivia

An irreverent list. Aristophanes included in two comedies the same list of foul-smelling objects, which mentioned the testicles of Lamia, making her gender ambiguous in that passage.
A warning from Horace. In his Ars Poetica, Horace criticised overly fantastic stories and cited as an example a tale in which a boy is pulled alive from the belly of a lamia.
