Celtic goddess of the thermal springs of Bath

Sulis

Sulis is a Celtic goddess linked to the thermal springs of Bath, in England, who was worshipped by the Romano-British as Sulis Minerva. She matters because she is one of the few well-documented cases of a Celtic goddess being paired with a Roman deity.

The myth

Sources on Sulis are scarce, and much of what is known comes from objects left at her shrine. Her name is linked to sight and light: some scholars connect it with Old Irish súil, "eye", while others relate it to the root for "sun", so she has been read as a solar deity, at least in pre-Roman times.

In the most common interpretation, Sulis was a nurturing protector of the waters with healing powers, and she was also asked to punish thieves. Worshippers wrote lead curse tablets asking that the culprit of a theft lose sleep, health or even life until the stolen goods were returned.

Worship and religion

Her shrine at Aquae Sulis, modern Bath, centred on a sacred spring already venerated by the Celts. Around the 60s to 70s CE the Romans built a temple there dedicated to Sulis Minerva, and the cult remained active until the mid-fourth century. Thousands of coins and offerings have been recovered from the spring, and evidence of sacrifice at the altar survives; the water was probably used in healing rites. The cult no longer exists today, but the baths of Bath still operate.

Legacy

The city's name and its Roman baths keep her memory alive, and the museum on the site displays many of the shrine's finds.

Ancient sources: The ancient sources that mention her are mainly inscriptions and lead curse tablets found at Bath, not literary texts from her own tradition.

Trivia

Curse tablets. About 130 lead curse tablets have been found in the spring, most addressed to Sulis to recover stolen items such as gloves or money.
Sacred coal. The Roman temple burned coal rather than wood in its altar fire, which was unusual.
