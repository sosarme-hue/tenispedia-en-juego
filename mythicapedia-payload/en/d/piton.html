serpent guardian of the Delphic oracle

Python

Python is a huge serpent in Greek mythology that guarded the sanctuary of the Delphic oracle, a site the Greeks regarded as the centre of the earth. His killing by Apollo is the myth that explains how the god took over the oracle.

The myth

According to tradition, Python was born from the earth, Gaia, after the flood, when the mud warmed in the sun and gave rise to animals and monsters. He was so vast that he terrified the newly created peoples. Older versions make him a female, while Simonides and other authors describe a male. He lived by a spring near Mount Parnassus, devouring men and beasts, and Hera had entrusted him with guarding her son Typhon.

Apollo, who had learned the art of prophecy, wanted to found his shrine there. In one account the god simply shot the serpent dead and claimed the oracle. In another, Hera sent Python to pursue Leto while she was pregnant with the twins, and when Apollo was four days old he tracked the serpent to the cleft in the rock where the priestess sat on her tripod and killed it with arrows. The body rotted, and the Greeks linked this to the names Pytho and Pythia. Apollo placed the bones in a cauldron, put them in a temple, and they were said to lie beneath the omphalos.

Worship and religion

Delphi became the most famous and revered oracle of the ancient Greek and Roman worlds, where the priestess, the Pythia, delivered prophecies. The Pythian Games, held in memory of the serpent, turned his death into a religious festival. Some accounts say Zeus ordered Apollo to purify himself for the act and to preside over the games as penance. The sources describe no living cult of Python.

Legacy

Robert Graves read the myth as a memory of Hellenic peoples taking over a pre-Hellenic shrine, while Erwin Rohde saw one god raising his temple on the grave of another. Karl Kerényi noted that some accounts merge Python with Typhon or with the female dragon Delphyne. Another reading treats the story as an allegory of the sun's rays dispersing the mists that rise from marshes.

Ancient sources: The Homeric Hymn to Apollo, Hyginus, Simonides and Clearchus of Soli preserve differing versions of the serpent's birth and death.

Trivia

Origin of the name. The Greeks explained the place name Pytho by the rotting of the serpent's corpse. The Greek verb pythesthai means to rot.
Games in his memory. Apollo instituted funeral games in honour of the serpent, known as the Pythian Games.
A female serpent. In the oldest versions Python was a female, while later authors present the serpent as male.
