mythological motif of the primordial waters that surround the cosmos

Cosmic ocean

The cosmic ocean is a motif found in many traditions in which the world appears enveloped by primordial waters that existed before creation. It appears in Mesopotamian and Egyptian myths, in Norse and Hindu traditions, and in Maya texts, and it explains how a habitable space emerged from watery chaos.

The myth

In Mesopotamian accounts, at first there were only Apsu, the freshwater, and Tiamat, the saltwater, mixed together without form. From their union the gods were born, and the conflict between the divine generations ended with Marduk's victory, after which he divided Tiamat's body to form the sky and the earth.

In Norse tradition, the world took shape from the meeting of the ice of Niflheim and the heat of Muspell in the void of Ginnungagap. Droplets of melting ice gave rise to the giant Ymir, and the cosmos was formed from his body. In other accounts, such as those of peoples in Asia and North America, a celestial being or a diving bird brings up mud from the bottom of the waters to create dry land.

In many myths the water keeps a threatening power: the serpents that embody it are defeated by gods or heroes, such as Ra against Apep, or Indra against Vritra, in order to hold back the chaotic waters.

Ancient sources: The Mesopotamian Enuma Elish describes Apsu and Tiamat before creation; Genesis speaks of the abyss over which the Spirit of God moved; the Poetic Edda tells of the origin of Ymir; and the Maya Popol Vuh recounts the sea that covered everything before the land appeared.

Trivia

Tehom. In Hebrew, the watery abyss of Genesis is called Tehom, a word that also appears among the aquatic monsters defeated by the Canaanite gods.
Oceanus in Homer. The Iliad calls Oceanus the origin of the gods, and Plutarch reports that the Egyptians identified Oceanus with Osiris.
