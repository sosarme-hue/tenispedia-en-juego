coronation stone of the kings of Tara

Lia Fáil

The Lia Fáil, or Stone of Destiny, is a standing stone on the Hill of Tara in County Meath, Ireland. Tradition holds that it served as the coronation stone of the kings of Tara and, by extension, of Ireland's High Kings, and its name gave rise to one of Ireland's poetic names.

The myth

In Irish mythology the stone was brought to Ireland by the Tuatha Dé Danann, a semi-divine people who, according to the eleventh-century Lebor Gabála Érenn, learned magic in four northern cities: Falias, Gorias, Murias and Findias. The stone came from Falias and is one of Ireland's four legendary treasures, alongside the Sword of Light, the Spear of Lugh and the Dagda's Cauldron. Some Scottish chroniclers, such as John of Fordun and Hector Boece, identified it with the Stone of Scone; in their account, King Murtagh lent it to his great-uncle Fergus for his coronation in Scotland, where it stayed. The Irish legend, as William Forbes Skene observed, reverses the story.

The stone was believed to roar under the feet of the rightful High King, to rejuvenate him and grant him a long reign. According to the Ulster Cycle, Cúchulainn split it in two with his sword when it failed to roar under his protégé Lugaid Riab nDerg; from then on it roared only for Conn of the Hundred Battles and Brian Boru.

Worship and religion

The stone was central to the inauguration rites of Tara, which took place beside it at the Inauguration Mound. No living cult is known today, and the hill is valued mainly as a historic site.

Legacy

The Tuatha Dé Danann were said to have called Ireland Inisfail, 'island of Fáil', after the stone's name, and this poetic name was widely used in Romantic and nationalist verse of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. The phrase Fianna Fáil, 'soldiers of Ireland', appears in the first line of The Soldier's Song, the national anthem, and gives its name to the Fianna Fáil political party.

Ancient sources: The eleventh-century Lebor Gabála Érenn describes the stone's origin; the Ulster Cycle recounts its splitting by Cúchulainn, and early Irish literature records that it roared before a false king.

Trivia

Inisfail. The Tuatha Dé Danann were said to have called Ireland Inisfail, 'island of Fáil', after the stone, so Fáil became another name for the island.
Resemblance to the Stone of Scone. The geologist Archibald Geikie noted similarities to the red sandstone of the Stone of Scone, although today it is not believed that the two come from the same quarry.
