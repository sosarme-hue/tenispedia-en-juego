Trojan king, son of Poseidon

Cycnus

Cycnus was a king of Colonae, in the Troad, son of the god Poseidon in Greek tradition. He fought for Troy in the war of the Iliad and died at the hands of Achilles, making him a notable figure of the Trojan cycle.

The myth

According to the myth, his mother abandoned him on the seashore, where fishermen found him. They named him Cycnus, "swan", because they saw a swan flying over him; another account explains the name by his white skin and fair hair. As an adult he became king of Colonae and married Procleia, with whom he had Tenes and Hemithea.

After Procleia's death, Cycnus wed Philonome, who fell in love with her stepson Tenes. Rejected, she falsely accused him before Cycnus, who sealed his sons in a chest and cast it into the sea. When he learned the truth, he had Philonome buried alive and sailed to Tenedos to reconcile with his children, but Tenes cut the anchor rope of his ship.

In the Trojan War, Cycnus fought alongside the Trojans. Being invulnerable to iron weapons, he was strangled by Achilles with the strap of his helmet. Ovid tells that his body vanished and was transformed into a swan.

Legacy

The poet Virgil drew on Cycnus as the model for the character Messapus in the Aeneid, also a son of Neptune and likewise invulnerable.

Ancient sources: Ovid mentions him in the Metamorphoses when recounting his transformation into a swan; Dictys of Crete and the scholia to the Iliad add variants about his children.

Trivia

Swan name. His Greek name, Kýknos, means "swan", and tradition explains it by the bird the fishermen saw above the abandoned boy.
Invulnerable like his father. As a son of the sea god, he was invulnerable to iron weapons, so Achilles had to kill him by strangling.
