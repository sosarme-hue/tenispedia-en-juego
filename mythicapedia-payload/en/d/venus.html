Roman goddess of love, beauty and fertility

Venus

Venus is the Roman goddess of love, beauty, desire and fertility, and also a protector of gardens. The Romans soon linked her with the Greek Aphrodite, from whom she borrowed much of her mythology, and Virgil made her the female ancestor of the Roman people through Aeneas.

The myth

According to Roman tradition, Venus had no origin myth of her own until she was linked with Aphrodite. Cicero, in De natura deorum, speaks of as many as four different Venuses: one born of Heaven and Day, another arising from sea foam, a third daughter of Jupiter and Dione who married Vulcan, and a fourth, identified with the Syrian Astarte, who according to tradition wed Adonis. For Hyginus, Venus was the daughter of Jupiter and Dione.

The best-known story is her affair with Mars. Venus was the wife of Vulcan, but she had a secret relationship with Mars. Vulcan discovered them, forged an unbreakable chain and trapped the lovers in bed before the gods. Their union produced Harmonia. Poets and philosophers recorded this version more often than official religion did. Cupid appears with different fathers depending on the author: no father, Mars or Vulcan, or Jupiter, according to Virgil. Venus was also hostile to the Sun, who had exposed her affair, and punished Pasiphae for neglecting her offerings.

Other tales show the goddess as a protector. During the voyage of the Argonauts she rescued Butes from the sea, and she incited the women of Lemnos to kill their husbands after years without sacrifices in her honour. She helped Jason by making Medea fall in love with him. Stories also explain the myrtle and the dove: the nymph Peristera was turned into a dove by Cupid and, from then on, Venus took the bird under her protection.

Worship and religion

Venus was celebrated at many festivals and had numerous epithets for her different functions. These included Venus Calva, "the bald one", linked to a tale in which Roman matrons offered their hair during a siege, and Venus Cloacina, associated with purifying Rome's great sewer. Venus Caelestis, a form merged with the Syrian goddess, is the earliest known Roman recipient of a taurobolium. Girls coming of age offered their toys to her, and brides made her an offering before the wedding. Myrtle, the rose, the dove and the scallop shell were sacred to her. Varro counted her among the chosen gods and among the Consentes.

Legacy

The goddess's name left its mark on the languages of Europe: from her root come "venerate", "venerable" and "venereal" in English. In art, Sandro Botticelli's The Birth of Venus is one of the most famous images of the goddess rising from the sea on a shell. In literature, Lucretius dedicated the opening lines of De rerum natura to her, presenting her as the force that brings life to humanity. In the dice game played with knucklebones, the best possible roll was called "Venus".

Ancient sources: Lucretius, De rerum natura; Virgil, Aeneid; Cicero, De natura deorum; Hyginus, Fabulae; Ovid.

Trivia

The best throw of the dice. In the dice game played with knucklebones, the luckiest roll possible was called Venus, a popular pastime among Romans of all classes.
Venus Calva. A late tradition explains this epithet through Roman matrons who offered their hair to make bowstrings during a siege of Rome.
