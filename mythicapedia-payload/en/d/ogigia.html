island of the nymph Calypso in Greek myth

Ogygia

Ogygia is the island that Homer's Odyssey places as the home of the nymph Calypso, daughter of the Titan Atlas. It matters because it is where Odysseus was held for seven years on his way home from the Trojan War.

The myth

In Homer's account, after the Trojan War Odysseus reached Ogygia by the will of Poseidon, who wanted to delay his return home. Calypso kept him there for seven years and offered him immortality if he stayed with her, but the hero refused, longing for home.

Athena complained to Zeus, who sent Hermes to order Calypso to release him. Calypso obeyed reluctantly, gave him food and wine, and Odysseus left the island on a small raft.

Before he departed, Calypso advised him to keep the Wagon constellation on his left. According to the poem, the voyage took seventeen days with favorable winds to reach the shores of the Phaeacians.

Legacy

Over time the island has been linked to real places such as Gozo in Malta, or Othonoi off Corfu, and ancient writers like Strabo and Plutarch placed it in the Atlantic. In 1685 the Irish historian Roderic O'Flaherty used it as an allegory for Ireland in his book Ogygia. Today an Antarctic island bears its name.

Ancient sources: Homer, Odyssey (books I, V and VI), where it appears as Calypso's home.

Trivia

An Antarctic island. An island in Antarctica is named Ogygia after the mythical island.
Ogygia as Ireland. In 1685 Roderic O'Flaherty used the name as a metaphor for Ireland in his history of the country.
