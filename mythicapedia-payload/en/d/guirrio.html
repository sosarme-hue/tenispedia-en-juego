festive figure of Asturian-Leonese folklore

Guirrio

The guirrio is a festive figure of Asturian-Leonese folklore, a masked character who leaps with a staff and chases young women during carnival and Christmas festivities. It belongs to the popular Iberian tradition of masked processions.

The myth

Rather than a mythical tale, the guirrio is a figure of popular tradition. Its name has several explanations: some link it to the Latin guirriar, 'to laugh', and others to guerrire, 'to frolic'.

He is dressed in white, with black gaiters, a red or green sash and a tall cone-shaped bonnet decorated with ribbons. He wears bells on his belt and, using a hazel staff, leaps high to land among the young women.

Worship and religion

He accompanies the aguilanderos and Christmas song groups, and in the Leonese carnival he is inseparable from the antruejo. The Guirrio of San Juan de Beleño, held on 1 January, is considered his oldest form, and only unmarried participants over fifteen may take part.

Legacy

The tradition remains alive in festivals in Asturias and León, and the Guirrio of San Juan de Beleño is declared of tourist interest.

Trivia

A word with several etymologies. Du Cange derived it from the Latin guirriar, 'to laugh'. Other scholars prefer guerrire, 'to frolic', because of the leaps these figures made.
A whip made of bladders. His assistants, the vexigueros, used a whip of three goat bladders inflated to lash the women.
A prehistoric reading. Constantino Cabal linked him to Paleolithic cave art, with its zoomorphic masks and movements.
