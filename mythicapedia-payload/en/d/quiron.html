wise centaur of Greek mythology, teacher of heroes and healer

Chiron

Chiron is the wisest and most just of the centaurs in Greek mythology, son of Cronus and the Oceanid Philyra. He is remembered as a healer and as the tutor of heroes such as Achilles and Asclepius, which is why he is seen as a forerunner of medicine and botany.

The myth

According to the oldest account, Chiron was born when Cronus, disguised as a horse, lay with the nymph Philyra while searching for Zeus. When she saw that her child had a horse's body below the waist, Philyra abandoned him in shame and asked to be turned into a linden tree. Chiron was raised by Apollo, who taught him medicine, herbs, music, archery, hunting, gymnastics and prophecy, and helped him rise above his wild nature.

The sources do not agree on his parentage. Xenophon makes him a half-brother of Zeus through a nymph called Nais, and Suidas makes him a son of Ixion. Chiron lived in a cave on Mount Pelion, in Thessaly, where he married the nymph Chariclo and had several daughters, among them the prophetess Ocyrhoe, and a son, Carystus.

As a teacher he trained many heroes. Peleus sought his advice to win Thetis and, according to tradition, Chiron saved him from Acastus's treachery. Peleus later brought his son Achilles to the cave, where the centaur raised him and taught him hunting and the arts of war. Asclepius, son of Apollo and Coronis, was also his pupil, as were Jason and Aristaeus, who later became a god of beekeeping and cheesemaking. Actaeon learned hunting from him until Artemis turned him into a stag.

Worship and religion

The Magnesians offered sacrifices to him until a very late period, and the Cheironidae family, known for its medical knowledge, regarded themselves as his descendants.

Legacy

His name, derived from kheir ('hand'), is related to the Greek word for surgeon, and his image as a wise teacher appears in the Counsels of Chiron, a well-known didactic work in classical Athens. A moon of Saturn and the asteroid (2060) Chiron are named after him.

Ancient sources: Homer, Hesiod and later mythographers such as Xenophon, Hyginus and Ptolemy Chennus mention him in accounts of his pupils and his parentage.
