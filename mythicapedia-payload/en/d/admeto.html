king of Pherae in Thessaly, in Greek mythology

Admetus

Admetus was king of Pherae in Thessaly, famed for his hospitality and justice. His story is bound up with Apollo, who served as his herdsman, and above all with Alcestis, the wife who, according to the myth, died in his place before Heracles brought her back to the living.

The myth

According to tradition, Admetus was the son of Pheres, after whom the city was named, and of Periclymene or Clymene, as different authors put it. He sailed with the Argonauts and joined the hunt for the Calydonian Boar. His good character won the favour of Apollo, who had been sentenced to serve a mortal for a year, a punishment said in some versions to follow the killing of Delphyne and in others the killing of the Cyclopes. The god chose Admetus' home and became his herdsman, and in gratitude he made all the king's cows bear twins.

Apollo also helped him win Alcestis, daughter of Pelias, king of Iolcus. To claim her, Admetus had to drive a chariot yoked with a boar and a lion, a feat the god made possible. Admetus, however, neglected to sacrifice to Artemis, and the goddess filled his bridal chamber with snakes until, on Apollo's advice, he made the offering.

Apollo's greatest gift was persuading the Fates to postpone Admetus' death. They agreed on one condition: he must find someone to die in his place. His aged parents refused, and Alcestis chose to die for him. Euripides tells the story in his play Alcestis, where Thanatos leads the wife down to the Underworld and Admetus realises he does not wish to live without her. Heracles, passing through Pherae, wrestled with Thanatos at the tomb and returned Alcestis to the mortal world. Other accounts credit Persephone with her release.

Admetus had three children: Eumelus, who led a contingent from Pherae to the Trojan War, Perimela and Hipaso. One source adds that his wife was called Eriboea, perhaps another name for the same woman.

Legacy

The myth inspired Euripides' tragedy Alcestis and Christoph Willibald Gluck's opera Alceste, as well as Handel's opera Admeto, re di Tessaglia. It also inspired Benito Perez Galdos's play Alceste, Heinrich Friedrich Fuger's painting Alcestis Gives Her Life for Admetus and the 1986 animated short Heracles at Admetus' Court.

Ancient sources: Euripides, in his tragedy Alcestis, presents him as the husband who receives his wife's offer; Aeschylus, as cited, told the episode in which Apollo makes the Fates drunk.
