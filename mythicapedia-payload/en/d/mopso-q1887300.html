seer and legendary founder in Greek mythology

Mopsus

Mopsus is a seer in Greek mythology, son of the seeress Manto, linked by tradition to the oracle at Claros. His reputation for unerring wisdom gave rise to a Greek proverb and to many anecdotes about his rivalry with the seer Calchas.

The myth

According to the tradition, Mopsus was the son of Manto, daughter of the seer Tiresias, and of Rhacius of Caria or, in other versions, of Apollo himself. He distinguished himself at the siege of Thebes and was especially revered at the court of Amphilochus at Colophon, on the Ionian coast of Asia Minor.

The anecdotes about his feats come mainly through John Tzetzes. Amphilochus consulted him about a war: Mopsus predicted great calamities, while Calchas, the Greeks' seer at Troy, promised success. Amphilochus followed Calchas, but Mopsus's prediction came true, and Calchas died soon after. In another story the two seers tested each other: Mopsus correctly named how many figs a tree bore (ten thousand and one) and how many young a pregnant sow would have, and Calchas, defeated, died of grief.

Later Amphilochus entrusted his kingdom to Mopsus for a year, but on his return Mopsus refused to give it back, and the two killed each other in single combat. Another version says Heracles killed him. Mopsus was regarded as founder of cities in Pamphylia and Cilicia, such as Mopsuestia ("the house of Mopsus") and Mallos, where the co-founders were said to be buried in separate tumuli so that neither could see the other's.

Worship and religion

Mopsus officiated at Apollo's altars at Claros, and tradition links him with seer lineages such as the Melampodidae, the Iamidae of Olympia and the Eumolpidae of Eleusis. In the Roman imperial period the oracle at Claros reached its greatest splendour. It lay in a crypt leading to a sacred spring, whose waters were said to come from Manto's tears. There was also an oracular site at Mopsucrene, the "spring of Mopsus". The sources do not say that his cult survives today.

Legacy

A Greek proverb comparing something to Mopsus's certainty expresses his fame for infallible prediction. A bilingual Hittite-Phoenician inscription and a Hittite tablet found in Turkey are cited as independent non-Greek sources on a Mopsus and on Greek migration in Asia Minor, dated to around 1245–1230 BC.

Ancient sources: The anecdotes come mainly through John Tzetzes, a Byzantine mythographer of the twelfth century who drew on earlier sources; the text does not cite specific ancient Greek works.

Trivia

The bet on the figs. According to Tzetzes, Calchas tested Mopsus by asking how many figs a nearby tree bore. Mopsus answered ten thousand and one, and when the figs were counted, he was proven right.
The sow and the ten piglets. In the same episode, Mopsus predicted that a pregnant sow would give birth the next day to ten young, only one of them male. At dawn the prediction came true.
