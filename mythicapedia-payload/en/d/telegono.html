son of Odysseus and Circe in Greek mythology

Telegonus

Telegonus is, in Greek mythology, the son Odysseus had with the enchantress Circe, and the man who unknowingly killed his father. His story, told in the Telegony, closed the Trojan epic cycle and continued the Odyssey, which makes him a link between Greek myth and the traditions of Italy.

The myth

In the most widespread version, Circe raised Telegonus on an island and, when he came of age, revealed the identity of his father and sent him to look for him. A storm drove him to Ithaca, where, driven by hunger, he began plundering the island. Odysseus and Telemachus rushed out to defend it and, in the fighting, Telegonus killed his father with a lance tipped with the venomous spine of a stingray. Neither of them knew their kinship.

When the truth came out, Telegonus carried his father's body to Circe, and in some accounts she restored him to life or made him immortal. Telemachus married Circe and Telegonus married Penelope, with whom he had Italus, who according to some accounts gave his name to Italy. Hesiod mentions him only in passing, as a ruler of the Tyrrhenians alongside his brothers. The Telegony of Eugammon of Cyrene, now lost apart from a summary, makes him a central figure. Another tradition makes him the son of Odysseus and the nymph Calypso.

Legacy

His most lasting role is that of a founder. Roman tradition made him the founder of Tusculum, near Rome, and in some versions of Praeneste. Latin poets called Tusculum the « walls of Telegonus », as Propertius does, and the Mamilia clan of Rome claimed descent from him and Penelope through their daughter Mamilia. Sophocles also wrote a tragedy on the episode, now lost.

Ancient sources: Hesiod's Theogony, the Telegony of Eugammon of Cyrene (lost, known through a summary preserved by Proclus), and the Latin sources that record Hyginus's version.

Trivia

Walls of Telegonus. Latin poets used this name for Tusculum, as Propertius does, and the Mamilia clan of Rome claimed descent from him and Penelope.
A fate similar to Oedipus. A lost tragedy by Sophocles, Odysseus Acanthoplex, resembles the myth of Oedipus: a father tries to avoid a fate that ends up fulfilled through his son.
