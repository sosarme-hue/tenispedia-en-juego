Greek nymph transformed into a laurel tree

Daphne

Daphne is a nymph of Greek myth, usually described as a daughter of a river god, who was pursued by Apollo and turned into a laurel tree to escape him. Her story explains why the laurel became sacred to the god and why its wreaths later crowned victors.

The myth

In the best-known version, Daphne was the daughter of the Thessalian river god Peneus, although other accounts make her a daughter of the Arcadian river god Ladon, born to Gaia, or of the mortal king Amyclas. Eros, annoyed that Apollo had mocked his archery and singing, shot the god with a golden arrow that kindled desire and struck the girl with a leaden one that made her recoil from love.

Apollo chased her relentlessly. She fled while he tried to reassure her that he meant no harm. Just as he was about to catch her, Daphne called on her father for help, and he turned her into a laurel. Apollo, still feeling her heart beat beneath the bark, declared that the tree would be his and adopted it as his emblem.

Parthenius, drawing on the historian Phylarchus, tells a different story. Daphne was a mortal huntress, daughter of Amyclas, who wished to remain a virgin. The youth Leucippus disguised himself as a girl to join her band, but his deception was exposed during a bath, and the huntresses killed him with their spears. Apollo then entered the story, and Daphne prayed to Zeus, who changed her into a laurel. Pausanias and Hyginus offer further variants, and in Hyginus it is the earth goddess Gaia who shelters the girl.

Worship and religion

At Delphi, the Pythian Games, held every four years in honour of Apollo, awarded winners a wreath of laurel gathered in the Vale of Tempe in Thessaly. In Sparta, Artemis Daphnaia was worshipped at a sanctuary at Hypsoi, where she had her own sacred laurels, and at Eretria archaeologists have excavated a seventh- and sixth-century BCE temple to Apollo Daphnephoros, meaning "laurel-bearer".

Legacy

The laurel wreath came to honour generals, athletes, poets and musicians, and the title of Poet Laureate descends from this custom, dating back to the early Renaissance in Italy. Jacopo Peri and Jacopo Corsi premiered the opera Dafne in 1598 to a libretto by Ottavio Rinuccini, and a famous sculpture by Gian Lorenzo Bernini captures the moment of her transformation. Linnaeus gave the name Daphne to a genus of fragrant-flowered shrubs whose leaves somewhat resemble laurel.

Ancient sources: Ovid retells the story in the Metamorphoses; it is also recorded by Parthenius, Pausanias and Hyginus.

Trivia

Laurel prize. Winners of the Pythian Games received a laurel wreath, and from this tradition came the custom of crowning poets and musicians with laurel.
Two fathers. Depending on the source, Daphne's father is the river god Peneus, the god Ladon or King Amyclas, showing that her genealogy varies between authors.
