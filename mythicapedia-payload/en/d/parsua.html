23rd tirthankara of Jainism

Parshvanatha

Parshvanatha is the 23rd of the 24 tirthankaras, the spiritual guides who show the way out of the cycle of rebirth in Jainism. He is the earliest figure of the religion that scholars accept as historical, and he is regarded as the founder or reviver of its ascetic community in ancient India.

The myth

According to Jain tradition, he was born in Varanasi to King Ashvasena and Queen Vamadevi of the Ikshvaku dynasty. At thirty he renounced worldly life and became a wandering ascetic. He is said to have lived one hundred years and attained moksha on Mount Sammeda, today Parasnath Hill in Jharkhand. He is shown with a serpent hood over his head, and his worship includes the serpent deities Dharanendra and Padmavati.

The stories of his life come from much later texts. The earliest account is a very short chapter of the Kalpa Sutra, composed centuries after him and modelled on the life of Mahavira. Jain tradition places him about 273 years before Mahavira, which would put him in the 9th and 8th centuries BCE. Historians usually date him between the 8th and 6th centuries BCE, and some suggest his lifetime was much closer to Mahavira's.

Worship and religion

Jains worship him in temples and through images, and Mount Sammeda remains an important pilgrimage destination. His serpent-hooded image is especially popular, and his veneration continues today in both major Jain traditions.

Legacy

His teachings are at the root of the split between Digambara and Svetambara Jains, who disagree about how much Mahavira changed his message. The earliest known bronze images of him date from the 2nd century BCE and are now held in Indian museums.

Ancient sources: The Kalpa Sutra and Acaranga Sutra, Jain texts that mention him briefly; the Isibhasiyam also records his teachings.

In art

Trivia

Cobra hood. His image usually shows a serpent hood over his head, a feature that sets him apart from other tirthankaras.
A follower in the Buddha's family. A Buddhist commentary names Vappa, the Buddha's uncle, as a follower of Parshvanatha's tradition.
