Yan Emperor, legendary ruler of prehistoric China

Yandi

Yandi, the Yan or Flame Emperor, is a legendary ruler of prehistoric China who was later venerated as a fire god. He matters because traditions place him among the ancestors of Chinese culture, alongside the Yellow Emperor.

The myth

According to tradition, Yandi was the son of Shaodian and Nüdeng, and he had a son named Linkui. His descendants practised slash-and-burn farming, and the name of their line, Yan ("flame"), suggests that fire was their tribal totem. Many writers, ancient and modern, identify him with Shennong, though there was never an ancient consensus. A 2004 academic conference in China reached a general agreement that the two were the same person, while another view holds that "Flame Emperor" was a title passed down among several tribal lords.

The central episode is his defeat. Attacked by Chiyou, he later came into conflict with the tribes of Huangdi, the Yellow Emperor. After three battles, the last at Banquan, he surrendered to Huangdi, and the two tribes merged into the Yanhuang confederation. Together they defeated Chiyou at Zhuolu. Sima Qian's Records of the Grand Historian treats the Battle of Banquan as fact, which marks the point where myth begins to blend into history.

Worship and religion

The sources say little about specific rites of his worship; over time he was venerated as a god of fire.

Legacy

The Han Chinese have called themselves "Descendants of Yan and Huang". Under the Five Elements model, the fiery red of Yandi precedes the yellow earth of Huangdi, and the custom of linking a colour to each dynasty may have begun with the Flame Emperors. Ancient Vietnamese historians also regarded him as an ancestor of their earliest kings.

Ancient sources: The Zuo Zhuan, which in 525 BC recognises his descendants as masters of fire; the Shan Hai Jing, which includes a list of Flame Emperors; and Sima Qian's Records of the Grand Historian.

Trivia

A name of fire. Yan literally means "flame". K. C. Wu suggests the title may relate to using fire to clear fields in slash-and-burn agriculture.
Knotted records. Before writing was established, record keeping may have used knotted strings, perhaps similar to the Andean quipu.
