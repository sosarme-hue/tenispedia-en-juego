phantom island of the North Atlantic

Frisland

Frisland is a phantom island that appeared on most maps of the North Atlantic from the 1560s through the 1660s, placed between Iceland and Greenland. Its story matters because it shows how a cartographic error could persist for centuries and become a lasting geographical tradition.

The myth

The island first appears in 1558, when Nicolò Zeno the Younger published a series of letters and a map that claimed to describe a voyage made by his ancestors, Nicolò and Antonio Zeno, in the 1390s across the northern Atlantic. According to the story, the material had been found in a storeroom of the family home in Venice. In these letters, Frisland appeared alongside other lands such as Eslanda, Engroenland, Estotiland and Icaria.

Over the following decades, the island was copied onto maps by Mercator (1569), Ortelius (1570) and Blaeu (1630), among others, and was sometimes drawn near Greenland. Today it is thought that the name was first given to Iceland and that the double island was a positional error. Another hypothesis, defended by the Canadian map curator Alban Berson, identifies Frisland with the Faroe Islands, based on resemblances of names and place names between Zeno's map and the first printed map of the archipelago, by Lucas Debes (1673).

Legacy

The island stayed on maps until explorers, chiefly from France and England, charted the North Atlantic more accurately and removed it entirely. It also appears in the French romance Histoire de Falitz et Melliflore (1818), where it forms part of a symbolic setting rather than a cartographic claim.

Ancient sources: Nicolò Zeno the Younger's letters and map (1558) are its earliest known mention, though they were published long after the voyages they described.

Trivia

Not Frisia. Despite its name, it should not be confused with the Frisian Islands, a real archipelago in the Wadden Sea shared by the Netherlands, Germany and Denmark.
Neighbour of another legendary island. Its supposed position was also close to that of the legendary island of Saint Brendan.
Shape on maps. It was drawn as a roughly rectangular island with three triangular promontories on its western coast.
