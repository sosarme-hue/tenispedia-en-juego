Yoruba god, chief of the orishas and creator of humankind

Obatala

Obatala is the highest orisha in the Yoruba religion, regarded as the king of the orishas and the creator of the earth and of human beings. He is linked with purity, peace, the head and everything white.

The myth

According to Yoruba tradition, Obatala is the oldest of the orishas and was entrusted by Olodumare with creating the earth. Some accounts say that, drunk on palm wine, he failed the task, and his younger brother Oduduwa took the opportunity to make dry land on the primeval ocean with the satchel Olodumare had given him. Olodumare then named Oduduwa God of the Earth.

Obatala was then charged with creating human beings. He climbed down a long gold chain carrying a snail shell full of sand, a white hen, a black cat and a palm nut. The hen scattered the sand over the ocean, forming mountains and valleys, and the nut grew into a forest. While modelling figures from clay he drank large amounts of palm wine, and in his drunkenness he made deformed beings. Once sober, he vowed never to drink again and to protect people with disabilities. The well-formed figures received a copper knife and a wooden hoe, and they prospered.

Other stories, known as patakíes, tell of his beautiful daughter and her three suitors, Ikú, Aro and Ofo, and of a humble hunter who, guided by Orula, brought the abani Obatala demanded and won the young woman's hand.

Worship and religion

Obatala is central to Yoruba worship and to Santería, where he is a head-guardian orisha often invoked to prevent conflicts among the orishas. His devotees dress in white, and white animals cooked in honey are offered to him. In Brazilian Candomblé he is venerated as Oxalá, and in Santería he is syncretized with Our Lady of Mercy. His worship remains alive today.

Legacy

Every year in Ife a festival honours him, and the coronation rites of the ooni affirm his authority over the crown and throne. In Brazil the snail Achatina fulica is offered in his name.
