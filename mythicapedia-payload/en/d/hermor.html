Norse god, son of Odin and messenger of the gods

Hermóðr

Hermóðr is a god in Norse mythology, son of Odin and brother of Baldr. He is best known for his journey to Helheim, the realm of the dead, to try to win Baldr back from the goddess Hel, which makes him the messenger par excellence of the Æsir.

The myth

According to Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda, after Baldr's death the gods were struck dumb with grief. Frigg asked who among them would ride the road to Hel to win her favour, offering a ransom in exchange for Baldr's return. Hermóðr agreed and set off on Sleipnir, Odin's horse. For nine nights he rode through deep, dark valleys until he reached the Gjallarbrú, a bridge covered in shining gold and guarded by the maiden Móðguðr, who told him that Baldr had already crossed and that he should ride down and north.

At Hel's gate, Hermóðr dismounted, tightened the saddle, remounted and spurred Sleipnir so that the horse leapt clean over the gate. In Hel's hall he saw Baldr seated in the place of honour and begged her to release him, citing the weeping of the Æsir. Hel replied that Baldr would only return if all things, living and dead, wept for him. Baldr gave Hermóðr the ring Draupnir to take back to Odin, and Nanna sent gifts for Frigg and for Fulla. Hermóðr then returned with the message.

Legacy

Because of his role as a swift divine messenger, he has been compared with the Greek Hermes and the Roman Mercury, gods who played a similar part.

Ancient sources: The Prose Edda (Gylfaginning, chapter 49) recounts his journey to Helheim. The Poetic Edda's Hyndluljóð names a mortal hero Hermóðr, and the skaldic poem Hákonarmál places him in Valhalla alongside Bragi.

Trivia

Odin's boy. In the Codex Regius, considered the best manuscript, he is called sveinn Óðins, 'Odin's boy', which may also mean 'Odin's servant'.
Not to be confused with Heremod. The Old English poem Beowulf features Heremod, a Danish king driven into exile, who should not be confused with the Norse god.
