Laufey

Laufey, also called Nál, is a figure in Norse mythology, the wife of Fárbauti and mother of the trickster Loki and his brothers Býleistr and Helblindi. Her name, linked to leaves, ties her to the plant world and to one of the lists of female divinities in the sources.

The myth

Laufey is known mainly through her son Loki. In Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda, in Gylfaginning, Laufey, also called Nál, is the wife of Fárbauti and the mother of Loki, Býleistr and Helblindi. In the Poetic Edda, Loki is often called Laufeyson, 'son of Laufey', instead of the expected patronymic Fárbautason, which stands out in a mythology where descent usually runs through the father.

Snorri also places her among the ásynjur in one of the þulur, lists of divine names, and notes that this ancestry may have led to Loki being counted among the Æsir. In the late text Sörla þáttr, Nál and Laufey are one and the same person, and her name is explained as 'needle', because she was slender and frail. John Lindow regards this detail as doubtful because of the text's late date. Some scholars suggest that her husband's name, Fárbauti, 'the dangerous hitter', might refer to lightning striking the leaves or needles of a tree and causing fire.

Legacy

The small island of Lauer, in southeastern Norway, owes its name to Laufey. Her presence in literature is limited to medieval sources and modern studies of Norse mythology.

Ancient sources: Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda (Gylfaginning and Skáldskaparmál), the Poetic Edda, which uses the name Loki Laufeyson, and the late tale Sörla þáttr.
