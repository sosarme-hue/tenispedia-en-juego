Mesopotamian god of plague and chaos

Erra

Erra is an Akkadian god of plague, mayhem and war, linked to periods of political upheaval in Mesopotamia. He was closely identified with Nergal, and he is best known through The Epic of Erra.

The myth

The Epic of Erra opens with the god sleeping restlessly beside his consort. He is woken by his adviser Išum and by the Seven (the Sibitti), sons of heaven and earth, each given a destructive destiny by Anu. They urge Erra to lead the destruction of mankind, and Išum tries in vain to calm his rage.

Foreign peoples invade Babylonia and are struck down by plague, and even Marduk, patron of Babylon, gives up his throne to Erra for a time. After a debate between Erra and Išum, the god marches against cities such as Babylon, Sippar and Uruk, and the world is turned upside down: the righteous and the wicked die alike. In the end Erra orders Išum to finish the work, withdraws to his seat in Emeslam with the Seven, and mankind is saved. A propitiatory prayer closes the poem.

Worship and religion

His main cult centre was Erasalm in the city of Kutha, now lost. In the poem, his seat is Emeslam. Parts of the text were inscribed on amulets used for exorcism and as protection against plague.

Legacy

Walter Burkert compared Erra and his seven warriors with the Seven against Thebes from Greek tradition, a parallel many Hellenists regard as having a historical basis. Scholars still debate which real events may have inspired the poem.

Ancient sources: The Epic of Erra, preserved in copies from Assur, Babylon, Nineveh, Sultantepe and Ur, which also refers to the flood myth of the Atrahasis.

Trivia

More copies than Gilgamesh. At least thirty-six copies of the Epic of Erra have been recovered, more than of the Epic of Gilgamesh, according to the Assyriologist Luigi Giovanni Cagni.
A text for amulets. Fragments of the poem were inscribed on amulets against plague and used in exorcisms, giving the text a magical function.
