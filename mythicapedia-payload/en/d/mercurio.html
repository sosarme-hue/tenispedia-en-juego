Roman god of trade and travel

Mercury

Mercury is the Roman god of commerce, travelers, eloquence, luck and thieves, and also the guide of souls to the underworld. The Romans identified him almost entirely with the Greek god Hermes, so he shares most of Hermes's attributes, and he became one of the most popular gods across the Roman world.

The myth

According to Roman tradition, Mercury was the son of Jupiter and Maia, daughter of the Titan Atlas. In his De Natura Deorum, Cicero distinguishes as many as five gods bearing this name, each with a different origin: in some versions his father is Caelus, in others Liber, and in one he is fathered by Jupiter himself with Maia. His offspring vary by author as well, and he is credited with children such as Evander, Autolycus or Pan.

His exploits are many. He stopped Amphion from killing Lycus, guided Priam to ransom the body of Hector, gave Odysseus an antidote against Circe's spells, and chained Prometheus to the Caucasus. On Jupiter's orders he killed the hundred-eyed Argus and freed Io. He lent his winged sandals to Perseus and led Paris to Mount Ida, where he was to judge the contest of beauty. Not all his deeds were noble: he sold Hercules to Queen Omphale.

He is credited with inventing the lyre, teaching letters, setting the months and discovering the constellations. Ovid tells that he carried Morpheus's dreams to sleepers from the valley of Somnus, and in the Fasti he is assigned to escort the nymph Larunda to the underworld. On the way he falls in love with her, and their union produces the Lares, the invisible household gods. His main role, however, remains guiding the dead to the afterlife.

Worship and religion

Mercury appeared among the numina indigetes of early Roman religion, although his character merged with that of Hermes when Rome absorbed Greek religion from the 4th century BC onward. He was shown with winged sandals and hat and carrying the caduceus, the herald's staff with two entwined snakes that Apollo gave to Hermes. He was often accompanied by a rooster, herald of the new day, a ram or goat, a symbol of fertility, and a tortoise, alluding to his invention of the lyre.

His temple in the Circus Maximus, between the Aventine and Palatine Hills, was built in 495 BC and dedicated on 15 May. It suited a swift god of trade and travel, since the site was a major commercial centre as well as a racetrack. Standing between the plebeian stronghold on the Aventine and the patrician centre on the Palatine, it also stressed Mercury's role as mediator. He had an assigned flamen, and his main festival, the Mercuralia, was also held on 15 May, when merchants sprinkled their heads with water from a sacred well near the Porta Capena.

Legacy

His popularity grew especially in the provinces. Julius Caesar wrote that he was the most widely worshipped god in Britain and Gaul and regarded as the inventor of all the arts. The Romans equated him with the Celtic god Lugus, who was often joined by the goddess Rosmerta, and with the Germanic god Wotan, whom Tacitus describes as the chief god of the Germanic peoples. That link with Wotan survives in the English name Wednesday and the French mercredi.

In language and science, his name gave us the planet Mercury, the chemical element mercury and the mercurial plant. The adjective "mercurial" describes something erratic, volatile or unstable, recalling his swift movements from place to place.

Ancient sources: Cicero in De Natura Deorum, Virgil in the Aeneid, where Mercury reminds Aeneas of his mission to found Rome, and Ovid in the Fasti; Julius Caesar and Tacitus also describe him among the conquered peoples.

Trivia

From god to weekday. The English name Wednesday and the French mercredi preserve the association of Mercury with the Germanic god Wotan.
A temple in dispute. When the temple was finished, the two consuls argued over who should dedicate it. The people gave the honour to Marcus Laetorius, a military officer, which angered the Senate.
