nine-tailed fox and courtesan of Emperor Konoe in Japanese legend

Tamamo-no-Mae

Tamamo-no-Mae is a legendary figure in Japanese mythology: a courtesan of unmatched beauty and learning who, according to tradition, was really a nine-tailed fox spirit (kitsune). She matters because her story gave rise to the Killing Stone, Sessho-seki, one of the best-known tales of supernatural foxes in Japanese folklore.

The myth

According to Muromachi period stories called otogizōshi, Tamamo-no-Mae was a courtesan of Emperor Konoe, who reigned from 1142 to 1155. She was said to be the most beautiful and intelligent woman in Japan, with a wondrous scent and clothes that never wrinkled or stained. Her presence made the emperor fall ill, and the astrologer Abe no Yasuchika revealed that she was a nine-tailed fox serving a wicked daimyo who hoped to seize the throne.

After she vanished from court, the emperor sent Kazusa-no-suke and Miura-no-suke to hunt her down. The fox appeared to Miura-no-suke in a dream, foretold his death and begged for her life, but the warrior refused. On the plains of Nasu he shot her with an arrow. Her body became the Killing Stone, and her spirit a poisonous gas that killed anyone who touched it, until the monk Genno performed Buddhist rites that let her spirit rest.

Legacy

The legend inspired the noh play Sessho-seki ("The Killing Stone") and the kabuki play Tamamo-no-Mae (or The Beautiful Fox Witch). The poet Matsuo Bashō wrote in his famous Oku no Hosomichi that he visited the stone at Nasu. The stone split in two on March 5, 2022, prompting jokes on Japanese social media about dark forces being set free.

Ancient sources: The Muromachi period otogizōshi, and the 1653 Tamamo no sōshi, which added the episode of the stone.

Trivia

A stone that still stands. The Sessho-seki split in two on March 5, 2022, though cracks visible for several years earlier make a natural cause highly likely.
A traveling fox. According to Edo period folklore, the same fox was said to have passed through China and India before arriving in Japan as Tamamo-no-Mae.
