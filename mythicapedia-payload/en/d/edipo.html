mythical king of Thebes, tragic hero of Greek mythology

Oedipus

Oedipus is the mythical king of Thebes and the protagonist of one of the most famous stories of Greek tragedy. According to the myth, he unwittingly fulfilled a prophecy that he would kill his father and marry his mother, bringing disaster on his city and family. His story embodies two lasting themes of Greek myth and drama: the flawed nature of humanity and the individual's place within a harsh destiny. Tradition makes him the son of Laius and Jocasta. At his birth an oracle foretold that he would kill his father, so he was left exposed on Mount Cithaeron with his feet pierced, which gave him his name, 'swollen feet'. The myth says he was raised in Corinth by Polybus and Merope, believing them to be his parents. After consulting the oracle at Delphi and learning his fate, he chose never to return to Corinth. At a crossroads near Thebes he killed a man who turned out to be Laius. He then defeated the Sphinx by solving her riddle, was made king, and married the widowed queen, Jocasta, not knowing she was his mother. When a plague struck Thebes, Oedipus investigated Laius's death and, with the help of the seer Tiresias, uncovered the truth. Jocasta took her own life, and Oedipus blinded himself with the pins of her dress. Versions differ on what followed, but in the best-known one he went into exile, guided by his daughter Antigone, and died at Colonus, where Theseus received him. The Odyssey mentions Epicasta, whose fate differs from Sophocles' account. Traditions also disagree about the number and identity of his wives and children.

The myth

According to the myth, Laius, king of Thebes, received an oracle warning that a son of his would kill him. After he had raped the young Chrysippus, who was under his protection at the court of Pelops, the oracle had sealed his fate. When Oedipus was born, Laius ordered him exposed with his feet pierced, but a compassionate shepherd saved him and handed him to the royal couple of Corinth.

As a young man, Oedipus heard rumours about his origins and consulted the oracle at Delphi, which revealed the same terrible destiny. Believing Polybus and Merope were his parents, he resolved never to return to Corinth. At a crossroads, after a quarrel over the road, he killed Laius and his herald without recognising his father.

On reaching Thebes he found the city terrorised by the Sphinx. In the most widespread tradition, sent by Hera, she posed a riddle to travellers and devoured those who failed to answer it. Oedipus replied that the answer was the human being, who crawls as a baby, walks on two legs as an adult and leans on a staff in old age. The Sphinx threw herself from a height, and Oedipus was made king and husband of Jocasta.

Their marriage produced Polynices, Eteocles, Ismene and Antigone. Later a plague fell on Thebes. In searching for Laius's killer, Oedipus learned the truth from the seer Tiresias: he was the murderer and the queen's son. Jocasta hanged herself, and Oedipus blinded himself.

Accounts of his end vary. In one, he continued to reign; in another, he was exiled or imprisoned by his sons. He cursed Polynices and Eteocles, who ended up fighting over the throne, and only Antigone accompanied him. At Colonus Theseus took him in, and there he died. Another tradition, recorded by Lysimachus of Alexandria, says his remains were buried at Eteonus, in a precinct of Demeter.

Worship and religion

According to tradition, Oedipus had a tomb in a sanctuary of the Erinyes between the Acropolis and the Areopagus in Athens. The same tradition, recorded by Lysimachus of Alexandria, places another burial at Eteonus in Boeotia, where the oracle ordered that the worshipper of Demeter should not be disturbed. There is no evidence of an organised cult of Oedipus.

Legacy

Oedipus is one of the most influential figures in Western tragedy. Sophocles staged him in Oedipus Rex, Oedipus at Colonus and Antigone, plays that do not form a trilogy, since they were produced in different years. Sigmund Freud took his name for the Oedipus complex, a central concept of psychoanalysis.

Ancient sources: The earliest reference appears in the Odyssey, where Epicaste, Oedipus's mother, goes down to Hades; Homer, Hesiod, Pindar, Aeschylus and Euripides also mention him in fragments, and Sophocles treats him in his three Theban plays.

Trivia

A name with meaning. Oedipus means 'swollen feet', a reference to the injuries to his ankles when he was abandoned as a baby.
A name for psychology. Sigmund Freud named the Oedipus complex after this myth.
