Sandy Island

Sandy Island was a supposed island that appeared on maps and nautical charts of the South Pacific for more than a century, east of New Caledonia, although it never existed. Its story is a well-known cartographic error that spread from map to map until a research ship in 2012 found only deep ocean where it had been drawn.

The myth

Rather than a myth, the story of Sandy Island is a modern account of error and repetition. Captain James Cook marked a "Sandy I." in the area in 1774, but its real position lay several degrees of longitude further east than the phantom island that later appeared around the 160th meridian.

In 1876 the crew of the whaling ship Velocity reported sandy islets in the same region, described with specific coordinates. The island then turned up on an 1881 German map, an 1895 British Admiralty chart and a Soviet atlas of 1967. France removed it from its charts in 1974, and an aerial survey in 1979 found nothing there, although it kept appearing in other publications.

In November 2012 the Australian research ship RV Southern Surveyor sailed through the area and recorded depths of more than 1300 metres. The island soon disappeared from Google Maps and other maps.

Legacy

The island became a standard example of cartographic error in geography textbooks and popular science. Proposed explanations for its origin include a miscalculated position and confusion with rafts of floating pumice from undersea volcanoes.

Trivia

An undersea volcano. In 2008 the French hydrographic service found an altimetry anomaly in the area that could correspond to an undersea volcano around 1474 metres deep.
Errors that were copied. Scientists who studied it concluded that the island persisted in digital coastline databases because of a digitizing error, and from there spread into other data sets.
