first man and first king in Iranian tradition

Gayōmart (Keyumars)

Keyumars, also called Gayōmart in Zoroastrian texts, is the first human being in ancient Iranian mythology and, in the Shahnameh, the first shah of the world. He matters because he embodies the origins of humanity, justice and kingship in Persian imagination.

The myth

His Avestan name, Gayō Marətan, is usually read as "the living mortal", and in Zoroastrian tradition he was the first human, a being neither male nor female. According to Middle Persian texts, he was the first to worship Ahura Mazda. The first male and female humans, Mashya and Mashyana, bear names derived from the idea of "mortal"; other traditions make them his children, although the more common version treats them as his siblings.

In Ferdowsi's Shahnameh he is the first king, ruling a people who lived in mountain caves and wore leopard skins. He introduced kingship, the preparation of food and justice, and received the farr, the supernatural radiance reserved for kings. His son Siāmak was killed by a demon sent by Ahriman; after a year of mourning Keyumars fought again, and at his death he left the throne to his grandson Hushang. Al-Masudi and al-Tabari give Islamic versions in which Keyumars is the first sovereign of a people who chose him to bring order.

Legacy

Keyumars is a common personal name in contemporary Iran. His figure opens the Shahnameh, the great Persian national epic, which makes him the starting point of the country's legendary history.

Ancient sources: The Avesta, Middle Persian texts such as the Denkard, Ferdowsi's Shahnameh from the 11th century, and the chronicles of al-Masudi and al-Tabari.

Trivia

A name meaning "mortal life". The Avestan name breaks down into "life" and "mortal", an etymology that defines him as the human being par excellence.
A king of a world without cities. In the Shahnameh, his first subjects lived in mountain caves and wore leopard skins before he taught them kingship.
