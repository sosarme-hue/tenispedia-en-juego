Ptah

Ptah is a creator god of ancient Egyptian mythology, honoured as the "master builder" and patron of craftsmen and architects. His main cult centre was the city of Memphis, and his standing in Egyptian belief explains why he was seen as both creator and healer.

The myth

According to the Memphite cosmogony, Ptah created himself and, as creator, conceived the universe in his mind and spoke it aloud. As the words were uttered, the world took shape and the gods arose, understood as expressions and aspects of their creator. Memphite priests even described him as both father and mother of Atum, and in this account the gods of Heliopolis are the teeth and lips of Ptah, made by him through his heart and tongue.

In the same tradition, Ptah ordered the land: he set the regions (nomes), built the cities, assigned each god a place of worship, raised their temples and fixed the offerings they were to receive. In the Memphite triad he was the husband of Neith, later of Sekhmet, and the father of Nefertem. He was also said to be the father of the sage Imhotep. In later periods he was merged with Osiris, giving rise to the funerary god Ptah-Sokar-Osiris.

Worship and religion

Originally from Memphis, where his cult was centred, with a great temple where kings were crowned in later periods. He was also venerated at Deir el-Medina, where a shrine to Ptah who listens to prayers was built, and at sites such as Karnak, Abydos and Pi-Ramesses. The high priests of Ptah were chiefs of the craftsmen and bore the title "Master builder". He also held a place in the Memphite triad and in the New Kingdom triad alongside Amun and Ra.

Legacy

The name of Egypt derives from Hat Ka Ptah, "the House of the Spirit of Ptah", the name of a temple in Memphis, which the Greeks rendered as Aigyptos. Homer used it for both the river and the country. Several pharaohs of the Nineteenth Dynasty, such as Merneptah and Siptah, bore his name, as did individuals such as Ptahhotep.

Ancient sources: Egyptian texts of the Memphite cosmogony and hymns invoking him, as well as the Shabaka Stone, present him as creator; Homer uses a name for Egypt linked to his cult.
