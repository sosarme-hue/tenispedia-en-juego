Slavic goddess of the dawn

Zorya

Zorya is the Slavic personification of dawn, sometimes imagined as a single goddess and sometimes as three sisters who open the gate of the sky for the Sun. She survives mainly in folklore, popular incantations and modern Slavic paganism.

The myth

In Eastern Slavic traditions, Zorya lives in the Palace of the Sun and is the sister of the Sun, the Moon and Danica, the Morning Star, with whom she is sometimes identified. In the morning she opens the palace gate so the Sun can begin his journey across the sky, and in the evening she closes it when he returns home. She also guards his white horses and is described as a virgin.

In Russian tradition she often appears as two sisters: Zorya Utrennyaya, the Morning Zorya, and Zorya Vechernyaya, the Evening Zorya, each standing on one side of the Sun's golden throne. Some later tales speak of three Zoryas with a special task. Her headquarters was said to be on the island of Buyan, far away across the sea.

Scholars point to parallels with dawn goddesses in other Indo-European cultures, although her name is not related to the reconstructed *H₂éwsōs. She is linked with red, gold, yellow and rose, appears alongside Saint George and Saint Nicholas, and owns a golden boat and a silver oar. Some scholars also compare her to the Lithuanian Morning and Evening Stars, Aušrinė and Vakarinė.

Worship and religion

Her cult is known only through folklore, and there is no evidence of ancient temples or priesthoods. In Russian tradition she was invoked for good harvests and health, and people asked for her help in childbirth and in treating babies. In folk incantations she appears as a supreme power to be appealed to, sometimes alongside the Virgin Mary, and she was called on to banish sadness, sleeplessness and nightmares. Today she is a popular figure in modern Slavic paganism.

Legacy

Her most visible presence today is in modern Slavic paganism, where she is honoured as a goddess of dawn. The sources record no other role in art, literature or language.

Ancient sources: No ancient text names her directly. The closest sources are the late thirteenth-century Chludov Psalter, which includes a miniature depicting her, and the Vedic hymns, which speak of Ushas.

Trivia

A relief in India. A cave temple at Nashik, India, from the 2nd or 3rd century AD, shows two women, one lighting the circle of the Sun and the other waiting for it at sunset, a scene very similar to the Slavic miniature.
A Russian saying. A Russian saying claims the sun will not rise without the Morning Zoryushka, showing how essential she was thought to be for the start of the day.
