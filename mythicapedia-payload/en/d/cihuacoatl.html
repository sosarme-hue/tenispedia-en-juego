Aztec goddess of midwives and women who died in childbirth

Cihuacōātl

Cihuacōātl is a goddess of Aztec mythology linked to childbirth, midwives and women who died giving birth, who were honored as fallen warriors. Her name was also the title of the second-highest office in the Mexica state, which gave the figure a strong political dimension.

The myth

According to Aztec tradition, Cihuacōātl protected women who died in childbirth, whose spirits were honored as fallen warriors and accompanied the sun. She is usually described as a mature woman with a face painted red and black, a crown of eagle feathers and a shield, holding a weaving tool. She is said to have helped Quetzalcoatl create the present humanity by grinding bones from earlier ages and mixing them with her blood.

Other accounts make her the mother of Mixcoatl, whom she abandoned at a crossroads. Tradition says she returns there to weep for her lost son and finds only a sacrificial knife. Her nocturnal apparitions, with cries like those of a woman in agony, were read as ominous omens.

Worship and religion

Cihuacōātl was associated with midwives and with the sweat lodges where they worked, and was paired with Quilaztli and linked to the city of Culhuacan. Her figure also carried political weight, since the title cihuacoatl designated the chief advisor and second-in-command to the tlatoani.

Ancient sources: Bernardino de Sahagún's accounts record her apparitions and epithets, such as Tonantzin or Yaocíhuatl.

Trivia

An office with her name. The title cihuacoatl was given to a male high official, second in government to the tlatoani.
Women warriors. Childbirth was compared to warfare, and women who died in it were honored as fallen warriors.
