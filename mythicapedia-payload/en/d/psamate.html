Nereid of Greek mythology, mother of Phocus

Psamathe

Psamathe is one of the Nereids, daughters of the sea god Nereus and the Oceanid Doris, and one of the few with an individual identity in the Greek sources. Her story of love, metamorphosis and revenge is said to explain the name of her son Phocus, which recalls the word for seal.

The myth

According to Hesiod's Theogony, Psamathe is the daughter of Nereus and Doris, and in a later catalogue of goddesses who bore children to mortal lovers she is named as the mother of Phocus by Aeacus, king of Aegina. Later authors say that, to escape Aeacus's advances, she turned herself into a seal, which by tradition explains her son's name.

Phocus was killed by his half-brothers Peleus and Telamon, sons of Aeacus and Endeis, who were then exiled from Aegina by their father. In Ovidio's account, Psamathe took revenge by sending a huge wolf with murderous jaws and fiery eyes against Peleus's herd. Desperate, Peleus begged her for mercy, and eventually his sister Thetis interceded on his behalf; the wolf was turned to marble. Other versions say the beast was sent by divine will, or that Thetis herself turned it to stone.

In Euripides, Psamathe is the wife of Proteus, king of Egypt, with whom she had Theoclymenus and Eido, later called Theonoe, who knew divine things.

Ancient sources: Hesiod mentions her in the Theogony, Ovid in the Metamorphoses, and Euripides in a tragedy about Proteus.

Trivia

The name of Phocus. By tradition, the name of Phocus recalls his mother's transformation into a seal, since phoke means "seal" in Greek.
A stone wolf. In Ovid, the wolf sent against Peleus's herd is turned to marble instead of being destroyed.
