Eurydice

Eurydice is the wife of the musician Orpheus and one of the best-known figures in Greek myth about love, death and a journey into the underworld. Her story, known mainly through Latin authors, has inspired some of the most influential retellings in Western culture.

The myth

Eurydice was a young woman from Thrace who was married to Orpheus, a musician who loved her deeply. In the tradition, on their wedding day he played cheerful songs while she danced across the meadow. Her origin varies between authors: sometimes she is described as a dryad or naiad nymph, sometimes as a mortal, and no clear genealogy places her.

While walking in the woods, Aristaeus tried to abduct her. She fled, but she stepped on a snake, was bitten on the foot and died. In another version, the bite happens while she walks with a group of naiads.

In despair, Orpheus decided to go down into the underworld. With his lyre he persuaded Charon to ferry him across the Styx and the hound Cerberus to let him pass. Before Hades and Persephone he pleaded for his wife, and his music moved them enough to allow him to take her back, on one condition: he must not look at her until both had left the realm of the dead. Orpheus walked on without turning, but almost at the exit, before Eurydice had crossed the threshold, he could not hold back and looked behind him. She faded back into the darkness, and he was sent out of the underworld alone.

Without her, Orpheus wandered the world with his lyre until he met the maenads, the wild followers of Dionysus, who tore him apart. According to the tradition, his soul returned to the underworld, where in the Elysian Fields he was reunited with Eurydice.

Legacy

Her story has inspired many operas, among them Jacopo Peri's Euridice (1600), Claudio Monteverdi's L'Orfeo and Christoph Willibald Gluck's Orfeo ed Euridice. In the twentieth century Rainer Maria Rilke wrote a poem about the journey out of the underworld, and H.D. retold the myth from Eurydice's point of view. Carol Ann Duffy revisited her in a feminist key, and Anaïs Mitchell's musical Hadestown moves the story to the Great Depression. On screen she appears in Jean Cocteau's Orphée (1950) and in Orfeu Negro (1959). The video game Hades (2020) tells the aftermath of the myth throughout a playthrough.

Ancient sources: Virgil introduces the name of Aristaeus and the tragic ending, and Ovid mentions her with a group of naiads; Plato, through Phaedrus in the Symposium, gives a critical version of Orpheus.

Trivia

A name with meaning. Her name has been read as 'wide justice' or 'profound judgment'. This etymology appears in Fulgentius, a mythographer of the late fifth or early sixth century AD.
Echoes in other traditions. The myth is often compared with the biblical story of Lot's wife, turned into a pillar of salt for looking back, and with the Japanese story of Izanagi and Izanami.
