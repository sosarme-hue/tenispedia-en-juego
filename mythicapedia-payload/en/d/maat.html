Egyptian goddess of truth, justice and cosmic order

Maat

Maat was the Egyptian goddess who embodied truth, justice and the balance of the cosmos, daughter of the sun god Ra and wife of Thoth. Her importance lies less in her myths than in the principle she stood for: the force that kept the world from chaos and that was meant to guide everyday life, from the pharaoh down to the humblest farmer.

The myth

According to Egyptian tradition, Maat came into being with the order of the world at creation, when the sun god Ra rose from the primeval mound and set his daughter in place of isfet, or chaos. As Ra's daughter, her role was to hold back destructive forces. Each night Ra descended into the Duat, the underworld, where he had to defeat Apep, the serpent of evil, in order to be reborn at dawn. Ra's victory over Apep was understood as Maat's victory over chaos.

Her best-known episode is the judgment of the dead. In the hall of Osiris, the heart of the deceased, seat of conscience, was weighed on a balance against an ostrich feather, the emblem of Maat. If the heart weighed the same as the feather, the dead person gained eternal life; if not, the devourer Ammit consumed it. Myths about the goddess are scarce, and she appears mostly as a symbol and an abstract principle rather than as a protagonist of great stories.

According to some accounts, Thoth, god of wisdom and writing, was her partner, while other texts pair her with Seshat, goddess of writing and measurement. She is also linked to Shu, who wears the same feather emblem, and her iconography shows a woman with a feather on her head, holding a uas sceptre and an ankh.

Worship and religion

Maat was worshipped at Karnak, where she had her own sanctuary, and she had a temple at Memphis. She was also venerated at the temple of Deir el-Medina and in many Egyptian temples dedicated to other gods. Priests made daily offerings and performed rituals to maintain the order of the world, and kings often offered small statues of the goddess to show that they upheld universal balance.

Legacy

The concept of Maat became a reference point for Egyptian law and ethics, and she is associated with judges, who in some periods wore her image. Her feather is one of the most recognisable symbols of ancient Egypt, and her name was adopted by historical figures such as Queen Hatshepsut. Scholars also compare her to notions of order and virtue in other cultures, such as Greek harmony or the ideas of Confucius and Taoism.

Ancient sources: The Pyramid Texts and the Coffin Texts mention her, and the Book of the Dead depicts the weighing of the heart with her feather.

Trivia

Royal names. Some kings included her name in their titles, such as Meri-Maat, meaning Beloved of Maat.
A feather on the head. Her ostrich feather was so distinctive that the god Shu also wears it, although the exact meaning of this emblem remains uncertain.
