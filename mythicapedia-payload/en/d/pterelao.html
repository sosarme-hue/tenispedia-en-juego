king of the Taphians in Greek legend

Pterelaus

Pterelaus is a king of the Taphians in Greek legend, the son of Taphius and, in some accounts, a grandson of Poseidon. His strength lay in a golden hair that granted him immortality, and his story, marked by the betrayal of a daughter, is one of the tales of the war between the Taphians and the Teleboans.

The myth

According to tradition, Poseidon gave him immortality by placing a single golden hair on his head: as long as it kept growing, Pterelaus could not be defeated. His city was impregnable, since an oracle had declared it would not fall while he lived.

His sons schemed for the throne of Electryon and stole his cattle, which sparked a war with Amphitryon and Cephalus. In a duel between the sons of both kings, nearly all of them died; only Everes, son of Pterelaus, and Licymnius, brother of Alcmene, survived.

His fall came through betrayal. His daughter Comaetho, who had fallen in love with Amphitryon, pulled out the golden hair and left her father defenceless. Pterelaus was killed, and his realm was handed over to Amphitryon's allies, including Cephalus, whose followers became known as Cephallenians. In another version, Cephalus threw himself off Cape Leucas out of remorse for the death of Pterelaus, whom he also loved.

The sources differ on his genealogy: the English text makes him the son of Taphius, who in another account was the son of Poseidon and Hippothoë, while the Spanish text calls him a grandson of Poseidon. He is credited with several sons, including Chromius, Tyrannus, Antiochus, Chersidamas, Mestor and Everes, and a daughter, Comaetho. The story resembles that of Samson and Delilah, and that of Scylla and Nisus, in which a woman also betrays her father for love. Some scholars link it to the Comiria, a ritual of Libyan origin spread across Asia Minor and Greece, in which a sacred king was betrayed by a woman at the summer solstice, shorn of his hair and sacrificed.

Trivia

A parallel story. Comaetho's betrayal resembles that of Samson and Delilah, and that of Scylla and Nisus, where a woman also betrays her father for love.
A death out of remorse. In one version of Cephalus' death, he jumped from Cape Leucas out of remorse for Pterelaus, not for the death of his wife Procris.
