Roman god of harbours, gates and navigation

Portunus

Portunus was the ancient Roman god of harbours, gates and navigation, a deity who protected sailors and merchants. His figure is closely linked to Janus and to the idea of a passage or access, both on land and at sea.

The myth

Latin authors gave Portunus a maritime role. Through his identification with the Greek Palaemon, son of Leucothea, the god aids sailors during storms, as Ovid and Hyginus recount. Virgil places him alongside the Nereids, Phorcus and Panopea, benevolent sea deities who reinforce his protective role at sea.

In origin, however, Portunus seems to have been a god of passages and fords. According to the view of linguist Giuliano Bonfante, later taken up by Dumézil, his name may go back to a time when the ancestors of the Latins lived in pile-dwelling villages in Central Europe, where the village entrance served both as a landing place for boats and as a gate.

Worship and religion

Rome dedicated a temple to him near the Tiber harbour, traditionally located in the Forum Boarium. Every year, on 17 August, the Portunalia were celebrated, presided over by his priest, the flamen Portunalis. He was depicted with a key, a symbol of his power over doors and passageways.

Legacy

His name is linked to porta ('gate') and portus ('harbour'). From the Latin adjective importunus, associated with contrary winds and untimely waves, comes the Spanish verb importunar, which keeps the sense of hindering or annoying.

Ancient sources: Ovid and Hyginus identify him with Palaemon; Virgil mentions him alongside the Nereids, Phorcus and Panopea.

Trivia

A festival with keys. During the Portunalia, keys were thrown into a fire for good luck, in a very solemn and mournful manner.
Two faces. Like Janus, Portunus was shown with two heads facing opposite directions, on coins and as a ship's figurehead.
