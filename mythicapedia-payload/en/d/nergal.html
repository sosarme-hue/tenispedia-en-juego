Mesopotamian god of the underworld and lord of the dead

Nergal

Nergal is the Mesopotamian god of the underworld and lord of the dead, also associated with war, plague and disease. His cult was one of the most long-lasting in Mesopotamia, and his story with the goddess Ereshkigal makes him one of the central figures of the Sumerian and Babylonian realm of the dead.

The myth

According to tradition, Nergal was a son of Enlil and Ninlil. One day the gods planned a banquet, but cosmic order forbade the sky gods from descending to the underworld and the underworld gods from ascending. Ereshkigal, queen of the netherworld, did not attend and sent her minister Namtar in her place. When he arrived, the gods rose to greet him and then knelt, but Nergal stayed seated, and this slight was taken as an offence that demanded punishment.

Enki came to Nergal's defence and advised him how to approach the goddess: he must not accept a seat, food or drink in the underworld, he must not wash his feet, and above all he must resist the temptation to unite with Ereshkigal. Nergal descended accompanied by seven scorpions, carrying a wooden throne as a gift, passed through the seven gates and stood before her. He refused everything she offered until she briefly revealed her naked body and he gave in. They spent six days together, but on the seventh Nergal slipped away at dawn and returned to the sky.

When Ereshkigal woke and found him gone, she fell from her throne in grief. Namtar went up to look for him, but Enki had altered his appearance with a spell, and he failed to recognise him; he returned claiming he had seen only a minor god without a crown who blinked constantly. Furious at the deception, the goddess threatened to send all her subjects to the earth until the dead outnumbered the living. Nergal then came back, broke the seven gates and seized her by the hair to behead her. Ereshkigal confessed her love and offered to share her realm, and from then on they ruled the underworld together.

Worship and religion

Nergal's main cult centre was Kutha, in northern Babylonia, where his great temple, E-Meslam, stood. There he was also known as Meslamtaea, 'he who comes out of Meslam'. At first his worship was concentrated in the north, with a notable exception at Girsu under Gudea of Lagash. From the Ur III period he became an important deity in the south as well, and in the Neo-Babylonian state pantheon he ranked third in importance, after Marduk and Nabu. His cult persisted in Babylonia and Assyria, and a few sources suggest it still survived under Achaemenid rule. No living cult exists today.

Legacy

Nergal was identified with many local and foreign deities. The West Semitic god Resheph, also a god of war, plague and death, and the Elamite Simut, probably a warrior god, shared his traits and his association with the planet Mars. In later Jewish tradition he came to be listed among demons and was even identified with Satan. Demonologists such as Collin de Plancy and Johann Weyer described him as the chief of Hell's secret police, a spy serving Agliareth.

Ancient sources: He appears in the myth of Nergal and Ereshkigal and in the Epic of Erra, which describes his rampages and the efforts of his attendant Ishum to stop them.

Trivia

Lord of the great city. His Sumerian name can be translated as 'lord of the great city', a euphemistic way of referring to the ruler of the underworld.
A name that travelled. It appears in Hebrew texts as nēregal, showing how his name reached other traditions of the Near East.
