Celtic god of healing, the sea and hunting

Nodens

Nodens is a Celtic god associated with healing, the sea, hunting and dogs. He was worshipped mainly in Roman Britain, at the temple complex of Lydney Park in Gloucestershire, and his name is linked to later figures in Irish and Welsh tradition, such as Nuada and Nudd.

The myth

Nodens has no surviving myth of his own, and we know him mostly through his cult. His linguistic relatives appear in medieval Irish and Welsh stories, where the best-documented figure is Nuada, first king of the Tuatha Dé Danann. According to the tradition, Nuada lost a hand in battle and was barred from kingship; the physician Dian Cecht and the craftsman Creidhne made him a silver hand, and later his son Miach gave him one of flesh and blood.

In Wales, Nudd is listed in the Triads among the three most generous men in the land. He had two sons, Edern and Gwyn ap Nudd, and Gwyn came to rule the Welsh Otherworld.

Worship and religion

His main shrine stood at Lydney Park, on a bluff overlooking the Severn Estuary. The rectangular temple, with chambers at its far end, has been read as an incubatio, where the sick slept in the hope of a dream vision. Pilgrims left offerings, among them a dozen figures of dogs and a bronze arm whose spoon-shaped nails suggest iron deficiency, as well as stamps used by oculists for eye ointment. Inscriptions equate him with Mars in his role as healer, and one curse pairs him with Silvanus, a hunting god. There is no evidence that his worship survives today.

Legacy

J. R. R. Tolkien was invited to study the Latin inscription from Lydney, and scholars have seen an echo of Nodens in Celebrimbor, the Elvish smith of Middle-earth whose name means silver-hand. He also appears in the works of Arthur Machen and in H. P. Lovecraft's Cthulhu Mythos.

Ancient sources: No ancient literary texts narrate him; he is known mainly from votive inscriptions and a lead curse tablet found at Lydney Park.

Trivia

Healing dogs. Lydney yielded a dozen figures of dogs, an animal associated with healing in antiquity. One has a human face, suggesting the god might have been imagined in animal form.
An uncertain name. Scholars disagree on the etymology of Nodens. Tolkien linked it to a Germanic root meaning to catch or to acquire.
Coins and pins. The Lydney Park complex has yielded over 8,000 coins, along with hundreds of bracelets and pins left as offerings.
