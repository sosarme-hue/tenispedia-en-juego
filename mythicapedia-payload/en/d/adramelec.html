god of Sepharvaim, from the Semitic tradition

Adramelech

Adramelech is an ancient Semitic god whom the Bible mentions only briefly as a deity of Sepharvaim, a city linked to Mesopotamia. Over time he became a demon in Judeo-Christian tradition and in later demonology.

The myth

In the Second Book of Kings, the Sepharvites, deported by the Assyrians to Samaria, burned their children as offerings to Adrammelech and Anammelech, the gods of their city. The Bible says little more about them.

The name has been read in several ways. According to A. R. Millard and other scholars, it may derive from a form like Addîr-melek, 'the majestic king', an epithet also applied to Baal. Sepharvaim is usually, though not certainly, identified with the twin cities of Sippar on the Euphrates, north of Babylon.

In medieval demonology, as described in Collin de Plancy's Infernal Dictionary, Adramelech presides over the senate of demons, serves as Chancellor of Hell and supervises Satan's wardrobe. He is usually shown with a human torso, a mule's head and a peacock's tail.

Worship and religion

No temples or rituals specific to Adramelech are known. The idea of children being burned in a heated bronze statue comes from Greek sources and has no archaeological confirmation. His worship did not survive.

Legacy

John Milton mentions him in Paradise Lost as a fallen angel. Robert Silverberg devoted the short story 'Basileus' to him, and Graham Masterton summons him in the 1978 horror novel The Devils of D Day. He also appears in the video game Shin Megami Tensei IV: Apocalypse and the manga Black Clover.

Ancient sources: The Hebrew Bible, in 2 Kings 17 and 19 and Isaiah 37.

Trivia

Many spellings. The name appears in several forms, such as Adramelec, Adramelech, Adrammelech or Adar-malik, depending on the language and the source.
A demon with a peacock tail. In modern demonology he is described with a mule's head and a peacock's tail, a mix of animals that does not appear in the ancient texts.
