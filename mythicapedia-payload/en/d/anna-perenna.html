Roman goddess of the year's cycle

Anna Perenna

Anna Perenna was an ancient Roman goddess linked to the cycle of the year and its renewal. Her festival, held on the Ides of March, marked the start of the year in the old Roman calendar, and myth identifies her with Anna, Dido's sister in Virgil's Aeneid.

The myth

According to Ovid, Anna was Dido's sister, the queen of Carthage. After Dido's death, Anna fled the Numidians and ended up on a ship that a storm carried to Lavinium, Aeneas's settlement. There Lavinia grew jealous and plotted to kill her, but Dido appeared to Anna in a dream and warned her. Anna slipped away at night, fell into the river Numicus and drowned. Later she appeared to Aeneas and his men and explained that she was now a river nymph hidden in the amnis perennis, the perennial stream, and that this was why she was called Anna Perenna.

Ovid also records another version, which he says comes closer to the truth: during the secession of the plebs, an old woman named Anna from Bovillae handed out cakes each morning to the hungry rebels, and they came to worship her as a goddess. Once deified, she posed as Minerva to gain entry to Mars' bedchamber, but when he lifted her veil he discovered the deception. The episode explains the coarse jokes at her festival, held in March, the month of Mars.

Worship and religion

Her festival was held on the Ides of March in a sacred grove beside the first milestone of the Via Flaminia. It was very popular with the plebeians, who drank wine, sang for as many years of life as cups they drank, and joined in dances and bawdy songs. Public and private sacrifices were offered to ask for a prosperous year. Two places of worship are documented: Buscemi in Sicily, and Rome, where a votive fountain dedicated to her was uncovered in 1999.

Ancient sources: Ovid describes her in his Fasti; Macrobius and John the Lydian also record her offerings and sacrifices.

Trivia

Inscriptions in Sicily. In 1899 inscriptions dedicated to Anna and Apollo were found at Buscemi in Sicily, evidence of a local cult.
A proposed Etruscan origin. In the 1930s Franz Altheim suggested that Anna Perenna was originally an Etruscan mother goddess, and that her link to Aeneas was developed to strengthen her association with Rome.
