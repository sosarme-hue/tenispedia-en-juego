far northern land in Greek and Roman geography

Thule

Thule was the name ancient Greek and Roman writers gave to a remote land at the northern edge of the known world, often described as an island. Later it became a byword for any distant place beyond the limits of the known world.

The myth

The first to write of Thule was the Greek explorer Pytheas of Massalia, around 320 BC. According to his account, it lay six days' sail north of Britain, and in midsummer the sun never set there. His descriptions survive only through later authors, who were often sceptical.

Polybius accused him of exaggeration and described Thule as a place where land, sea and air blended into a jelly-like mass. Strabo doubted that the island existed, while Pliny the Elder repeated the idea of days and nights lasting six months. In Greek myth, Thule was also the capital of Hyperborea, the realm of the gods.

Legacy

The name gave rise to the Latin phrase ultima Thule, used in the Middle Ages for any very distant place. Over time Thule was identified with Iceland or Greenland. In the twentieth century the Thule Society, a German esoteric group, influenced National Socialism. The Thule Air Base in Greenland and the asteroid Thule also took their names from the ancient land.

Ancient sources: Pytheas (4th century BC), Polybius, Strabo, Pliny the Elder, Tacitus, Solinus and Servius mention Thule in their works.
