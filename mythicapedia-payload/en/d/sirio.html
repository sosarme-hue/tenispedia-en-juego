Sirius

Sirius is the brightest star in the night sky and, in Greek and Roman mythology, a divinity who personifies it. Known as the Dog Star, as the main star of Canis Major, it was associated with the scorching heat of summer.

The myth

According to tradition, Sirius was the faithful dog of the hunter Orion. When Orion was killed by the scorpion sent by Gaia, the gods lifted him into the sky as a constellation, and his dog followed him among the stars.

Another story, possibly from a lost comedy, tells how Sirius fell in love with Opora, goddess of fruit. Unable to be with her, he burned ever more fiercely. Boreas, the north wind, eventually brought Opora to him, and since then Sirius blazes each summer during the harvest.

Worship and religion

Little is known about its cult. Some sources suggest that on the island of Ceos it received summer sacrifices in the Hellenistic period, though doubts remain about whether this worship really existed. The Ceans watched its rising from a hilltop: a clear, bright star was a sign of good health, while a faint one was an omen of trouble.

Legacy

Its Latin name, Canicula, gave us the word canicula, used for the hottest days of summer.

Ancient sources: Hesiod names it in Works and Days, Homer alludes to its brilliance in the Iliad, and Apollonius of Rhodes describes it as beautiful but dangerous to flocks.

Trivia

A red star in the texts. Several Greek and Roman authors describe Sirius as red, alongside Mars and Antares. In reality it is a blue-white star.
Dog-faced men. In Lucian's satirical A True Story, the people of Sirius send the dog-faced Cynobalani, riding giant winged acorns, to fight against the Moon.
