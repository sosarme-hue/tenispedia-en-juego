Roman goddess or pastoral spirit of shepherds and flocks

Pales

Pales was a Roman deity who protected shepherds, flocks and livestock. Ancient sources disagree on whether Pales was male or female, and the name may cover more than one divinity; the festival linked to it, the Parilia, was one of Rome's oldest rural celebrations.

The myth

Pales has no leyenda of her own and appears in few stories. According to Florus, Rome was founded on April 21, the day of the Parilia, a detail he presents as a sign of the city's humble pastoral origins. Ovid, in the Fasti, describes the shepherds' ritual: they asked forgiveness for unintended offences against sacred groves, nymphs and rural spirits, and begged the goddess to keep away disease, wolves and barren flocks.

Varro explains the festival's name as coming straight from Pales, just as the Cerealia take theirs from Ceres. Arnobius, meanwhile, records the view of Cesius, who made Pales a male steward of Jupiter, a detail he uses to show how contradictory pagan ideas about the gods could be.

Worship and religion

The rite involved sheep pens cleaned and decorated, sulphur and herb smoke to purify the flocks, and offerings of milk, millet and cakes. Shepherds washed, drank milk mixed with boiled wine and leapt over bonfires. Florus says the consul Marcus Atilius Regulus built a temple to Pales in 267 BC after defeating the Salentini, and a calendar records a festival for 'the two Pales' on July 7, perhaps marking its dedication.

Legacy

Boccaccio identified her with Ops, and in 16th- and 17th-century pastoral plays she appears as an assistant to Pan.

Ancient sources: Varro, Ovid (Fasti), Arnobius and Florus; Virgil presents her as a goddess.

Trivia

Foundation and shepherds. Florus links the founding of Rome to April 21, the day of the Parilia, an ancient shepherds' festival.
Goddess or god?. Sources disagree on Pales's gender: Varro writes Pales as male, while Virgil and Ovid write Pales as female.
