Hindu sage with deer horns

Rishyasringa

Rishyasringa, 'the one with deer horns', is a sage (rishi) of Hinduism who appears in the Ramayana and the Mahabharata, and also in the Buddhist Jatakas. His story shows how a celibate ascetic was drawn into the world of men and how his arrival brought rain to a kingdom.

The myth

According to the Mahabharata, the sage Vibhandaka, son of Kashyapa, was aroused on seeing the apsara Urvashi and emitted his seed into a river. A doe, who was a cursed apsara, swallowed it and became pregnant; after giving birth to a boy with deer horns, she was freed from the curse and returned to heaven. Vibhandaka raised the boy in isolation in the forest, unaware that women existed, and the boy gained miraculous powers through his chastity.

King Romapada of Anga had offended a Brahmin, and no other priest would perform his sacrifices. Indra withheld the rain, and the kingdom fell into drought and famine. Romapada was told that only a man of perfect chastity could bring rain back. He sent courtesans who, while Vibhandaka was away, seduced Rishyasringa and brought him to Anga; the rains came, and the young man married Shanta. In the Ramayana, Rishyasringa is the priest of the sacrifice by which King Dasharatha obtained his sons, among them Rama.

The Buddhist Jatakas tell variant versions in which the sage, called Isisiṅga, is seduced by a girl sent by Sakka, loses his powers, repents and returns to the forest.

Worship and religion

According to local tradition, Rishyasringa is the principal deity of the Banjar valley in Kullu, Himachal Pradesh, where he is called 'Shringa Rishi'. His temple at Chehni Fort houses an image of him beside the goddess Shanta. At Kigga, near Sringeri in Karnataka, another temple is dedicated to him and Shanta, and the lingam with which the sage merged is believed to be kept there. Sringeri is also known as Shringagiri, 'the hills where Rishyasringa resided', and the Tungabhadramahatmya places his birth in the area. His worship continues at these temples today.

Ancient sources: The Ramayana and the Mahabharata, and the Buddhist Jatakas (Naḷinikā Jātaka and Alambusā Jātaka).

Trivia

A name from his horns. The boy is born with deer horns, and his name literally means 'horn sage'.
Thirty-two children. In the Mahavastu version, Ekaśr̥ṅga gives up chastity, becomes king and fathers 32 children before retiring to the forest again.
