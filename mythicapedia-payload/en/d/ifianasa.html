daughter of Agamemnon in Greek myth

Iphianassa

Iphianassa is a daughter of Agamemnon and Clytemnestra in Greek myth, an obscure figure often identified with Iphigenia. She matters mainly because ancient sources blur the line between the two sisters.

The myth

In Homer's Iliad, Book 9, she appears as one of Agamemnon's three daughters, whom he offers in marriage to Achilles to win him back to the fighting with promises of wealth and women. Unlike later accounts of Iphigenia, she is portrayed as a young woman who is still alive.

Sophocles mentions her as living in the palace of Aegisthus and Clytemnestra alongside Electra and Chrysothemis, yet she does not appear as a character in the surviving plays of Aeschylus and Euripides about Orestes and Electra. That absence fuels the theory that she and Iphigenia were one and the same.

Lucretius, in De rerum natura, says her father sacrificed her at Aulis on an altar of Diana to secure a safe voyage, and he uses the episode to argue that superstition can cause enormous harm.

Ancient sources: Homer's Iliad mentions her in Book 9, Sophocles names her in his work, and Lucretius recalls her sacrifice in De rerum natura.

Trivia

A philosophical example. Lucretius uses Iphianassa's sacrifice as a case to argue that misunderstood religion can lead to great evils.
One sister, two names. Many scholars think Iphianassa and Iphigenia may be the same character told under different names.
