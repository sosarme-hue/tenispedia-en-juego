Phrygian mother goddess, worshipped in Greece and Rome

Cybele

Cybele is the great mother goddess of Anatolia, venerated in Phrygia and later adopted by the Greeks and Romans. She was regarded as mistress of wild nature, mountains and the fertility of the land, and her mystery cult spread across the Mediterranean world.

The myth

According to tradition, Cybele was mother of the gods and also of humans and animals. Her consort was Attis, a young Phrygian shepherd who, after betraying her with the nymph Sagaritis, was driven mad by the jealous goddess and castrated himself. Ovid tells this story, which explains the violence of her rites.

Greek mythographers identified Cybele with Rhea, while the Romans linked her to Ops, Tellus and Ceres and reinvented her as a Trojan goddess connected to the prince Aeneas, making her an ancestor of the Roman people. Her image includes lions drawing her chariot and a crown of towers, a symbol of city walls.

Worship and religion

In Phrygia, her main centre was Pessinos, where a black stone representing the goddess was worshipped. Greek colonists carried her cult to mainland Greece, and in Athens she had a shrine in the Metroon. Her priests were eunuchs called Galli, an unusual feature in Greek religion.

In Rome, the Senate brought the stone from Pessinos in 204 BC, during the Second Punic War, and built her a temple on the Palatine. Her festivals, held between March and April, included processions, music, the celebration of Attis's resurrection and, in imperial times, the taurobolium, a bull sacrifice. Roman citizens could not become priests because castration was forbidden in Rome.

Legacy

Cybele appears in classical art, and her image of the mother goddess on a lion-drawn chariot became iconic. Her name survives in the Fountain of Cybele in Madrid, and the Athenian Metroon gave its name to a site in antiquity. Mythographers and humanists studied her as one of the great mother goddesses of the ancient world.

Ancient sources: Ovid recounts her story with Attis in the Metamorphoses, and Greek and Roman texts link her to Rhea and the Magna Mater.

Trivia

Sacred stone. Her Phrygian representation was a black stone, probably a meteoritic iron, which the Roman Senate moved to Rome.
Violets from blood. According to tradition, violets sprang from Attis's blood, so the sacred pine in her festivals was decorated with them.
