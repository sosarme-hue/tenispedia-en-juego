Mesopotamian mother goddess of birth

Nintu

Nintu, whose name means “Lady of Birth”, is a mother goddess of ancient Mesopotamia, best known for her role in the creation of humankind. Most of her stories come from the Akkadian tradition, where she also appears under other names such as Belet-ili or Mami.

The myth

According to the tale, Nintu pinched off fourteen pieces of primordial clay, seven on the right and seven on the left, with a brick between them. From these came the first seven pairs of human embryos, or seven womb goddesses who gave birth to the first humans. In another version, the gods kill one of their own, and his blood and flesh, mixed with clay, are used to create humankind. The goddess may then have become Belet-ili, “Mistress of the Gods”.

In the Akkadian epic Atrahasis, Nintu appears alongside Enlil and Enki when the growing human population becomes a nuisance to the gods. To curb it, they decide that one third of women will not give birth successfully, since a demon called Pasittu will snatch babies from their mothers’ laps. In the Myth of Enki and Ninhursag, Enki sees her on the shore of Dilmun and wants to possess her. She replies that no man will make her his in the marsh, yet she eventually consents and Ninsar is born. The Sumerian story of Ziusudra also names her, when Enki speaks of halting the destruction of his human creatures before her.

Ancient sources: She appears in the Akkadian epic Atrahasis, the Myth of Enki and Ninhursag and the Sumerian story of Ziusudra, where she is named with this epithet.

Trivia

Pasittu. In Atrahasis, a demon called Pasittu snatches babies from their mothers’ laps, a punishment that would affect one third of women.
Dilmun. In the Myth of Enki and Ninhursag, her encounter with Enki takes place in Dilmun, where she eventually consents to be with him.
