Roman god of oaths

Dius Fidius

Dius Fidius was an ancient Roman god, said by tradition to have Sabine origins, who guarded oaths and punished those who broke them. He was linked to Jupiter, and his name was thought to relate to Fides, good faith.

The myth

According to Varro, his teacher Aelius Stilo understood the name as meaning son of Jupiter, and identified the god with Hercules. Modern linguists doubt this explanation, and the claim of a Sabine origin is also disputed.

Dius Fidius was not simply an aspect of Jupiter but a separate entity, sometimes called Semo Sancus. Both guaranteed good faith: the most solemn public oaths belonged to Jupiter, while private ones fell to Dius Fidius.

Worship and religion

His shrine had no roof, since oaths were thought to be valid only when sworn under the open sky. At Rome, the shrine of Semo Sancus also held matters of public concern, such as Rome's first international treaty, with Gabii.

Legacy

His name survives in the Latin interjection medius fidius, equivalent to mehercule, meaning by Hercules.

Ancient sources: Varro records the explanation of his teacher Aelius Stilo, and the Iguvine Tables mention a related god, Fisus Sancius.

Trivia

A roofless temple. The Roman shrine had no roof, as oaths were believed to belong under the sky.
Treaties in his shrine. Matters of public importance, such as Rome's first international treaty, with Gabii, were kept in his shrine.
