Northwest Semitic sky god

Baalshamin

Baalshamin, whose name means 'Lord of the Heavens', was the sky god of the Northwest Semitic peoples, especially important in Phoenicia and in pre-Islamic Palmyra. His attributes were the eagle and the lightning bolt, and in Palmyra he shared worship with Aglibol and Malakbel.

The myth

According to Phoenician accounts, Baalshamin became king of the gods after overthrowing the realm of the sea god Yam, and he fought Mot, lord of death, barrenness and drought. The outcome of that struggle set the rhythm of nature: seven years of fertility and plenty, or else of drought and famine.

Accounts differ on his family. In the work attributed to Sanchuniathon, the deity the Greeks called Uranus stands for Baal Shamem; he is the father of Baal Hadad, who is castrated by his own son, and he struggles against the sea before allying himself with Hadad.

Worship and religion

His main cult centre was Palmyra, where a temple was built in the year 17 and later rebuilt. There he often appeared in a triad with Aglibol and Malakbel, shown wearing Roman armour. In Arabia, Safaitic prayers invoked him for rain, and blood sacrifices were offered to win it; rain was seen as his own physical presence. Tradition holds that his cult also reached Rome with Palmyrene soldiers.

Legacy

His name came to be used as a title for other gods, such as Hadad, and for Zeus. In Greek texts from Nabataea he was equated with Zeus Helios, Zeus in his role as a sun god.

Ancient sources: Phoenician inscriptions such as that of Yehimilk, dating to the 10th century BCE, the treaty of Esarhaddon with Tyre, and the mythology attributed to Sanchuniathon.

Trivia

Healing pools. According to Safaitic texts, Baalshamin was believed to give healing properties to the rainwater pools that formed.
An idol among Aramean gods. The Syriac poet Jacob of Serugh names him as an idol alongside other Aramean gods of Mesopotamia, such as Nebo and Bel.
