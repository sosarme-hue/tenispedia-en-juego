Sumerian goddess, vizier and messenger of Inanna

Ninshubur

Ninshubur is a Mesopotamian goddess regarded as the sukkal, or divine attendant, of the goddess Inanna. Her role as messenger and intercessor made her one of the most popular deities in everyday worship.

The myth

In Sumerian tradition, Ninshubur accompanied Inanna as her servant and friend through many of her exploits, and helped her fight the demons sent by Enki after Inanna stole the me, the sacred rules of civilization.

In the story of Inanna's descent to the Netherworld, her mistress instructed her that if she did not return within three days, she should plead with Enlil, Nanna or Enki. When Inanna was trapped in the underworld, it was Ninshubur who begged Enki for her release. Enki created two beings who entered the realm of the dead and brought the plant and the water of life, with which Inanna was revived. She is described as an unmarried virgin, although some records make her one of Inanna's lovers. In later Akkadian mythology she became a man, and in a hymn to Nergal she appears as vizier of the underworld.

Worship and religion

Her original cult center was Akkil, but already in the Early Dynastic Period she was worshipped in nearby Uruk. She was also introduced to the pantheon of Lagash, where her center was Girsu and several kings regarded her as their personal deity. She was further worshipped in Ur, Adab, Nippur and Malgium. Her popularity in everyday religion came from the belief that she could intercede with higher-ranking gods, and many personal names invoke her.

Legacy

Some scholars believe that the Greek god Hermes may have been partly modelled on Ninshubur, given their similar roles as messengers and travellers among the gods.

Ancient sources: She appears in Sumerian texts such as Inanna's Descent to the Netherworld and Inanna and Enki, and in the Akkadian hymn to Nergal.

Trivia

Many names. Babylonian god lists record around fourteen names and titles for Ninshubur, including Papsukkal and Sukkalanna, meaning 'heavenly vizier'.
A goddess who became a man. In later Akkadian mythology Ninshubur appears as a male figure, possibly through merging with other divine messengers.
