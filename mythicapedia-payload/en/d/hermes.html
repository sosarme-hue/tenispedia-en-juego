Greek Olympian god, herald and messenger of the gods

Hermes

Hermes is one of the Olympian gods of Greek mythology, herald and messenger of the gods and protector of travelers, merchants and thieves. A archetype of the divine trickster, he links separated realms, from the world of the living to the underworld, and his name gave rise to words such as hermeneutics and hermaphrodite.

The myth

According to tradition, Hermes is the son of Zeus and the Pleiad Maia. His first night of life already reveals his character: he slips out of his mother's care and steals the cattle of his brother Apollo, an episode that makes him the protector of thieves. The most extensive accounts of his exploits are found in the Homeric Hymn to Hermes, which presents him as a many-sided deity. He is also credited with inventing the lyre and the syrinx, teaching letters, establishing the months and discovering the constellations.

In most stories he acts as messenger of Zeus, but when he acts on his own initiative he relies on cunning and deception rather than direct violence. Homer already shows him sharing the role of herald with Iris. His role as interpreter explains his link with speech and mediation between languages, which is why the tongues of sacrificed animals were offered to him. Hermes also guides the souls of the dead toward the underworld and, according to the Homeric Hymn to Demeter, leads Core back to her mother Demeter. He is further said to carry dreams to mortals.

His offspring include Pan, Hermaphroditus, Priapus, Tychon, Abderus, Etalides, Autolycus, Silenus, Myrtilus and Daphnis. His partners include Antianeira and Daera. Genealogies vary by author: in some traditions Pan is Hermes's son, while in others he is an older deity tied to the mountains of Arcadia, from whom Hermes inherited functions such as guiding travelers and serving as messenger.

Worship and religion

Athenian generals made sacrifices to Hermes before preparing an expedition, invoking him as Hegemonios, 'the one who leads', or Agetor, 'leader'. Many of his statues were set up on roads and at gates, and travelers offered him sacrifices before setting out, seeking his protection on dangerous journeys. The herms, stone pillars crowned with his head, marked boundaries, roads and graves. In dice games an olive leaf was offered to him, since he was regarded as a giver of wealth and good luck. Later he was also assigned the protection of gymnasiums, a function that developed late. The sources do not document a cult that survives today.

Legacy

His name gave rise to words such as hermeneutics, hermaphrodite, hermetism and herm. In late antiquity he lent his name and identity to Hermanubis, Hermopolis and Hermes Trismegistus. The Romans identified him with Mercury, a name linked to merx, 'merchandise', the origin of the words merchant and commerce. The caduceus is today a symbol of commerce, and after the Renaissance it appeared in heraldic crests. In modern times his image was sometimes confused with the Rod of Asclepius, which bears a single snake and is an emblem of medicine.

Ancient sources: The Homeric Hymn to Hermes and the Homeric Hymn to Demeter, as well as the Homeric poems, where he already appears as herald of the gods.

Trivia

A lucky find. In Greek, an unexpected stroke of luck was called hermaion, a term that reflects the god's association with the unforeseen and the favorable.
A proposed Mesopotamian origin. In 1916, Arthur Frothingham suggested that Hermes descended from a Mesopotamian snake god similar to Ningishzida, who also appears holding a caduceus in art.
