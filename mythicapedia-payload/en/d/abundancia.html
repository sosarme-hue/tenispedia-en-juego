Roman goddess of abundance and prosperity

Abundantia

Abundantia (Latin for abundance) was the Roman goddess who personified prosperity and success. She was one of the virtues that imperial propaganda linked to the Golden Age under the emperor, and her most recognisable attribute is the cornucopia.

The myth

According to the Augustan poet Ovid, the goddess has a part in the story of Acheloüs, the river god, from whose forehead Hercules tore one horn. The naiads took the horn and turned it into the cornucopia, which was then granted to Abundantia. Other myths explain the origin of this object in different ways.

She has little mythology of her own and appears mostly in art, coinage and literature. On Neronian coins she is linked with Ceres and equated with Annona, who embodied the grain supply. Like Annona, she was a virtue in action in places such as the harbour where grain entered the city.

Worship and religion

Her cult is known mainly through art and imperial coinage. On a vase from Lezoux, in the Roman province of Gallia Aquitania, she appears in Mithraic iconography, seated and holding a cornucopia as a symbol of the abundance that stems from Mithras' act. There is no evidence that her worship survived as such.

Legacy

In later Western art she is often shown with her cornucopia and sheaves of corn or wheat. She may have survived in some form in Roman Gaul and medieval France: the bishop William of Auvergne mentions a Domina Abundia, who also appears in the Roman de la Rose as Dame Habonde, a figure who brought prosperity to those who left her offerings and whom he regarded as idolatry. Nineteenth-century folklorists linked her to Celtic fairies.

Ancient sources: The Augustan poet Ovid, in the myth of Acheloüs and the origin of the cornucopia.

Trivia

A horn with a history. According to Ovid, the cornucopia was born from a horn torn from a river god. Other myths give different origins for it.
A goddess on coins. Abundantia appears on the coins of many emperors, from Trajan to Galerius, with several different inscriptions.
