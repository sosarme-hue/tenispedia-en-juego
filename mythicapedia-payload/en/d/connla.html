son of Cú Chulainn, hero of the Ulster Cycle

Connla

Connla is the son of Cú Chulainn and the warrior woman Aífe, a character in the Ulster Cycle of Irish mythology. His story, in which a father unknowingly kills his own son, is one of the best-known tales in Celtic folklore.

The myth

According to the tale Aided Óenfhir Aífe, Cú Chulainn left his son in Scotland in his mother Aífe's care, giving her a gold thumb-ring as a token. Once grown, Connla set out for Ireland under three geasa: he could not turn back, he must never refuse a challenge, and he must never reveal his name.

When he landed at Tracht Eisi, his martial skill drew the attention of the Ulaid. Conall Cernach went out to face him but was knocked off his feet by a sling shot and disarmed. When Cú Chulainn approached, his wife Emer warned him that the youth was his own son, but he would not step back. In the struggle in the water, Connla gained the upper hand until his father used the gae bolga, a weapon only he had been taught to use. Dying, Connla greeted each hero of the Ulaid in turn, and the tale says that for three days no calf of the region's cattle survived.

Legacy

W. B. Yeats retold Connla's death in the poem "Cuchulain's Fight with the Sea" (1892), though in it the youth is made the son of Emer rather than Aífe.

Ancient sources: The earliest version survives in the Yellow Book of Lecan, written in late Old Irish and dated to the 9th or 10th century; a later version appears in manuscript TCD 1336.

Trivia

Parallel with Theseus. Connla's story resembles that of Theseus, who was raised far away by his mother, takes up the tokens left by his father Aegeus, and is recognised by him after a series of combats.
Rostam and Sohrab. The Persian Shahnameh tells of a duel between father and son, with a jewel as a token of identity, that closely resembles Connla's death.
