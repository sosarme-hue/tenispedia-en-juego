Canaanite goddess of war and fertility

Anat

Anat was a goddess of Semitic origin, worshipped at Ugarit and other Canaanite cities, who combined warfare with fertility. Her role as a fierce warrior and ally of the god Baal makes her one of the most vigorous figures in the Bronze Age pantheon.

The myth

In the Ugaritic texts, written during the Bronze Age, Anat appears mainly in the Baal Cycle. There she is a loyal ally of the storm god, helping him win a palace of his own, and when Mot, the personification of death, kills him, she mourns him and takes revenge on Mot. The nature of her relationship with Baal is uncertain, and researchers still debate whether they were lovers, siblings, or both.

Egyptian tradition tells her story differently. In the contest between Horus and Set, Anat is described as a foreign daughter of Ra, and the goddess Neit proposes giving her in marriage to Set as the prize for the loser. In another tale, Set turns into a ram and assaults her; Anat expels his semen with such force that it strikes Set on the head and wounds him, and Ra sends Isis to heal him.

Worship and religion

Anat received regular offerings at Ugarit, and her worship spread through Phoenicia, Syria, Cyprus, Israel, Palestine and Egypt. In Egypt she had temples at Avaris and Beth-Shan, was worshipped at Memphis, and Ramesses II enlarged her shrine at Pi-Ramesses. She is usually shown armed, and some sources call her the «Queen of Heaven». On Cyprus she was associated with the Greek goddess Athena because of their similar character.

Legacy

Her name appears in Egyptian personal names: a ruler of the Sixteenth Dynasty was called Anat-her, Ramesses II's daughter was named Bint-Anat («Daughter of Anat»), and one of his horses was called Ana-herte. A dog carved in a temple at Beit el-Wali bore the name «Anat is in power».

Ancient sources: The Ugaritic texts, such as the Baal Cycle and the Epic of Aqhat, along with Egyptian and Phoenician inscriptions that mention her.

Trivia

A daughter with her name. Ramesses II named his daughter Bint-Anat, meaning «Daughter of Anat», and she later became his wife.
A divine horse. One of Ramesses II's horses was named Ana-herte, meaning «Anat is satisfied».
