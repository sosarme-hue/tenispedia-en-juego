local Greek goddess of the island of Aegina

Aphaia

Aphaia was a local Greek goddess worshipped almost exclusively at her sanctuary on the island of Aegina, in the Saronic Gulf. Over time she came to be identified with Athena, Artemis and the nymph Britomartis, and her temple is one of the most notable buildings of Classical Greece.

The myth

According to the Cretan tradition recorded by Pausanias, Britomartis was the daughter of Zeus and Karme, granddaughter of Euboulos, and a huntress much loved by Artemis. Fleeing Minos, who had fallen in love with her, she threw herself into fishing nets, and Artemis made her a goddess.

Antoninus Liberalis tells a different version: Britomartis reached Aegina in a boat with the fisherman Andromedes, who tried to seize her. The young woman leapt into the sea and took refuge in a wood, where she vanished. From then on the Aeginetans called her Aphaia, the name that on Crete corresponded to Diktynna, and they offered sacrifices on that spot.

Worship and religion

Aphaia was worshipped almost only at her sanctuary on Aegina, on a 160-metre peak at the northeastern end of the island. The surviving temple was built around 500 BCE on the site of an earlier one that had burned around 510 BCE. Archaeology suggests she was already a local deity linked to fertility and the agricultural cycle, and under Athenian dominance she was identified with Athena and Artemis. A potsherd found in the precinct of the temple of Apollo at Bassae, in Arcadia, may bear a dedication to her, which would be the first known inscription to her outside Aegina.

Ancient sources: Pausanias, in his Description of Greece, and Antoninus Liberalis, in his Metamorphoses, along with a lost ode by Pindar composed for the Aeginetans.

Trivia

An inscription beyond Aegina. A 5th-century BCE potsherd found at Bassae (Arcadia) may carry a dedication to Aphaia. If confirmed, it would be the first written mention of the goddess outside her island.
Two names for one goddess. The Aeginetans called her Aphaia, while on Crete she was known as Diktynna, a name tied to the nets into which Britomartis threw herself.
