Egyptian god of wisdom, writing and the Moon

Thoth

Thoth is the Egyptian god of wisdom, writing, magic and the Moon, usually shown with the head of an ibis or, less often, of a baboon. He matters because he acted as scribe of the gods and counselor of Ra, and because, after the Greeks arrived, he merged with their Hermes, giving rise to the figure of Hermes Trismegistus. According to the tradition, Thoth created the five epagomenal days that completed the calendar. In the version recorded by Plutarch, the year had only 360 days and the goddess Nut was barren during them. Thoth won a portion of the Moon's light, five days, and in that time Osiris, Set, Isis, Horus and Nephthys were born. In the Osiris cycle, Thoth gives Isis the words that allow her to conceive Horus. Later, his counsel and enchantments help Horus defeat Set. He also appears as a wise adviser and, in some tales, as the husband or father of Seshat, goddess of writing. At the judgment of Osiris, Thoth records the result of weighing the deceased's heart against Maat's feather. When the scales are level, an ape form of the god, Aani, signals it. For this role he is regarded as the scribe who records divine decrees. His cult centred on Hermopolis Magna, where he presided over the House of Life, and was also honoured at Hermopolis Parva, Serabit el-Khadim and Tuna el-Gebel, where a necropolis of baboons and ibises lies. His festivals fell on the 1st and 19th days of the first month of the season Akhet. In the Late Period his cult grew in importance, and millions of ibises were mummified in his honour. Scribes often kept an image of Thoth in their offices. In the Book of the Dead, copied on the papyrus of Ani, the scribe addresses Thoth as the bearer of his ink-jar and writing palette. In the Greek era, thinkers linked him with Hermes, and that union produced Hermes Trismegistus, author of the Hermetica. The Greek name of his cult city, Hermopolis, means the city of Hermes. Plato mentions him in the Phaedrus as the inventor of writing.
