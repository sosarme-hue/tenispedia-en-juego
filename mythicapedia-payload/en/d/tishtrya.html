Zoroastrian god of rain and the star Sirius

Tishtrya

Tishtrya, also known as Tishtar or Tir, is a benevolent yazata of Zoroastrianism and ancient Iranian religion, linked to rain and to the star Sirius. He matters because he frees the heavenly waters that sustain life on earth.

The myth

According to the Avestan tradition, Tishtrya fights the demon Apaosha, the embodiment of drought. Weakened by a lack of human worship, he initially loses, and he appeals to Ahura Mazda, who offers a sacrifice that restores his strength. Empowered, the god defeats the demon and rain flows over fields and pastures. The story stresses the value of votive offerings.

In the Tishtar Yasht, his swift movement toward the waters is twice compared to an arrow shot by Arash, the best archer among the Iranians, which gives him the epithet of swift flyer.

Worship and religion

The thirteenth day of each month and the fourth month of the Zoroastrian calendar bear his name, and the Tiregan festival was linked to him. In the Achaemenid and Hellenistic periods he was identified with Sirius and with Apollo, patron of Delphi, and was thus associated with oracles.

Legacy

The fourth month of the Iranian civil calendar is still called Tir. In modern Persian, Tishtar refers to Sirius.

Ancient sources: The Avesta, especially the Tishtar Yasht, and Pahlavi texts such as the Bundahishn.

Trivia

An Avestan hymn. The account of his struggle was incorporated by Ferdowsi into the Shahnameh.
A swift arrow. His movement is compared to an arrow, and his epithet means swift-flying.
