Irish hero and hunter of the Fenian Cycle

Fionn mac Cumhaill

Fionn mac Cumhaill is a hunter, warrior, druid and poet from Irish Celtic mythology, whose stories also spread to Scotland and the Isle of Man. He leads the Fianna, a band of young roving hunter-warriors, and his wisdom comes from a mysterious salmon.

The myth

According to the tale, Fionn was the posthumous son of Cumhall, leader of the Fianna, and Muirne. After his father fell at the Battle of Cnucha, killed by Goll mac Morna, the boy was born in the house of Fiacal mac Conchinn and raised in secret in the forest of Sliabh Bladhma by the druid Bodhmall and the warrior woman Liath Luachra, who taught him hunting and war. As an adult he served local kings incognito, but each one sent him away once they recognised him, fearing they could not protect him from his enemies.

As a young man, the poet Finn Éces had spent seven years trying to catch the salmon of knowledge. When he finally caught it, he asked Fionn to cook it. While roasting it, the boy burned his thumb and put it in his mouth, gaining the fish's wisdom; from then on, sucking his thumb revealed whatever he wished to know. Later, the tradition says, he slew Áillen, the fire-breathing being who each Samhain lulled the men of Tara to sleep and burned the palace. Afterwards he was recognised as leader of the Fianna, and Goll became his loyal follower.

In the tale of Diarmuid and Gráinne, an aged Fionn pursues the couple who fled his intended wedding. Years later, during a boar hunt, Diarmuid is fatally wounded; Fionn lets the healing water slip through his fingers before reaching him, and Diarmuid dies. As for his end, a popular version says he sleeps in a cave beneath Dublin, ready to wake and defend Ireland in its hour of need.

Legacy

The name Fionn is linked to the Fenian Brotherhood, the nineteenth-century Irish revolutionary organisation that took its name from these legends. The Scottish name Fingal comes from James Macpherson's epic rewriting of the stories.

Ancient sources: The stories of Fionn form the Fenian Cycle, largely narrated by his son Oisín, and are preserved in medieval Irish texts such as The Boyhood Deeds of Fionn and the Acallam na Senórach.

Trivia

The Giant's Causeway. According to legend, Fionn built the Giant's Causeway as a path of stones to Scotland so he would not wet his feet. It shares its hexagonal basalt columns with Fingal's Cave.
A wise thumb. In the legend, Fionn's power comes from burning his thumb while cooking the salmon. From then on, sucking it let him learn whatever he wished.
