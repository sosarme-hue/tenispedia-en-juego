ancient Egyptian protector deity of the home

Bes

Bes is a protective deity of ancient Egypt, shown as a bearded dwarf with a thick mane, who watched over households, mothers, children and childbirth. He was worshipped alongside his female counterpart, Beset, and is one of the most recognisable figures in Egyptian art, since unlike most gods he is shown facing the viewer.

The myth

According to Egyptian tradition, Bes defended everything good and opposed everything bad. His main role was to guard homes at night, when malevolent spirits could attack sleepers. His image was therefore placed on doors, and amulets shaped like headrests were made in his honour. His frightening appearance was also believed to ward off venomous snakes and insects, and he was linked to musical instruments such as the harp and the drum.

His figure combines traits from several minor deities. He is usually shown naked or wearing a lion skin, with a feathered crown, sometimes with a large erect phallus, and holding knives or musical instruments. Because he drove away evil, he also came to stand for the pleasures of life, such as music, dance and sexual delight, and he was regarded as a protector of marriage. Some of his titles, such as «Lord of Punt» or «Lord of Nubia», point to a southern origin. Many scholars suggest his cult came from Nubia, perhaps because the word bes may have meant «cat» in Nubian, and because of his feline features and darker complexion.

Worship and religion

Bes was a household protector, and his images were kept in homes. His cult is best attested in the New Kingdom, although its beginnings may be earlier, and he is already mentioned in the Pyramid Texts. In the Ptolemaic period, chambers painted with his image and that of Beset were built at Saqqara, which Egyptologists link to fertility and healing rituals. He also appeared in tattoos on the thighs of dancers, musicians and servant girls. A ritual vase from the 2nd century BCE contained traces of Peganum harmala and Nymphaea caerulea, suggesting a magical or religious use of the drink. Evidence of his worship survives into the Coptic era, with a jug from Tell Edfu dated to the 10th century CE.

Legacy

Bes appears in Neil Gaiman's Sandman comics, in the animated series Mummies Alive! as a trickster, in Tad Williams's novel Mountain of Black Glass, and in Rick Riordan's The Kane Chronicles. He also features as a boss in the video game Realm of the Mad God and in Enki Bilal's Nikopol Trilogy. In the Egyptian film Secret Service Suitor he appears as a god of love. On Ibiza, the island the Phoenicians called the island of Bes and the Romans knew as Ebusus, his name survives in the island's early history.

Ancient sources: The Pyramid Texts are the earliest written sources that mention him.

Trivia
