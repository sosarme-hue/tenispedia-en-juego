Gallo-Roman goddess of horses

Epona

Epona was the Gallo-Roman goddess of horses, fertility and the protection of riders. Her cult reached Rome itself, where she became a favourite of the imperial cavalry and the only Celtic deity worshipped there in her own right.

The myth

Ancient writers rarely tell stories about Epona, so what we know of her origins comes mainly from later versions. One of them, preserved by Pseudo-Plutarch in the Parallela Minora, presents her as the daughter of a man named Fulvius Stellus, who hated women and had relations with a mare. From that union a girl was born and named Epona, who in the tale became the protector of horses.

Her link with horses also made her a guide for souls into the afterlife. She was shown seated on a horse, standing among a herd or feeding foals, and sometimes holding a key or a map to lead the dead onward. Many scholars point to her parallel with Rhiannon, a figure of Welsh legend who is also closely tied to horses.

Worship and religion

In Gaul and across the empire she was honoured as the guardian of horses, mules and donkeys, and of those who bred, tended or rode them. Images and altars to her were often placed in stables, and a festival in her honour was held in Rome on 18 December. There is no evidence that her worship survives today, although some folklorists have linked British customs such as the Padstow hobby-horse parades and the Welsh Mari Lwyd tradition to her, without firm proof.

Legacy

Her name lives on in the mare Epona of The Legend of Zelda series, who carries the hero on his journeys. She has also inspired folk metal songs, including A Rose for Epona by Eluveitie and Las crines de Epona by Salduie. The Welsh word ebol, meaning foal, preserves the same root.

Ancient sources: Juvenal, in his Satires, links her with stables; Apuleius mentions her in The Golden Ass, in a stable in Thessaly; and Pseudo-Plutarch records her in the Parallela Minora.

Trivia

The only Gallic goddess in the Roman pantheon. Epona was the only Gallic deity fully integrated into the Roman pantheon, which was unusual for Celtic gods adopted by Rome.
An inscription in Zamora. On the façade of the church at Paramio, in Zamora, an inscription to the goddess survives, spelling her name with a double P, which is not found in other inscriptions in the Iberian Peninsula.
