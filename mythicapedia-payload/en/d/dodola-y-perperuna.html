Slavic rain-calling rites of the Balkans

Dodola and Perperuna

Dodola and Perperuna are pagan rain-calling customs spread across the Balkans until the 20th century. They are traditionally associated with Slavic culture, although their origin remains debated.

The myth

This is not a myth with a fixed story but a rite whose core is songs and dances performed by girls or boys in procession. A main performer is dressed in fresh branches, leaves and herbs, and the group sings to bring rain during droughts, especially in summer, when crops, pastures and even human life are at risk.

According to one interpretation, Perperuna was a rain goddess and the wife of Perun, the thunder god. Recent research questions the existence of a female Slavic goddess and also proposes earlier Balkan influences. Albanian ritual songs invoke the Sun (Dielli), the sky or weather deity (Perëndi) and Ilia, Elijah, who in Christianized folklore has replaced the sun and thunder god.

Worship and religion

The rites worked as analogical, imitative magic meant to produce rain by imitation. Today they survive only in a few remote Albanian ethnographic regions, and only in rare years when summer is dry and without rainfall.

Legacy

Its name appears in many variants across Balkan languages and in Romanian, and etymologists have proposed links to words for pepper-tree and poppy, though these remain debated. It also serves as a case study for ritual parallels in the Caucasus, the Middle East and North Africa.

Ancient sources: The Vedas include prayers for rain addressed to the deity Parjanya, and medieval Russian chronicles such as those of Novgorod and Pskov record phrases that some scholars link to the custom.

Trivia

Many names. The custom goes by dozens of names depending on the people and region, such as Peperuda in Romania or Prporuša in Croatia.
A possible Lithuanian link. Dodola has been suggested to be related to Dundulis, the Lithuanian word for thunder and another name of the Baltic god Perkūnas.
