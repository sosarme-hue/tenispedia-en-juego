Mesopotamian god of writing and wisdom

Nabu

Nabu is the patron god of scribes, literacy, wisdom and the rational arts in Babylonian and Assyrian religion. Son of Marduk, he was especially honoured in the nearby city of Borsippa, and he shows how Mesopotamians understood learning and the recording of destiny.

The myth

According to Babylonian tradition, Nabu is the son of Marduk and his wife Zarpanitu, and the grandson of Enki. His wife was the goddess Tashmet. His standing grew as Marduk rose to become king of the gods, since Nabu took over the role of chief son of the father of the gods, once held by Ninurta, son of Enlil. He came to be presented as Marduk's beloved son while still serving as his scribe and minister.

The texts credit him with writing each person's fate on the sacred tablets, as the gods decide it. From this comes his great power: he was believed able to lengthen or shorten human life. He is shown wearing a horned cap, standing with his hands clasped in the ancient priestly gesture, and riding the winged dragon mušhuššu, which originally belonged to Marduk.

His origins seem to lie in the West: he may have been a West Semitic deity brought to Mesopotamia by the Amorites, probably together with Marduk. Even so, he took over the patronage of scribes once held by the goddess Nisaba.

Worship and religion

Nabu's temple was E-zida in Borsippa. At the Babylonian New Year festival his statue was carried from Borsippa to Babylon to pay respects to his father Marduk. Hammurabi, in the 18th century BC, built a throne for his cult. His symbols were the stylus resting on a tablet and the simple wedge, and fine clay tablets were offered at his temple. His worship continued until the 2nd century, when cuneiform fell out of use.

Legacy

In late Babylonian astrology, Nabu was associated with the planet Mercury. The Greeks equated him with Apollo, as a giver of prophecies, and with Hermes, and the Romans identified him with Mercury. In Mandaean cosmology, the name of the planet Mercury, Nbu, derives from his. The Bible mentions him as Nebo in Isaiah 46:1 and Jeremiah 48:1.

Ancient sources: He appears in Babylonian and Assyrian cuneiform texts, and in the biblical tradition under the name Nebo.

In art

Trivia

A statue in London. The British Museum holds a statue of Nabu that was originally erected in Nimrud, the Calah of the biblical sources, during the reign of Tiglath-Pileser III.
