Aztec goddess of death and obsidian butterflies

Itzpapalotl

Itzpapalotl, the "Obsidian Butterfly" or "Clawed Butterfly", is a goddess of Aztec religion linked to death, warfare, sacrifice and magic. She matters because she combines the traits of a skeletal warrior, a mother of women who died in childbirth and the ruler of the paradise of Tamoanchan.

The myth

According to tradition, Itzpapalotl was sent to earth wearing a cape that made her invisible. She appears with a skeletal face and butterfly wings tipped with obsidian blades, and in some images her hands and feet are jaguar claws.

The Annals of Cuauhtitlan tell that, during the migration of the Chichimecs, the goddess captured four hundred mixcoas and devoured them. She then ordered the Chichimecs to shoot at several red animals, but Iztac Mixcoatl hid inside a barrel cactus. Itzpapalotl charged at the plant, yet Mixcoatl shot her with arrows and called up the dead mixcoas, who struck her down. After her death she was burned, and her ashes were used by the warriors to paint their faces. She was then turned into one of the stars that accompany the sun.

Worship and religion

Itzpapalotl was patron of a day in the Aztec ritual calendar, associated with the cihuateteo, women who died in childbirth. She is linked to a Chichimec creation ritual in which warriors emerged from a seven-chambered cave, Chicomoztoc, and caves carved to imitate it have been found in the Barranca del Aguila region since the 1990s.

Legacy

A feature on Venus, the Itzpapalotl Tessera, bears her name. She appears in Laurell K. Hamilton's novel Obsidian Butterfly, in the Netflix film No One Gets Out Alive, and in the game Genshin Impact.

Ancient sources: She is mentioned in the Annals of Cuauhtitlan and in the Manuscript of 1558, written in Latin letters but of pre-Hispanic origin.

Trivia

Clawed butterfly. Her name can mean "obsidian butterfly" or "clawed butterfly", and the second meaning seems more likely.
Venus. A surface feature on Venus is named after her.
