Roman goddess of the new year and wellbeing

Strenua

Strenua, also called Strenia, was a goddess of Roman religion associated with the new year, purification and wellbeing. According to Varro she was of Sabine origin, and her name gave rise to the word strenae, the new-year gifts Romans exchanged as good omens.

The myth

Tradition linked the goddess to a shrine (sacellum) and a sacred grove (lucus) at the top of the Via Sacra. According to the story, the Sabine king Tatius was the first to treat the sacred branches (verbenae) of a fertile tree in Strenia's grove as auspicious signs of the new year, and the custom of exchanging gifts supposedly grew from this.

The New Year's Day rite included a procession that carried twigs from the grove to the citadel (arx). The earliest mention dates it to 153 BC, when consuls began taking office at the start of the year, although it is unclear whether the celebration had always fallen on that date.

Worship and religion

Strenua appeared in the lists of deities (indigitamenta) kept by priests to ensure the correct divinity was invoked in public rituals. The procession of the Argei began at her shrine. During the Principate, the strenae often took the form of money.

Legacy

The word strenae gave rise to French étrennes and Italian strenne, meaning a new-year gift. Some scholars have linked her cult to the Italian Befana tradition.

Ancient sources: Varro calls her a Sabine goddess, and St. Augustine connects her name with making a person strenuus, vigorous. Johannes Lydus records the Sabine word strenae as meaning wellbeing.

Trivia

Gifts of money. During the Principate, the New Year's gifts called strenae often took the form of money.
A link to the Befana. In 1823 Rev. John J. Blunt argued that the Italian Befana was heir to the goddess Strenia, since both gave similar gifts: figs, dates and honey.
