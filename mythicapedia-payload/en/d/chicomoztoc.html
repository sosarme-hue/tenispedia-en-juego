mythical origin place of the Nahua peoples

Chicomoztoc

Chicomoztoc is the mythical place of origin of the Nahuatl-speaking peoples of central Mexico, including the Mexica, according to the tradition of the so-called Seven Nahuatlaca tribes. Its name, meaning “place of seven caves”, makes it a key point in the migration stories of these peoples.

The myth

According to the Codex Boturini, after leaving Aztlan the migration reached Chicomoztoc, where the groups settled for a time. From there, the codices list seven successive migrations: Xochimilca, Chalca, Tepaneca, Acolhua, Tlahuica, Tlaxcalteca and Mexica. Other codices add further peoples who also left the place, such as the Cuitlahuica, Huexotzinca and Matlatzinca.

The relationship between Aztlan and Chicomoztoc is debated. Tezozomoc does not distinguish between Aztlan, Chicomoztoc and Culhuacan; Diego Durán holds that they are the same place; Sahagún does not mention Aztlan, while Boturini sees Aztlan as the origin and Chicomoztoc as a single stage of the journey. In the Historia Tolteca-Chichimeca and the Mapa de Cuauhtinchan No. 2, seven tribes live in Chicomoztoc, fast and draw blood for four days, and after a New Fire ceremony settle in the Puebla valley.

The name is linked to the birth or beginning of a people. The caves have been compared to the wombs from which the groups were born, or to the seven orifices of the human body.

Legacy

On Cerro Culiacán in Guanajuato, some historians have identified the Chicomoztoc of the colonial chronicles, with seven main caves among more than two hundred. The place also appears in Clive Cussler's novel Lost Empire, where it is set on an island in South Sulawesi, Indonesia, and becomes the origin of the Aztec ancestors. Its caves also feature in New Age Mayanism.

Ancient sources: Codex Boturini, the Historia Tolteca-Chichimeca, the Mapa de Cuauhtinchan No. 2, and the chronicles of Tezozomoc, Diego Durán and Sahagún.

Trivia

A name with three roots. Chicomoztoc comes from the Nahuatl chicome (“seven”), oztotl (“cave”) and the suffix -c (“place”).
Caves as wombs. It has been proposed that the caves symbolize the wombs from which the peoples were born, or perhaps the seven orifices of the body.
More than two hundred caves. Cerro Culiacán has more than 200 caves, and its seven main ones show evidence of human use.
