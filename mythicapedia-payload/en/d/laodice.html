Trojan princess, daughter of Priam

Laodice

Laodice is a Trojan princess, daughter of Priam and Hecuba, known for her beauty and for her dramatic fate during the fall of Troy. She appears in the Iliad and other Greek sources, which do not fully agree on her husbands or her end.

The myth

According to the tradition, Laodice was the most beautiful of Priam's daughters. Before the Trojan War, Acamas, son of Theseus, came to Troy as an envoy of the Achaeans to recover Helen through diplomacy, and Laodice fell in love with him. Their son Munitus was born, and in one account he was entrusted to Aethra, Acamas' grandmother, who was then a slave of Helen. After the war, Acamas took the boy with him. Years later, while hunting with his father in Thrace, Munitus was bitten by a snake and died.

The sources disagree about her marriage. The Iliad presents her as the wife of Helicaon, son of Antenor, while Hyginus' Fabulae make her the wife of Telephus, king of Mysia, who left the city to be healed of a wound and, once cured, refused to attack Troy out of respect for her. Another account gives her a son named Munychus, born of a union with Demophoon, Acamas' brother.

On the night Troy fell, fearing she would become a captive, Laodice prayed to the gods to be swallowed by the earth, and a chasm opened beneath her. Pausanias, however, lists her among the captive Trojans painted in the Lesche at Delphi, and he assumes she was later freed, since no poet mentions her as a captive and the Greeks would not have harmed her, being married to a son of Antenor, a guest-friend of Menelaus and Odysseus.

Ancient sources: She appears in Homer's Iliad, Hyginus' Fabulae, the Bibliotheca attributed to Apollodorus, and Pausanias' Description of Greece.

Trivia

A woman with two fates. According to Pausanias, Laodice was not swallowed by the earth; instead she is depicted among the captive Trojan women in the Lesche at Delphi.
A death by serpent. Her son Munitus was said to have died after being bitten by a snake while hunting with his father in Thrace.
