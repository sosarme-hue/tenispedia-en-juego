medieval land of plenty

Cockaigne

Cockaigne is an imaginary land of medieval myth where no one has to work and food, comfort and pleasure are available without limit. It was a popular dream among peasants and Goliard poets, and also a parody of the asceticism of the age.

The myth

In medieval tales, Cockaigne is a place where rivers run with wine and milk, the mountains are made of cheese, and roasted piglets wander about with knives stuck in their backs, ready to be eaten. Nobody has to work, society's rules are turned upside down, and wishes are granted instantly.

In the 13th-century French poem The Land of Cockaigne, the houses are built of barley sugar and cakes, and the shops hand out their goods for free. It is a land of contraries where abbots are beaten by their monks and nuns show their bottoms, and in some versions it rains cheese from the sky.

Over time the name was also applied to Jauja, a rich Andean region, and from there to a popular Spanish ballad printed in 1660, The Island of Jauja.

Legacy

Pieter Bruegel the Elder depicted Cockaigne in his painting Luilekkerland (1567). The name gave rise to the Spanish cucaña pole game, in which a greased pole must be climbed to reach a prize. Charles Baudelaire mentioned it in his prose poem L'invitation au voyage, and Edward Elgar used it for his overture Cockaigne (In London Town) in 1901. Carl Orff included the song Ego sum abbas Cucaniensis in Carmina Burana.

Ancient sources: A 13th-century French poem, The Land of Cockaigne, and medieval Goliard verse, in Latin and vernacular languages.

Trivia

A game named after it. The cucaña pole game, in which players climb a greased pole, takes its name from this imaginary land.
A culinary origin. The term is thought to come from the Latin coquina (kitchen) or from a word for small sweet cakes sold at fairs.
