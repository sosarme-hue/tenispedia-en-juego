ten-headed demon king of Lanka in the Ramayana

Ravana

Ravana is the main antagonist of the Hindu epic Ramayana and the rakshasa king of the island fortress of Lanka. Usually depicted with ten heads, he is at once a learned scholar and a devotee of Shiva, which makes him a complex and widely recognised figure in Hindu tradition.

The myth

According to the Ramayana, Ravana was the son of the sage Vishrava and the rakshasa princess Kaikasi, and his half-brother was Kubera, the treasurer of the gods. Born of a Brahmin father and a demonic mother, he was half Brahmin and half asura. As a young man he mastered the Vedas and performed such a severe penance that, to win Shiva's attention, he cut off one head after another until he was about to lose the last. Shiva appeared and granted him power and knowledge of weapons.

He then obtained from Brahma invulnerability against gods, celestial beings, demons and beasts, but not against humans, whom he despised. He drove Kubera out of Lanka and ruled the island with a reputation for prosperity. Later, according to some accounts, he tried to lift Mount Kailash, and Shiva pressed the mountain down on him until, after years of singing, he set him free; Ravana then composed the Shiva Tandava Stotra in his honour.

The central episode is the abduction of Sita, Rama's wife, whom he held in the Ashoka grove. Rama, aided by the vanara king Sugriva and his army, marched on Lanka, and after a long war killed Ravana and rescued Sita. Tradition holds that Vishnu had arranged for Rama to be born as a man precisely to carry out this vengeance, since Ravana and his brother Kumbhakarna were former doorkeepers of Vaikuntha cursed to be reborn as enemies of the god.

Worship and religion

Ravana is revered as Shiva's most distinguished devotee, and images of him often appear beside those of the god in temples. In Hindu tradition he is also honoured as a scholar of the Vedas and the sciences. In India and Sri Lanka, the festival of Dussehra each year features the burning of a large effigy of him, symbolising the victory of good over evil.

Legacy

He appears in traditional Rajasthani painting and in many versions of the Ramayana. Treatises on astrology and pharmacy are attributed to him, though the attributions are traditional. The name Dashamukha, 'ten-faced', made him one of the most recognisable images in Indian mythology.

Ancient sources: He appears in the epics Ramayana and Mahabharata, and in Buddhist, Jain and Vaishnava texts such as the Bhagavata Purana.

Trivia

Dussehra. Each year, during the Hindu festival of Dussehra, a large effigy of the ten-headed Ravana is burned to mark his defeat.
Ten heads. According to the text, the ten heads may symbolise great knowledge spreading across the ten directions.
