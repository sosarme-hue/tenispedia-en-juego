Egyptian god of paths, war and the dead

Wepwawet

Wepwawet, also known as Upuaut, is an Egyptian god shown as a jackal or wolf, linked to war, kingship and the afterlife. His name means "opener of the ways", and he matters because he led the dead through the Duat and went ahead of armies and the pharaoh.

The myth

According to Egyptian tradition, Wepwawet came originally from Abydos in Upper Egypt, although his main cult developed in Asyut. The Pyramid Texts say he was born in the Peru-nu, the shrine of the goddess Wadjet, while an alternative version has him emerge from a tamarisk bush. For this reason he was often confused with Anubis, and eventually came to be considered his brother.

Over time his role as a warrior merged with that of a guide of the dead. He accompanied Osiris on journeys to distant lands and opened the path for spirits across the western desert to the Duat. He stood at the prow of Ra's solar barque and, according to some sources, also symbolised the winter solstice. At Abydos he was regarded as a son of Isis and Osiris, a clear case of syncretism with Horus.

He was shown as a black jackal, sometimes with a white head, or as a man with a jackal's head. He carried a mace and a bow, and was occasionally dressed as a soldier. The Greeks identified him with a wolf, which gave the name Lycopolis to his city of cult.

Worship and religion

His cult began in Asyut, which the Greeks and Romans called Lycopolis, and was also practised at Abydos as "Lord of the West". He was venerated at Sais, Heliopolis, Memphis and several necropolises. He led military, religious and civil processions and went before major celebrations such as the Heb Sed and the Mysteries of Osiris at Abydos. In funerary belief he assisted at the Opening of the Mouth ceremony and guided the deceased into the netherworld.

Legacy

His name was given to the remote-controlled robot that first explored the ventilation shafts of the Queen's Chamber in the Great Pyramid of Khufu, a project shown in a well-known National Geographic documentary. In popular culture he is the supreme deity of the Kordofan faith in the video game Crusader Kings III, and the personal totem of Thu in Pauline Gedge's Lady of the Reeds novels.

Ancient sources: The Pyramid Texts mention his birth in the Peru-nu and describe him as Ra who has gone up from the horizon; he also appears in reliefs in the temple of Seti I at Abydos.
