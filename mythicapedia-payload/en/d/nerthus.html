Germanic goddess of fertility and cultivated earth

Nerthus

Nerthus is a goddess of the Germanic peoples recorded by Tacitus, associated with the fertility of cultivated land and with a ritual wagon procession. Her name is the linguistic precursor of Njörðr, a Vanir god of Norse mythology, which links her to the Scandinavian tradition.

The myth

According to Tacitus in the Germania, several Germanic tribes worshipped Nerthus. Her wagon, covered in white cloth, was kept in a sacred grove on an island in the ocean, and only a priest could touch it. When he sensed the goddess was present, heifers drew the cart, and her progress was marked by celebration: no wars were fought and iron objects were locked away.

When the goddess had had her fill of human company, the priest returned the wagon to its sanctuary. Slaves then ritually washed the goddess, the wagon and the cloth in a secluded lake, and, according to Tacitus, the slaves were drowned there immediately. Norse mythology preserves no equivalent story. Njörðr took on many of her traits, and Jörd took over the role of mother earth. Some scholars propose that Nerthus was the sister-wife of Njörðr and mother of Freyr and Freyja, though this remains a hypothesis.

Worship and religion

Her cult was popular among some Germanic tribes, especially in what would become Germany, and the procession took place in spring. Some scholars suggest it may have originated in the Stone Age, though this is conjecture without direct evidence. There is no record that it survived the spread of Christianity.

Legacy

She had a place in German popular culture, mainly through the mistaken reading Hertha, which some scholars linked to the word for earth. Her wagon has been compared with those found at Dejbjerg in Denmark and Oseberg in Norway, and her name is studied as the root of Njörðr in Norse philology.

Ancient sources: Tacitus's Germania, written in the first century AD, is the main ancient source mentioning her, and medieval manuscripts of the work preserve her name in varying forms.

Trivia

A name with variants. Medieval manuscripts of the Germania record several forms of the name, such as Nertum or Herthum. The reading Hertha was popular until the Codex Aesinas, discovered in 1902, confirmed Nerthus.
A chariot pulled by cats. In Norse tradition, Freyja's chariot was pulled by cats, a detail some scholars compare with Nerthus's cow-drawn wagon.
