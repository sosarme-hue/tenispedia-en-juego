Chinese celestial god and cosmic force

Tian

Tian (天) is the Chinese character for heaven, carrying two meanings: physical sky and religious force. It was a central concept in Chinese thought, present in Mohism, Taoism and Confucianism.

The myth

According to Chinese tradition, Tian appears as the supreme deity of the Shang, called Shangdi or simply Di. When the Zhou overthrew them, their own Tian merged with that god and gained the power to alter the Mandate of Heaven, the principle that justified a ruler's legitimacy. A virtuous sovereign received that mandate; a corrupt ruler could lose it.

Modern historians believe the Zhou Tian was originally their own, although the Zhou claimed their deity came from the Xia dynasty. Over time the emperor became known as the 'Son of Heaven', and Tian came to be both an impersonal force and a personal god, which makes it hard to tell which one is meant.

Worship and religion

Under the Zhou dynasty, nobles made the worship of heaven central to their political philosophy. Emperors offered sacrifices to Tian and Earth, and Mount Tai was the most important site for these rites. Before the 20th century, its worship was considered an orthodox cosmic principle in China.

Legacy

Tian remains part of the Chinese language in expressions such as 'all under heaven' (tianxia), used to mean the world or the empire. It also appears in the title of the 'Heavenly Questions' (Tianwen), a section of the Chu Ci. In Confucian thought, reverence for heaven is linked to harmony between humanity and nature.

Ancient sources: The Analects of Confucius record his words about Heaven, and the Chu Ci includes the Heavenly Questions, where the term appears.

Trivia

A character with two meanings. The same character 天 denotes both the physical sky and a religious force. That is why the term can refer to a personal being or to an impersonal principle.
Variant spellings. One variant written with the characters for 'two' and 'person' literally means 'two humans'. Another, combining 'blue' and 'vital energy', is used for the blue sky.
