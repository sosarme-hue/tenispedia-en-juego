second legendary king of Athens

Cranaus

Cranaus was, according to Athenian tradition, the second legendary king of Athens, succeeding Cecrops. He is counted among the autochthons, born from the soil of Attica, and his name became tied to the identity of the region's people.

The myth

According to the myth, Cranaus succeeded Cecrops on the throne of Athens and ruled for nine or ten years. He married Pedias, a Spartan woman and daughter of Mynes, with whom he had three daughters: Cranaë, Cranaechme and Atthis.

Tradition holds that Atthis died young, and in her memory Cranaus gave her name to the region, which had previously been called Actaea after its first king, Acteus. This is how Attica got its name. Other accounts say that Atthis, by Hephaestus, was the mother of Erichthonius; the scholar Tzetzes instead makes Erichthonius the son of Hephaestus and Athena, daughter of Cranaus. Some sources also make Rarus a son of Cranaus, without naming his wife.

His reign coincides with the flood of Deucalion. Deucalion fled to Attica from the waters with his sons Hellen and Amphictyon. Amphictyon, who had married one of Cranaus's daughters, rebelled against his father-in-law and seized power. Cranaus fled to the deme of Lamptrae, where he died and was buried; Pausanias says his tomb was still there in his day.

Worship and religion

Cranaus was venerated as a hero in Athens, and his priests came from the family of the Charidae. The people of Attica were called Kranaoi, or 'descendants of Cranaus', and Athens was also known as Kranaa or Kranaai.

Ancient sources: His story appears in classical sources that recount Athens's early kings, such as Pausanias, who describes his tomb at Lamptrae.

Trivia

Origin of the name Attica. According to tradition, the region takes its name from Atthis, the daughter of Cranaus, who gave her name to Attica after dying young.
A tomb Pausanias saw. The geographer Pausanias states that Cranaus's tomb was still standing at Lamptrae in his time.
