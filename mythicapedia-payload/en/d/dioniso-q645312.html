Greek god of wine, festivity and ecstasy, identified with Bacchus by the Romans

Dionysus

Dionysus is the Greek god of wine, vegetation, fertility, festivity and religious ecstasy. The Romans identified him with Bacchus and with their own Liber Pater. He matters because he joined wine to religious experience and gave birth to Western theatre.

The myth

According to tradition, Dionysus was the son of Zeus and the mortal Semele. Orphic texts also present him as a son of Zeus and Persephone, or as an underworld aspect of Zeus. Accounts place his birth in Thrace, describe him travelling abroad, and have him arrive in Greece as a foreigner who must struggle for acceptance.

His chief attribute is the thyrsus, a fennel staff sometimes wound with ivy and dripping with honey, which serves as a beneficent wand and as a weapon against those who oppose his cult. Wine is his earthly incarnation: it eases suffering, brings joy and inspires divine madness. His followers, the maenads, perform ecstatic dances. He is sometimes described as a dying-and-rising god, which has invited comparisons with Jesus, though scholars stress important differences.

Worship and religion

His worship took many forms. His festivals included sacred dramas based on his myths, which laid the foundations of Western theatre. The maenads offered blood sacrifices to feed the dead, since the god acted as a go-between for the living and the dead. In Rome, independent celebrations called Bacchanalia were seen as subversive, and the state banned them, allowing only tightly supervised, reduced forms.

Legacy

The word bacchanal comes from the Roman Bacchanalia, and the name Bacchus entered language and art. His link to theatre makes him one of the roots of European drama.

Ancient sources: Nonnus of Panopolis, in his Dionysiaca, and the Byzantine encyclopedia Suda, based on classical sources, record his names and etymologies; Mycenaean tablets from Pylos attest his name already around the thirteenth century BC.

Trivia

A honey-dripping staff. The thyrsus, his attribute, was a fennel staff that could be wound with ivy and drip with honey.
A Mycenaean name. His name appears in Linear B tablets found at Pylos, suggesting his worship is very ancient in Greece.
