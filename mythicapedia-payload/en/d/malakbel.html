sun god of Palmyra

Malakbel

Malakbel was the sun god of Palmyra, a caravan city in pre-Islamic Syria. He was worshipped alongside the moon god Aglibol, and his name means ‘Messenger of Baal’, presenting him as servant of the chief deity.

The myth

Most of what is known about Malakbel comes from inscriptions and artistic representations, so his myth has to be reconstructed with caution. His name casts him as the messenger of Baal, and in Palmyra he appears as the companion of the moon god Aglibol. In Babylon he was equated with the sun god Shamash; the Greeks identified him with Hermes and the Romans with Sol.

He was often grouped with the supreme god Baalshamin in a local triad that celebrated the eternity of the heavens and the benefits of heat combined with moisture. At other times he appeared beside the goddess Al-lat. A marble altar from Palmyra shows the four seasons, which symbolised the sun’s yearly cycle, and in the triad all three gods wear Roman armour.

Worship and religion

His main place of worship was the sanctuary known as the Holy Garden, tended by the Bene Komare tribe. It had two altars, a sacred cypress and a bath, whose construction received a donation of 2,500 denarii in 182 AD. His cult also reached Rome, where by the early 2nd century there was a shrine on the right bank of the Tiber, near wine warehouses, where he bore the epithet Invictus.

Legacy

In 274 AD the emperor Aurelian dedicated a large temple to Sol Invictus in Rome. Some scholars believe that cult had Syrian origins and may derive from Malakbel, but the connection cannot be confirmed and will probably remain unresolved.

Ancient sources: Palmyrene and Roman inscriptions from the 1st to 3rd centuries AD, such as the bilingual one of 122 AD and the one from El Kantara around 178 AD; the sources do not cite ancient literary texts that mention him.

Trivia
