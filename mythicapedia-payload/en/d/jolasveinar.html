Icelandic Christmas prankster figures

Yule Lads

The Yule Lads are thirteen mischievous pranksters, sons of the ogress Grýla and Leppalúði, who come down from the mountains of Icelandic folklore in the days before Christmas. Rooted in Nordic tradition, their story shifted over the centuries from frightening children to leaving them small gifts.

The myth

According to Icelandic tradition, Grýla is a mountain ogress who, at Christmas, goes out looking for disobedient children to cook in a large pot. Her husband, Leppalúði, is lazy and rarely leaves the cave they share in the lava fields of Dimmuborgir. They live there with the Yule Cat (Jólakötturinn), a huge, vicious cat that devours anyone who has not received new clothes before Christmas Eve, and with their sons.

The Yule Lads arrive in town one by one in the days leading up to Christmas. Each has a name that describes his favourite kind of mischief, such as stealing food or making noise. Well-behaved children find a small gift in the shoe they have left on the windowsill; naughty ones find a rotten potato. Versions vary by region: some speak of nine Lads, others make them Grýla's brothers, and an eastern Icelandic tale has them coming from the sea.

Legacy

In modern times they have become kinder figures, often compared to Santa Claus. They are usually shown in late-medieval Icelandic clothing, though at children's events they sometimes appear in the traditional Santa Claus costume. Jóhannes úr Kötlum's 1932 poem fixed their thirteen canonical names.

Ancient sources: The Yule Lads first appear in the seventeenth-century Poem of Grýla; the ogress Grýla is already named in medieval texts such as Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda, though not yet linked to Christmas.

Trivia

A king who objected. The King of Denmark objected to using the Yule Lads as a disciplinary tool, and in 1746 their myths were officially banned from instilling excessive fear in children.
Thirteen canonical names. The poem Jólin koma, published in 1932 by Jóhannes úr Kötlum, established the thirteen names and personalities now considered canonical.
