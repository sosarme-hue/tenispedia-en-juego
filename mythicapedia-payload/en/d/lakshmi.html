Hindu goddess of fortune, wealth and beauty

Lakshmi

Lakshmi is one of the principal goddesses of Hinduism, associated with fortune, wealth, prosperity, beauty and abundance. She is the consort of Vishnu and, together with Parvati and Sarasvati, forms the triad of goddesses known as the Tridevi.

The myth

According to tradition, Lakshmi was born from the sea foam during the churning of the ocean of milk. The Ramayana tells this story for the first time, and it resembles the birth of Venus in Roman mythology. The Devi Bhagavata offers another version of the same episode, while the Vishnu Purana makes her the daughter of the sage Bhrigu and his wife Khyati.

In the Vedic texts her name had a different meaning. In the Rig Veda, lakshmi refers to a mark or sign of good omen rather than to a goddess. Later, the Shri Sukta hymn presents the goddess Sri as personified fortune. The Shatapatha Brahmana describes her as the daughter of Prajapati and the mother of Kama, the god of love. Over the centuries Lakshmi grew in importance until she became the consort of Vishnu. According to one legend, the gods exiled her, and she took refuge in the ocean of milk, where she was reborn during the churning. Her elder sister, Alakshmi, is the goddess of misfortune.

Lakshmi accompanies Vishnu whenever he descends to earth as an avatar. She was with Sita, wife of Rama, and with Radha, the herdswoman beloved of Krishna. She is usually shown seated or standing on a lotus, with four arms, letting gold coins fall from one hand, and flanked by two white elephants, symbols of good fortune.

Worship and religion

Lakshmi is worshipped throughout Hinduism, especially in the Vaishnava tradition, where she is seen as the divine energy of Vishnu, and in Shaktism, which regards her as the prosperity aspect of the supreme goddess. She is also honoured as the Ashta Lakshmi, a group of eight manifestations representing eight sources of wealth. Temples such as the one at Orchha, in north central India, are among the places of worship. Lakshmi Puja is held during Navaratri, and she is honoured at Deepavali and at Sharad Purnima, also called Kojagiri Purnima. Her worship remains alive today in India and among Hindu communities abroad.

Legacy

Lakshmi has appeared in Indian art for centuries. Gupta period sculpture and coins often show her flanked by lions. Archaeological finds and ancient coinage suggest she was revered as early as the first millennium BCE. Images of her have also been found in Hindu temples across Southeast Asia, dating from the second half of the first millennium CE. As a symbol, the Lakshmi star has eight points formed by two squares overlapping at 45 degrees, and it has been compared to the Tartessian star.

Ancient sources: Rig Veda, Atharva Veda, Shatapatha Brahmana, Ramayana, Devi Bhagavata and Vishnu Purana.

Trivia

A sign, not a goddess. In the Rig Veda, the word lakshmi refers to a mark of gratitude or good omen. The divine figure appears later, in subsequent texts.
Lions in the Gupta period. Gupta period sculpture and coins often show her with lions, a different iconography from the more common elephants.
