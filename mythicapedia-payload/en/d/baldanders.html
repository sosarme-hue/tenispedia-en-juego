protean creature of Germanic literary myth

Baldanders

Baldanders, whose name means "soon-different" or "soon-changed", is a creature of Germanic literary myth with protean properties. The figure grew out of a poem by Hans Sachs and reached its best-known form in Grimmelshausen's novel Simplicissimus.

The myth

In the novel, the protagonist comes across a stone statue of an ancient Germanic hero dressed as a Roman soldier. Its occupant, Baldanders, claims to have met Sachs in 1534 and offers to teach him the art of talking with inanimate objects. After a riddle made of invented words, the being changes into an oak, a sow, a sausage, a meadow of clover, a flower, a mulberry tree and a silk carpet. The cycle of the oak, whose acorns feed the sow, which becomes sausage and then human food, symbolises the constant change of nature and society.

Legacy

Jorge Luis Borges controversially proposed that the creature on the novel's frontispiece is a composite with a satyr's head, wings and a fish's tail. Baldanders also appears in the webcomic What Happened When by Andrew Scott and Carlos Morote, and lends its name to a giant in Gene Wolfe's The Book of the New Sun.

Ancient sources: It appears in the Continuatio (1669) of Grimmelshausen's Simplicissimus, which draws on a poem by Hans Sachs dated 1534.

Trivia

A giant in Wolfe. In Gene Wolfe's saga, the name Baldanders belongs to an enormous, intellectually gifted giant whose physical growth never stops.
A contested identification. Borges suggested that the creature on the frontispiece is a satyr-headed, winged, fish-tailed composite, a reading that is not universally accepted.
