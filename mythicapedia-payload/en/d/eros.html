Greek god of love and desire

Eros

Eros is the Greek god of love and desire, usually shown as a winged young man armed with a bow and arrows. The Romans called him Cupid. His image shifted from a primordial force of the cosmos to the son of Aphrodite, and that later version became one of the most recognisable figures of classical mythology.

The myth

According to Hesiod's Theogony, Eros was one of the first beings to arise, after Chaos, Gaia and Tartarus, and he is described as the most beautiful of the immortal gods, able to loosen the limbs and capture the hearts of gods and men. Other ancient authors gave him a different origin. Aristophanes, in The Birds, has him hatched from an egg laid by Night; in that version Eros joins with Chaos and fathers the race of birds. Parmenides places him first of all the gods.

In later stories Eros is the son of Aphrodite, and sometimes of Ares, appearing as a mischievous boy who shoots arrows to kindle love between gods and mortals. Texts such as the Argonautica show this, where Hera asks Aphrodite to make her son cause Medea to fall in love with Jason, and Ovidio's Metamorphoses tells how Venus is grazed by her son's arrow and falls for a mortal man.

His most important story of his own is that of Eros and Psyche, known mainly from Apuleius's Latin novel The Golden Ass. Aphrodite, jealous of the beauty of the mortal princess Psyche, orders her son to make her fall in love with the ugliest creature on earth. Instead Eros falls in love with her and takes her to his palace. The envy of Psyche's sisters leads her to break her husband's trust, and Eros leaves her. Psyche wanders the world searching for him and, after completing the tasks Aphrodite sets her with supernatural help, is made immortal and lives among the gods with her husband.

Worship and religion

The worship of Eros was rare in ancient Greece, far less important than that of Aphrodite. In Thespiae he was venerated in the form of a rough stone in a fertility cult, and the Erotidia, festivals in his honour, were celebrated there. In Athens he shared a very popular cult with Aphrodite, and the fourth day of every month was sacred to him. His statues stood in the palaestras, the gathering places where men met their beloved, and the Spartans offered him sacrifices before battle. He was also worshipped in mystery cults such as the Orphic rites and the Eleusinian mysteries, where he was called Protogonos, the firstborn.

Legacy

Eros gave his name to the word eros, meaning desire and sexual attraction, from which terms such as erotic are derived. The Romans identified him with Cupid, and his figure as a winged child became the putti of medieval and Renaissance Christian art, which in turn influenced the iconography of cherubs. That image of Cupid became a major icon of Valentine's Day.

Ancient sources: Hesiod in the Theogony; Aristophanes in The Birds; Plato in the Symposium; and Apuleius in The Golden Ass.

Trivia

Keeper of keys. He received the epithet Kleidouchos, 'key-bearer', because he was thought to hold the key to hearts.
The liberator. He was called Eleutherios, 'the liberator', because love was understood as a force that sets people free.
