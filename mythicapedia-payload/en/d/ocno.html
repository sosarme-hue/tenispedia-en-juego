figure from Greek myth punished in the Underworld

Ocnus

Ocnus (or Ocno) is a figure from Greek myth who dwells in the Underworld and embodies hesitation and fruitless toil. His name is linked to the Greek word oknos, meaning reluctance or hesitation, and he became a proverbial image of endless, wasted labour.

The myth

According to myth, Ocnus spends eternity in the Underworld twisting a rope of rushes while a donkey eats it as fast as he makes it. Unlike other condemned figures, the ancient sources never name the offence that earned him this punishment, which is why his story has been read as a symbol of unending effort.

Genealogies vary. He is usually called a son of King Tiberinus Silvius of Alba Longa, or of the river god Tiber, and of the seer Manto, daughter of Tiresias. Virgil and later writers credit him with founding Mantua, named after his mother, as well as Bologna, Cesena and Perugia. In Spain, several authors credit him with founding Madrid, though his link with the Underworld figure is unclear.

Pausanias describes him in a painting by Polygnotus and notes that Ionians spoke of “weaving the rope of Ocnus” for pointless work, linking the phrase to a hardworking man whose wife squandered everything he earned. Diodorus Siculus and Propertius also mention him. An alternative version shows him loading logs onto the donkey, which keep falling off.

Worship and religion

Diodorus Siculus records a festival near Memphis, at Acanthompolis, that re-enacted the myth: one man twisted a long rope while others unravelled it behind him. Priests carried water every day to a perforated jar. The sources record no living cult of Ocnus.

Legacy

The phrase “weaving Ocnus’s rope” survived as a proverb for pointless labour. Goethe retold the story in his Faust. Luis Cernuda titled his prose poem Ocnos (1942), written about Seville during his exile in Scotland and expanded in two later editions. In 1988, José Luis Turina presented a musical setting of some of its poems at the Teatro Real in Madrid. Philosophers have read the myth differently: Ulrich von Wilamowitz-Moellendorff saw it as punishment for moral weakness, while Julius Evola took it as an image of the cycle of human birth and death.

Ancient sources: Pausanias, Diodorus Siculus, Propertius and Virgil mention him in their works, and Virgil is the first to credit him with founding cities.

Trivia

A bird called oknos. According to Pausanias, diviners who watched birds called the heron oknos, the largest and most beautiful of its kind.
Mantua, in honour of his mother. Tradition credits Ocnus with founding modern Mantua in honour of his mother, Manto.
Goethe at the door. Goethe’s account of the myth serves as the introduction to both Cernuda’s work and Turina’s.
