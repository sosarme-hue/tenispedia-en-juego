lost land of Arthurian legend

Lyonesse

Lyonesse, known in Spanish as Lionís, is a prosperous land in Arthurian legend that stretched between Cornwall and the Isles of Scilly and that, according to the tale, was swallowed by the sea. It matters because it was the homeland of the knight Tristan and because its sinking became one of the best-known stories of a drowned land in Celtic tradition.

The myth

According to medieval accounts, Lyonesse was a kingdom with a mild climate, fields that yielded several harvests a year, splendid castles and one hundred and forty parish churches. Its men were strong and its women beautiful and noble. It was the homeland of Tristan, son of King Meliodas. After his father's death, Tristan was to inherit the throne, but the land sank while he was at the court of his uncle, King Mark of Cornwall.

Accounts of the catastrophe differ. One tradition blames a terrible crime, punished by God with a single night's storm and a giant wave. Another remembers a lone survivor who escaped on horseback to higher ground. The flood is dated to around 1099, or ten years earlier in other versions. Breton and Welsh legends tell similar stories, such as the city of Ys and the kingdom of Cantre'r Gwaelod.

Legacy

The lost land has inspired many works. The poet Algernon Charles Swinburne wrote the epic poem Tristram of Lyonesse (1882), and Thomas Hardy titled a poem about a journey to Cornwall When I Set out for Lyonnesse (1914). Alfred Tennyson places the final battle between Arthur and Mordred in Lyonesse in his Idylls of the King, and local fishermen still say that on calm days one can hear church bells beneath the sea.

Ancient sources: Medieval versions of the Arthurian matter, especially the French Prose Tristan, where the name Léonois appears, from which the English Lyonesse derives.

Trivia

A Cornish name. In Cornish, the lost land was called Lethowsow, meaning 'the milky ones'. The name refers to the constant white water surrounding the Seven Stones reef, which lies on the supposed site of its capital.
Horseshoes on the coat of arms. Some Cornish families, such as the Vyvyans, use three horseshoes on their coat of arms. They claim descent from Trevelyan, the survivor who escaped on horseback.
