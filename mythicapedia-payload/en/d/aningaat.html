brother of the Moon in Inuit mythology

Aningaat

Aningaat is a figure in Inuit folklore who, together with his sister, becomes the Moon and the Sun. The story explains why the two chase each other across the sky and why the Moon looks grey and smudged.

The myth

Aningaat and his sister are orphans who live with their grandmother, and he is blind. After a polar bear that the grandmother hides from the boy, a loon takes him under the water and restores his sight by licking his eyes. While whale hunting, the boy harpoons his grandmother, who sinks; in some versions she becomes a narwhal. Later, in the darkness of a festival, the sister is assaulted; she smears soot on her face to recognise the attacker, and Aningaat is marked with it too. The people's laughter humiliates her; she cuts off a breast and offers it to her brother. They flee with torches into the sky, where they become the Sun and the Moon: her torch keeps burning and his goes out.

Legacy

In 1908 Frank Wilbert Stokes painted the story on a mural in the Eskimo Hall of the American Museum of Natural History in New York. The artist Victoria Mamnguqsualuk has reimagined it in prints as a feminist tale.

Trivia

A 1908 mural. The painter Frank Wilbert Stokes depicted the myth in a mural in the Eskimo Hall of the American Museum of Natural History in New York.
The sister's names. In an account recorded by Hans Egede in Greenland, the sister is called Malina or Ajut.
