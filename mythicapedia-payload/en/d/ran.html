Norse goddess of the sea and the drowned

Rán

Rán is the Norse goddess of the sea and ruler of those who drown. In the tradition, she catches seafarers in her net and carries them to her home at the bottom of the ocean, making her one of the most feared marine figures in Scandinavian mythology.

The myth

According to the sources, Rán is the wife of Ægir, a jötunn who personifies the sea, and together they had nine daughters, goddesses of the waves. She is usually linked with a net that she uses to catch those who go to sea. She barely appears in the Poetic Edda, but in Reginsmál Loki borrows her net to catch the pike that guarded Andvari's gold.

In the Prose Edda, Snorri Sturluson tells how the gods discovered Rán's net, and explains that gold came to be called 'fire of Ægir' because of this story. While Ægir stands for the gentler side of the sea, Rán embodies its darker, treacherous face. Some sources suggest her daughters may be the mothers of Heimdall, though the link is unclear.

Worship and religion

The texts do not describe temples or festivals dedicated to Rán. Sailors, however, are said to have carried a piece of gold on board, to hand over to the goddess in case of shipwreck so that she would be less cruel to them. The sources say nothing about any modern worship.

Legacy

In skaldic poetry her name is used to refer to the sea through kennings such as 'Rán's land', 'Rán's hall', 'Rán's way' and 'Rán's bed'. Egill Skallagrímsson mentions her alongside Ægir in Sonatorrek, his poem mourning a son drowned at sea.

Ancient sources: She appears in the Poetic Edda, Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda, Völsunga saga, Friðþjófs saga and skaldic poetry such as Sonatorrek.

Trivia

A net that caught everyone. In the Prose Edda, the gods discover that Rán catches everyone who goes to sea. That story explains why gold could be called 'fire of Ægir'.
A name meaning theft. The Old Norse common noun rán means 'plundering' or 'theft', so many scholars think the goddess's name carries that meaning.
