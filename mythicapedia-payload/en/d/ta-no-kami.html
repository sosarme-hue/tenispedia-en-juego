Japanese kami of the rice fields

Ta-no-Kami

Ta-no-Kami is a Japanese agricultural kami, believed to watch over the rice harvest or to bring a good one. Its name means «rice fields», and it is one of the most cherished protectors of farmers.

The myth

Rather than the hero of grand tales, Ta-no-Kami is a presence that watches over the fields and guards a good harvest. The ancient chronicles Kojiki and Nihon Shoki mention rice kami such as Ukanomitama and Toyouke Bimeno Kami, regarded as female and also named in the Engishiki.

Its image varies by region: in the north it is called Nougami, in Kinki Tsukuri Gami, and in western Japan Sanbai Sama. It is linked to mountain faith and to the spirits of the dead.

Worship and religion

Farmers hold ceremonies through the agricultural year: at the new year, at the start of sowing, and at rice transplanting, when the kami is welcomed (saori) and later sent off (sanaburi). Harvest thanksgiving includes dancing, special foods and visits to the community shrine. In Kagoshima and parts of Miyazaki, Tanokansaa is venerated through stone statues since the eighteenth century. Scarecrows are considered variations of this kami.

Legacy

The fox, seen as Ta-no-Kami's messenger, gave rise to small fox shrines (kitsune tsuka), from which Inari shrines developed.

Ancient sources: The Kojiki and Nihon Shoki, which record the rice kami Ukanomitama and Toyouke Bimeno Kami; also the Engishiki.

Trivia

Stone statues. In Kagoshima and parts of Miyazaki, farmers set up stone statues of Tanokansaa, shaped like a Buddha, a Shinto priest or a peasant.
Sacred planting. In ancient times, the rice-planting women known as saotome played a religious role, and the work was bound by many taboos.
