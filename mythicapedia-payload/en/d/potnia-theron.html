Mistress of Animals, a Greek epithet of the goddess

Potnia Theron

Potnia Theron, or "Mistress of Animals", is a title of the goddess Artemis in Homer's Iliad, applied also to female deities of many ancient cultures shown with animals. It is a widespread motif in ancient art across the Mediterranean and the ancient Near East, and it matters because it shows a female figure holding two animals, one on each side.

The myth

The word potnia, meaning "mistress" or "lady", was a Mycenaean Greek term inherited by Classical Greek and cognate with Sanskrit patnī. Homer uses it for Artemis, and scholars regard the formula as well established.

In the Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite, the goddess is followed across the sacred slopes of Mount Ida by fawning wolves, lions, bears and panthers, which, under her spell, mate in their dens. Mount Ida is elsewhere linked with the mother goddess Cybele, although in this text the focus is the mistress of wild beasts.

Ancient imagery offers parallels: a Minoan seal shows a goddess with a spear flanked by lions, and similar figures appear in Egypt and Mesopotamia. Some scholars suggest the Greek Potnia descends from a Neolithic mother goddess, though the link is uncertain.

Worship and religion

At Sparta, the goddess Orthia, later absorbed into Artemis under Olympian influence, had a sanctuary whose cult image was an ancient wooden statue, a xoanon. Mycenaean tablets from Pylos mention several potniai with epithets, later associated with Demeter and Persephone.

Legacy

The motif of a lady dominating two animals became one of the most repeated themes in ancient art and iconography.

Ancient sources: Homer names the formula in the Iliad, and the Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite describes the mistress of beasts on Mount Ida.

Trivia

The Pylos tablets. On the Mycenaean Linear B tablets from Pylos, potnia almost always appears with an epithet indicating the goddess's place or function.
A very old figure. The oldest such depiction is the Seated Woman of Çatalhöyük, a clay sculpture made around 6000 BC.
