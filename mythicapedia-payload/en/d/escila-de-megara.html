Scylla of Megara

Scylla is, in Greek mythology, the daughter of King Nisus of Megara. Her story, best known through Ovid, explains the origin of a bird and serves as a warning about betrayal and reckless passion.

The myth

As the story goes, King Nisus of Megara possessed a single lock of purple hair that granted him and his city invincibility. When Minos, King of Crete, invaded the kingdom, the war dragged on without a clear winner.

From the battlements, Scylla watched the fighting and fell in love with Minos. In Ovid's telling, she wished she had no father so that she could pursue him, and she resolved to betray her people. One night she cut the lock while Nisus slept and handed it to the enemy, and Megara fell.

Minos, appalled, rejected her and sailed away. Scylla clung to the stern of his ship, but her father, now a sea eagle, pursued her. As she was about to fall into the sea, she found herself transformed into a bird, the ciris. Other versions say that Minos tied her to the prow of his ship and that she drowned. Ovid links the bird's name to the Greek verb keiro, 'to cut'.

Legacy

Linnaeus gave the name Passerina ciris to the painted bunting, the seven-colored finch, in memory of the unfortunate Scylla. Her story closely parallels that of Comaetho, daughter of Pterelaus, and tales of Pisidice and Leucophrye. Theodor Nöldeke regarded the story of al-Nadirah, recorded by al-Tabari, as derived from the tale of Scylla. Alexander Pope invokes her in his mock-heroic The Rape of the Lock as a warning to gallant young men.

Ancient sources: Ovid recounts her story in the Metamorphoses (Book VIII).

Trivia

A bird's name. The painted bunting carries the scientific name Passerina ciris, which Linnaeus chose in memory of Scylla.
A story that travels. Theodor Nöldeke considered the story of al-Nadirah, recorded by al-Tabari, to derive from the tale of Scylla.
