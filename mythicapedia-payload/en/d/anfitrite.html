Greek goddess of the sea, wife of Poseidon

Amphitrite

Amphitrite is, in Greek mythology, the goddess of the sea and the wife of Poseidon. Homer used her name almost as a synonym for the open ocean, and in later poetry it became a metaphor for the sea.

The myth

According to Hesiod's Theogony, Amphitrite was a daughter of Nereus and Doris and one of the fifty Nereids. Apollodorus, however, makes her a daughter of Oceanus and Tethys and counts her among the Oceanids, though he later lists her among the Nereids. With Poseidon she had Triton, a strong sea god who lives in golden palaces in the depths with his parents. Apollodorus adds Rhodos and Benthesikyme. Her offspring were also said to include fish, seals and dolphins.

The best-known story is that of her marriage. Amphitrite, who wished to remain a virgin, fled to the farthest reaches of the sea, near Mount Atlas. Many Nereids hid with her, and Poseidon sent searchers after her. A dolphin found her, persuaded her to return and brought her before the god. As a reward, Poseidon honoured the dolphin and placed it among the stars as the constellation Delphinus. Another version, recorded by Eustathius, says that the god first saw her dancing among the Nereids at Naxos and carried her off.

In a fragment of Bacchylides, Theseus visits his father Poseidon's undersea halls, where Amphitrite crowns him with a wedding wreath. Another tradition holds that this crown was a wedding gift from Aphrodite, passed from Amphitrite to Theseus, and that it was later set among the stars as the constellation Corona.

Worship and religion

Amphitrite had little cult of her own in Greece. She was of great importance in the archaic period, as the Homeric Hymn to Delian Apollo shows, where she attends the birth of the god. Later she appeared almost always beside Poseidon, and only when his command of the sea was to be emphasised. Pausanias saw her cult image in Poseidon's temple at the Isthmus of Corinth. Her cult does not survive today.

Legacy

Her name passed into poetry as a synonym for the sea, and it has been given to a genus of marine worms in the family Terebellidae and to the asteroid 29 Amphitrite. Several ships of the British Royal Navy and the United States Navy bore her name, and in 1936 Australia put her image on a postage stamp for a submarine telegraph cable. The Spanish poet Aurora Luque dedicated the poem "Anfitrite" to her in her collection Gavieras. In the gardens of La Granja de San Ildefonso in Segovia, a fountain shows her in a shell chariot drawn by dolphins, as an allegory of Isabel de Farnesio.

Ancient sources: Hesiod's Theogony, Homer's Odyssey, the Homeric Hymn to Delian Apollo, Pindar's Sixth Olympian Ode and Apollodorus's Library.

Trivia

A dolphin constellation. According to the story, the dolphin that found Amphitrite was rewarded with a place among the stars, in the constellation Delphinus.
A name meaning "the third". Her name is interpreted as "the third one who encircles [the sea]", although some scholars regard it as pre-Greek in origin.
