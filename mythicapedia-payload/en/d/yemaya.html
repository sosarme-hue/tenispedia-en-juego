Yoruba orisha of the waters and motherhood

Yemọja

Yemọja is one of the great river and sea deities of the Yoruba religion, mother of all the orishas and protector of women. Carried to the Americas by the African diaspora, her cult has taken new forms in Brazil and Cuba, where she is mainly linked with the ocean.

The myth

In her Yoruba origins, Yemọja is closely tied to the Ògùn River in Nigeria, from which her worship spread to other streams and springs. Accounts describe her as a motherly, fiercely protective figure who comforts her children and can even cure infertility, yet who can become destructive when angered, like the floodwaters of a turbulent river.

According to some traditions, when her waters broke a great flood formed the rivers and streams, and the first humans were born from her womb. Some priests believe she used her fresh water to help Obàtálá shape human beings from clay.

Early written accounts are unreliable: nineteenth-century European writers repeated an incestuous cosmology that is now seen as a set of misunderstandings. The surviving Yoruba sources are mainly songs and oriki passed down orally.

Worship and religion

Her original centre was Abeokuta, the city of the Egba people, where she is honoured at a shrine. In Brazil she is one of the most popular orishas, celebrated with public festivals on 2 February, when flowers and objects of beauty are offered to the sea, and with events in December and on New Year's Eve. In Cuba, within Santería, she is honoured on 7 September with a procession in Regla. Her worship remains very much alive in Brazil, Cuba and elsewhere.

Legacy

Her image has merged with the Virgin Mary through syncretism with Catholicism, especially with Our Lady of Regla and Our Lady of Navigators. In Brazil she has inspired music, literature and painting, and she is often shown as a woman rising from the sea. In Brazilian folklore she is also linked with Dona Janaína, a seductive figure associated with mermaids.

Ancient sources: The surviving myths come mainly from oral Yoruba tradition, in songs and oriki; there is no major ancient text describing her.
