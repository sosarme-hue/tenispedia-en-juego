Greek myth of Daphne's transformation into a laurel

Apollo and Daphne

Apollo and Daphne is a Greek myth in which the nymph Daphne turns into a laurel tree to escape the god Apollo. The story is traditionally linked to the origin of the laurel's association with Apollo and with victory.

The myth

Eros, annoyed when Apollo mocked his bow, fired two arrows. A golden one, which kindles love, struck Apollo, while a leaden one, which repels it, struck the nymph Daphne. She was a huntress who admired Artemis and rejected all her suitors, so Apollo pursued her relentlessly. Caught within reach, she called on her father, a river god (Ladon or Peneus depending on the version), who changed her into a laurel. Apollo vowed to honour the tree: he would wear its leaves, and its branches would crown heroes and champions.

According to Ovid, the god made the laurel evergreen.

Worship and religion

At Delphi, Apollo's priestess used laurel leaves, and the winners of the Pythian Games were given laurel wreaths.

Legacy

Gian Lorenzo Bernini carved the marble sculpture Apollo and Daphne between 1622 and 1625, which is now in the Galleria Borghese in Rome. Picasso also reworked the myth in painting, and it has been suggested that Klimt's The Kiss may depict the moment of the transformation.

Ancient sources: Parthenius is the earliest known source of the myth, but the most famous telling is Ovid's in his Metamorphoses.

Trivia

Oak wreaths. According to Ovid, the first winners of the Pythian Games were crowned with oak wreaths, because the laurel did not yet exist.
An Ovid retelling. The Roman poet Ovid told the story in his Metamorphoses, a collection of Greek fables first written around 8 CE.
