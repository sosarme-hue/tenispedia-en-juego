Hindu goddess of sacrificial offerings, wife of Agni

Svaha

Svaha is the goddess of sacrificial offerings in Hinduism, the personification of the ritual formula spoken when oblations are cast into the sacred fire. She is the consort of Agni, the fire god, and her name remains part of Hindu and Buddhist mantras.

The myth

According to tradition, Svaha is a daughter of Daksha, although some traditions make her a daughter of Brihaspati. She fell in love with Agni, who presided over the sacrifices of the Saptarishis, but he did not notice her. When Agni, troubled by desire for the sages' wives, withdrew into the forest to do penance, Svaha followed him. She took the forms of the sages' wives, though she could not imitate Arundhati, and united with him several times.

From this union was born Skanda, also called Kartikeya, who later commanded Indra's forces against the Asuras. As a reward, Skanda granted Svaha that her name be spoken at every sacrifice to the fire. In the Mahabharata, the sage Markandeya tells this story to the Pandavas.

Worship and religion

Svaha has no temples of her own, but her name is essential in fire sacrifices: without it, according to the texts, the gods reject the offering. In Thailand, where she is known as Mae Phra Pheling, she is venerated alongside other goddesses in protective rites and in Buddhist merit-making ceremonies.

Legacy

The word svaha ends many mantras in Hinduism and in Mahayana and Vajrayana Buddhism, and it is chanted in China, Japan, Korea and Tibet in variants such as sowaka or sabaha. Its meaning, 'well spoken' or 'good fortune', has given it a lasting place in religious language.

Ancient sources: The Rig Veda mentions her as the personified oblation, the Atharva Veda and the Yajur Veda record the ritual formula, and the Mahabharata narrates her story.

Trivia

A Buddhist ending. In Buddhist texts, svaha appears in dharanis inserted into Mahayana sutras such as the Lotus Sutra and became a common ending for mantras.
Made of the Vedas. According to tradition, her body consists of the four Vedas, and her six limbs are the six Vedangas, auxiliary disciplines of the Vedas.
