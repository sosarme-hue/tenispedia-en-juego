Greek god of medicine and healing

Asclepius

Asclepius is the Greek god of medicine and healing, son of Apollo, worshipped in many sanctuaries, above all at Epidaurus. He matters because his temples worked as centres of healing, and his symbol, a staff entwined with a serpent, is still linked with medicine today.

The myth

Accounts agree that Asclepius was the son of Apollo, but they disagree about his mother: some name Coronis, daughter of King Phlegyas, others Arsinoe, daughter of Leucippus. In one tale Coronis dies on the funeral pyre for being unfaithful to Apollo, and the child is snatched from the flames, by Hermes or by Apollo himself. Another version has the infant exposed on Mount Tittheion at Epidaurus, where a goat fed him and a dog guarded him until a shepherd saw a divine light around him.

Asclepius was raised and taught by the centaur Chiron, who instructed him in healing and in the use of medicinal plants. He became so skilled that, according to the stories, he could raise the dead. Hades complained to Zeus that he was stealing his subjects, and Zeus, fearing that the art of resurrection would spread among humans, struck him down with a thunderbolt. Ovid says he was then set among the stars as the constellation Ophiuchus, the Serpent-Holder.

His descendants were also healers. The Iliad mentions his sons Podalirius and Machaon, physicians who fight at Troy. Other lists give him five daughters, among them Hygieia (health) and Panacea (universal remedy). Sources differ on his wife: some name Epione, others Lampetia.

Worship and religion

The most important sanctuary was at Epidaurus in the northeastern Peloponnese, where the sick came seeking cures and a medical practice was linked to the temple. Other asclepieia were founded at Trikala in Thessaly and on the island of Kos, where Hippocrates may have begun his career. The Hippocratic Oath invokes Asclepius alongside Apollo, Hygieia and Panacea.

Legacy

The rod of Asclepius, a snake-entwined staff, remains a symbol of medicine and is often mistakenly confused with the caduceus of Hermes. The Aesculapian snake, a non-venomous species found across the Mediterranean, is named after him. The asteroids Asclepius and Aesculapia also bear his name.

Ancient sources: The Iliad names his sons Podalirius and Machaon, and Ovid recounts his placement among the stars.

Trivia
