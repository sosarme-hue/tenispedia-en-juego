Slavic god of the sun and light

Dazhbog

Dazhbog is a Slavic god of the sun and light, linked to fertility and wealth, and worshipped by the pre-Christian Slavic peoples. His name is usually understood as "giving god" or "dispenser of wealth".

The myth

According to tradition, Dazhbog lived in Rai, a land of endless summer in the east. He crossed the sky in a chariot drawn by four white horses with golden wings, producing sunlight with a "shield of fire". He rewarded the virtuous with precious goods and punished the wicked. Some versions make him the husband of Chislobog or Nochuna, the moon goddess, and the father of daughters who became stars.

The sources do not agree on his parentage. The medieval Hypatian Codex makes him a son of Svarog, whom it equates with Hephaestus, and calls him a "Sun tsar". Other writers link him with Svarozhits or Khors, and some mythologists have proposed that Dazhbog and Khors were one god. It is also unclear whether he was always male, since the word for "sun" in the Slavic languages is feminine or neuter.

Worship and religion

The pre-Christian Slavs venerated him across their tribes, and the Primary Chronicle records that Prince Vladimir the Great set up his statue in Kiev in 980, alongside other gods. In the same tradition, the people proudly called themselves "the grandchildren of Dazhbog". His worship does not survive as an organised religion.

Legacy

His name appears in The Tale of Igor's Campaign, a medieval epic from Kievan Rus'. His figure fuels debate about the solar nature of Slavic gods, and Serbian folklore features Dabog, a lame lord of the underworld associated with precious metals.

Ancient sources: The Primary Chronicle, the Hypatian Codex and The Tale of Igor's Campaign, medieval texts from Kievan Rus'.

Trivia

The grandchildren of Dazhbog. The ancient Slavs proudly called themselves "the grandchildren of Dazhbog", showing how central this god was to their identity.
A lame Dabog. In Serbian folklore, Dabog is a lord of the underworld who is lame, a trait that recalls the Greek smith Hephaestus.
