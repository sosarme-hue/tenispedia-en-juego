Egyptian goddess of water and the Nile cataracts

Anuket

Anuket (Anukis in Greek) was an ancient Egyptian goddess of water, linked above all to the cataracts of the Nile and to Lower Nubia. She was especially worshipped at Elephantine, near the First Cataract, and her role in bringing the annual flood made her essential to the fertility of the fields.

The myth

According to Egyptian tradition, Anuket had a Nubian origin. From the Middle Kingdom onward she formed, together with her parents Khnum and Satet, the Elephantine triad. The earliest texts present her as a daughter of Ra, while other traditions make her Satet's sister or a junior consort of Khnum. Her name, usually rendered as “the Embracer”, relates to the way she was shown with outstretched arms, an image that may have recalled the shape of the Nile and its two tributaries.

Her epithets reflect her role as guardian of Egypt's southern frontier. In the New Kingdom her southern character was stressed so strongly that one of her titles was “the Nubian”. She was associated with fast-moving water, which explains her link to papyrus. In the Ptolemaic period she was also connected with sexuality and fertility, partly through the cowrie shell, whose shape recalls the vulva.

As the goddess who brought the flood, she was at times called “Mother of the King”, since she was shown suckling the pharaoh. Her iconography presents her wearing a cylindrical crown of feathers and holding a papyrus sceptre and the ankh.

Worship and religion

Her cult spread through Elephantine, Philae, Komir, Deir el-Medina, Dendera and nearly all of Nubia. An important centre was the temple on Sehel Island, where a shrine or altar already existed in the time of the 13th Dynasty pharaoh Sobekhotep III, and Amenhotep II later dedicated a chapel to her. In the New Kingdom, a river procession in her honour took place at Elephantine during the first month of Shemu, alongside the processional festival of Khnum and Anuket.

Her festival began when the Nile started to rise, on the seventh day of the month of Thoth. People threw gold, jewellery and precious objects into the river as thanks for the life-giving water. During these days the taboo on certain sacred fish was lifted, and those fish were eaten as part of the ritual. Her sacred animal was the dorcas gazelle, common along the Nile in the First Cataract region, and a gazelle necropolis has been found at Komir.
