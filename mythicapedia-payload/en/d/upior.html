vampiric being of Slavic folklore

Upiór

The upiór is a vampiric being from Slavic and Turkic folklore, often regarded by scholars as a prototype of the modern vampire. It reveals how Slavic communities explained death, the soul and the dangers posed by poorly buried dead.

The myth

In Slavic folk belief, the soul left the body at death and wandered its neighbourhood for forty days before passing into eternal life. A violent or untimely death, the death of an unbaptised child or a sinner, or a body without proper burial could leave the soul unclean and the corpse open to possession.

An upiór was a person cursed before death, a suicide, a witch, or someone whose corpse had been desecrated. At night it drank blood, suffocated its victims and could haunt its own family, even visiting a widowed spouse. Plagues were blamed on these beings, which led communities to accuse and lynch innocent people.

Worship and religion

Protective measures included removing the corpse through a special opening, placing garlic, bricks or iron in its mouth, putting wild rose or hawthorn branches in the coffin, and performing vampire burials such as decapitation or burning.

Legacy

The word entered English through Byron and Polidori, and Bram Stoker popularised it with Dracula, turning the upiór into the recognisable vampire of literature and film.
