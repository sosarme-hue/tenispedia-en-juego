Trojan hero of Greek legend, son of King Mygdon

Coroebus

Coroebus was a hero of Greek legend, son of King Mygdon of Phrygia, who came to the aid of Troy during the war. He is remembered mainly for his death during the fall of the city and for the phrase his name left in the Greek language.

The myth

According to the tradition, Coroebus travelled to Troy out of love for Princess Cassandra, daughter of Priam, hoping to marry her. He arrived late, when the war had already been going on for a long time, and so he came to be remembered as a rather naive figure.

During the sack of the city, the stories say he persuaded some of his fellow soldiers, Aeneas among them, to put on enemy armour and pass unnoticed. When he tried to defend Cassandra from an assault by Ajax the Lesser, he was killed. Accounts differ on who struck the fatal blow: Pausanias mentions Neoptolemus or Diomedes, while the Aeneid credits Peneleos. His story survives in only a few texts and usually as a minor episode within the war.

Legacy

Pausanias mentions a memorial to Coroebus on the border of Phrygia, a sign that his memory lasted in the region. His name gave rise to the Greek proverb "more foolish than Coroebus", used of someone who arrived too late or acted clumsily. The same name also belongs to two other figures, a defender of Thebes killed by Parthenopaeus and a proverbial fool.

Ancient sources: Pausanias and Virgil's Aeneid, which record differing versions of his death.

Trivia

A memorial in Phrygia. Pausanias states that a memorial to Coroebus stood on the border of Phrygia, suggesting his memory was kept alive in the region.
Several characters with the same name. The name Coroebus also belongs to a defender of Thebes, killed by Parthenopaeus, and to a proverbial fool, so the figures are easy to confuse.
