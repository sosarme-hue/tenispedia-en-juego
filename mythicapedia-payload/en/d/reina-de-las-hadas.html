queen of the fairies in Irish and British folklore

Fairy Queen

The Fairy Queen is the female ruler of the fairies in the folklore and literature of Ireland and Britain. Her image has been reshaped for centuries and has helped define the modern picture of fairies.

The myth

In Irish tradition, Oonagh, Una or Nuala is the wife of Finvarra, the fairy king of the west, although he keeps other lovers. A human girl unwittingly insults her and must serve as her midwife. The queen tries to take revenge with a belt that would cut the girl in two, but a fairy servant warns her in time.

In English and Scottish ballads, the queen is sometimes kind, as in Thomas the Rhymer, and sometimes deadly, as in Tam Lin, where she pays a tithe to Hell every seven years.

Legacy

Shakespeare portrayed her as Titania in A Midsummer Night's Dream, and Spenser made her Gloriana, the heroine of The Faerie Queene. Barrie, Baum and Brandon Mull also brought her into their works.

Ancient sources: Irish tales were mostly passed on orally; the ballads collected by Francis James Child in the nineteenth century are their main written source.

Trivia

A borrowed name. Titania derives from Ovid, who uses it as an epithet of the Roman goddess Diana.
The queen of the féerie. Madame D'Aulnoy, who popularized the term fairy tale, was nicknamed la reine de la féerie.
