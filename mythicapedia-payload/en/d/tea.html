Titaness, goddess of light in Greek mythology

Theia

Theia is a Titaness of Greek mythology, daughter of Uranus and Gaia and wife of her brother Hyperion, with whom she was the mother of Helios, Selene and Eos. Her name means divine and links her to celestial brightness, which is why she matters as the mythic origin of the lights of the sky.

The myth

According to Hesiod's Theogony, Theia joined her brother Hyperion, the god of the heights, and bore Helios the bright sun, Selene the shining moon and Eos, who lights up all living things on earth. She came to be seen as the source from which all light flowed, and because she was thought to be the mother of sight, she was also linked to the power of seeing.

The Homeric Hymns give her the epithets Euryphaessa, meaning far-shining, and Ichnaie, and present her as a prophetess in the oracular temple of Phthia. Pindar calls her Theia of many names, a phrase that suggests she was merged with other mother goddesses such as Phoebe, Leto, Rhea and Cybele.

After the Titanomachy, tradition places her in the palace of Helios, beside the streams of the river Oceanus, where she waits each night for her son, who travels in a huge golden cup. Diodorus Siculus gives a different version in which Theia is Basileia, queen, the eldest of her brothers, who ruled after her father left and married Hyperion to produce heirs. Her jealous brothers killed Hyperion and drowned Helios in the river Eridanus. Selene threw herself from a roof, and Basileia, driven mad, vanished. In Diodorus's account, Helios appeared to her in a dream to say that he and his sister would be turned into immortal stars.

Worship and religion

The sources offer little information about her worship. Diodorus relates that after Basileia disappeared, the people raised altars to her and honoured her with kettledrums and cymbals. Her role as a prophetess at Phthia suggests a link with oracular practice, but there is no evidence of a widespread cult or of its survival to the present day.

Legacy

Her role as mother of the Moon gave her name to a hypothetical planet, Theia, which according to the giant impact hypothesis collided with the Earth and created the Moon. One of her epithets, Euryphaessa, is used as the scientific name of a species of Australian leafhoppers. On the Gigantomachy frieze of the Pergamon Altar, a female figure fighting beside Helios has been conjectured to represent Theia, although this is a modern interpretation rather than a certainty.

Ancient sources: Hesiod in the Theogony, the Homeric Hymns, Pindar in his Fifth Isthmian Ode, Stesichorus, Catullus, Diodorus Siculus and Plutarch.

Trivia

Theia of many names. Pindar calls her Theia of many names, and texts give her epithets such as Euryphaessa, Ichnaie or Basileia, which vary by author.
Eyes that shine. The ancient Greeks believed the eyes emitted an invisible ray of light that allowed sight, which is why Theia, as mother of sight, was linked to that power.
