Isles of the Blessed, the paradise islands of Greek mythology

Fortunate Isles

The Fortunate Isles, or Isles of the Blessed (makárōn nêsoi), were in Greek mythology the place where virtuous souls and heroic warriors rested eternally after death, a paradise lying far out in the Atlantic. Linked to the Elysian Fields, the idea shaped the Western vision of a happy afterlife.

The myth

In the Greek tradition, the souls of the virtuous spent eternity on these islands, under the sun, among green and ever-blooming landscapes, in contrast to Tartarus, where the damned suffered. Hesiod associated them with Elysium, a utopian place in the underworld thought to lie on the western ocean at the edge of the known world. Later, Pindar reduced the number of islands to one, pictured as a shady parkland where residents enjoyed athletics and music, the ideal life of the ancient Greek aristocracy. Another version reserved them for those who chose to be reincarnated three times and were judged pure enough each time.

In the ancient world they were placed beyond the western limits of Libya, the African continent. Plutarch located them in the Atlantic near Hispania and recorded that Sertorius, during a civil war in Rome, considered retiring to them. Pliny the Elder added that the sea washed monstrous putrid bodies onto their shores. Plato, in the Republic, has the philosopher king depart for them once his rule is over.

Legacy

They were often identified with the Christian heaven. They served as the setting for the dialogues of the dead, a literary genre Lucian of Samosata practised in his True History. Ptolemy used them as the reference for measuring geographical longitude, and they continued to define the prime meridian through the Middle Ages. Today they are traditionally identified with the Macaronesian archipelagos: the Azores, Madeira, the Savage Islands, the Canaries and Cape Verde.

Ancient sources: Hesiod, Pindar, Plato, Lucian of Samosata, Plutarch, Pliny the Elder and Philostratus.

Trivia

One island from many. Hesiod spoke of them in the plural, but Pindar reduced them to a single island, showing how the image changed over the centuries.
Monsters on the shore. Pliny the Elder reported that the sea constantly washed the putrefying bodies of monsters onto these islands.
