legendary founder of Nineveh

Ninus

Ninus (Nino) is, in Hellenistic tradition, the king who founded Nineveh and the first Assyrian empire. His figure, of uncertain origin, blends with that of Nimrod and with Queen Semiramis, and he matters because his story joins a Greek account of Assyria with a romance of love and power.

The myth

According to Greek historians, Ninus was the son of Belus, a name that may go back to a Semitic title such as Ba'al, meaning "lord". With the help of Ariaeus, king of Arabia, he conquered most of western Asia in seventeen years, defeated legendary kings such as Barzanes and Pharnus, founded Nineveh and reigned for fifty-two years.

During the siege of Bactra he met Semiramis, the wife of one of his officers, whom he took from her husband and married. Their son Ninyas succeeded him. After Ninus died, Semiramis governed the empire, raised a tomb for him near Babylon and, according to some, was the one who murdered him.

Legacy

The romance of Ninus and Semiramis gave rise to a first-century AD Greek novel and to mosaics found at Antioch on the Orontes. Isidore of Seville credited Ninus with inventing idolatry, an idea that shaped thinking for centuries. Shakespeare mentions him in A Midsummer Night's Dream, where the actors mispronounce "Ninus' Tomb" as "Ninny's Tomb". Edmund Spenser cites him in The Faerie Queene, and Lope de Vega wrote a sonnet titled "Al rey Nino". In London in 1846, Verdi's opera Nabucco was rewritten as Nino because of British censorship.

Ancient sources: Ctesias of Cnidus (c. 400 BC), physician at the Persian court, whose account Diodorus Siculus later expanded.

Trivia

Ninus the centaur. Some traditions credit him with training the first hunting dogs and taming horses, and for this he is sometimes shown as a centaur.
Ninny's Tomb. In Shakespeare's comedy, the actors mispronounce the name of Ninus's tomb, which sounds like "Ninny", meaning fool.
