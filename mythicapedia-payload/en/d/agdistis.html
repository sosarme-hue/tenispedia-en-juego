divine figure of Anatolia linked to Cybele

Agdistis

Agdistis is a divine figure of Anatolian origin, closely tied to the Great Mother Cybele and to her young companion Attis. Preserved mainly by Pausanias and the Christian apologist Arnobius, his story explains the origin of the goddess's eunuch priests.

The myth

According to the tradition of Pessinus, Zeus spilled his semen on the earth while asleep, and from it arose a being with both male and female organs, called Agdistis. The other gods, fearing it, cut off its male genitals, and from them grew an almond tree. A daughter of the river-god Sangarius ate an almond from the tree and became pregnant with Attis, a child left exposed who was raised by a goat and grew into a strikingly beautiful youth.

Agdistis fell in love with Attis. When Attis was about to marry the king's daughter, Agdistis appeared at the wedding and drove the guests to frenzy, and Attis and the king castrated themselves. Attis died of the wound, but Agdistis, full of remorse, persuaded Zeus that the body should never decay. In Arnobius's version Agdistis is born from a rock fertilised by Jupiter, and it is Liber who makes him drunk and binds him so that he mutilates himself; a pomegranate tree springs from his blood.

Worship and religion

Its main cult centre was Pessinus, from where the cult apparently spread to other parts of Anatolia, Aegean islands, mainland Greece, Crimea and Egypt. Inscriptions name her at Iconium, Methymna, Paros, Athens and Rhamnous, and in Egypt a naos built under Ptolemy II. Her clearest role is as companion of Cybele, sharing the city's sanctuary and the mourning for Attis.

Ancient sources: Pausanias, in his Description of Greece, and Arnobius of Sicca, in his Against the Pagans, preserve the fullest accounts of her story.
