Graeco-Egyptian god of souls and the underworld

Hermanubis

Hermanubis is a Graeco-Egyptian god who, according to tradition, leads the souls of the dead to the underworld. He arose from the fusion of Hermes from Greek mythology and Anubis from Egyptian mythology, and his popularity grew during the Roman rule of Egypt.

The myth

According to the myth, Hermanubis arose from the combination of two figures with similar roles: Hermes and Anubis were both conductors of souls. For this reason he is considered a son of Osiris and Nephthys, within the Egyptian divine family to which Anubis belongs.

His image joins a human body with a jackal's head, the animal linked to Anubis, and he carries the caduceus that belonged to Hermes. This iconography reflects the blending of both traditions and the role attributed to him within the Egyptian priesthood.

The name appears in many sources, mostly from the Roman period. Plutarch uses it as another name for Anubis in his underworld aspect, while Porphyry describes it as a "composite" and "half-Greek" name.

Worship and religion

He was especially popular during the Roman domination of Egypt, and he was associated with the Egyptian priesthood. Little is known about temples or rites dedicated specifically to him.

Legacy

His figure may have inspired Saint Christopher, the dog-headed saint who, like Anubis and Hermanubis, was a powerful ferryman protecting travelers.

Ancient sources: Plutarch and Porphyry, who cite his name, along with numerous inscriptions and literary texts, mostly from the Roman period.

Trivia

A composite name. Porphyry calls Hermanubis a "composite" and "half-Greek" name, a way of combining two gods that was uncommon in traditional Greek religion.
An early parallel. The god Hermaphroditus, attested from the fourth century BC onwards, also unites two gods, Hermes and Aphrodite, though differently from Hermanubis.
