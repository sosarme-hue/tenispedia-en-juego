Wadjet

Wadjet was the cobra goddess and protector of Lower Egypt and of kingship. Her name means "the Green One", a nod to the papyrus and the fertility of the soil, and her cobra form ended up as the uraeus on the crowns of the pharaohs.

The myth

According to one tradition, Wadjet was the daughter of Ra and served as his seeing eye. She found Shu and Tefnut, his lost children, and her proud father asked her to stay by his side to protect him. In her serpent form she struck any enemy who threatened him.

In another story, Wadjet is the nurse of the infant Horus. Together with his mother Isis, she protected him from his treacherous uncle Set by taking refuge in the swamps of the Nile Delta. The Pyramid Texts show her, alongside Nekhbet, nursing the dead king to sustain his divine nature. In the earliest images she appears as a cobra coiled around a papyrus stem, the plant that gave Lower Egypt its symbol.

Worship and religion

Her main centre was Dep, known to the Greeks as Buto, where she had a renowned oracle that may have inspired the oracular tradition later found in Greece. She also had a large temple at Imet (today Tell Nebesha), where she was worshipped as the "Lady of Imet". Later she was joined by Min and Horus to form a triad of deities, following an Osirian pattern. Her festival was held on the seventh day of the month of Paini. At Tebtunis, in the Faiyum, another temple was dedicated to her, and rituals were performed there in her honour.

Legacy

Amulets of the eye, in green and blue tones, survive in large numbers and were believed to bring fertility and protection. The cobra on her crown became the emblem of the rulers of Lower Egypt. The serpent entwined around a papyrus stem linked to her is also connected with the Mediterranean caduceus, though its origins may have been separate.

Ancient sources: The Pyramid Texts mention her, alongside Nekhbet, as the nurse of the dead king.

Trivia

The Nebty title. Her name appeared beside Nekhbet's in the royal title "The Two Ladies", symbolising rule over the Two Lands.
A nurse of pharaohs. A pendant from Tutankhamun's tomb shows the grown king being nursed by the goddess, an image with funerary and coronation meaning.
