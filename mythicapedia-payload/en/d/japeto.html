Greek Titan, father of Prometheus and Atlas

Iapetus

Iapetus is one of the twelve Titans of Greek mythology, a son of Uranus and Gaia and the father of Atlas, Menoetius, Prometheus and Epimetheus. He matters mainly because, in several traditions, he becomes the ancestor of mortal humanity and, through his sons, the source of some of its best-known traits.

The myth

In Homer's Iliad, Iapetus is the one Titan who appears imprisoned in Tartarus alongside his brother Cronus, after the end of the Titanomachy. Hesiod gives him a wife, the Oceanid Clymene, who in his account is the mother of Prometheus, although other sources name Asia, Asopis, Libya or Themis instead. Hesiod names his sons Atlas, the bold; Menoetius, the renowned; Prometheus, the crafty; and Epimetheus, the dim-witted.

Each of them is assigned a fault. Prometheus steals fire for mortals by deception, as Horace recalls in his Odes. Epimetheus accepts Pandora, the woman fashioned by Zeus, and with her come the ills of mankind. Atlas is condemned to hold up the sky at the edge of the world. In Hesiod's Works and Days, Prometheus is called a son of Iapetus with no mother named, while in Aeschylus's Prometheus Bound he is the son of Themis, yet Atlas still remains his brother.

Later versions multiply. Virgil lists him among the children of Earth alongside Ceus and Typhoeus; Hyginus makes him a giant, the son of Tartarus and Earth; and Silius Italicus says he lies buried beneath the island of Inarime, the usual fate of defeated giants. Pindar, for his part, speaks of human lineages descending from the daughters of the race of Iapetus, through Pyrrha, daughter of Epimetheus.

Legacy

Iapetus has had a notable afterlife in biblical interpretation. Some creationists, such as John Milton, have equated him with Japheth, the son of Noah, because their names resemble each other and their myths overlap, although specialists in Indo-European linguistics reject this identification firmly. Josephus made Japheth the ancestor of the Japhetites, that is, the peoples of Europe, and that idea, joined to the similarity of the names, fused the two identities from the early modern period to the present.

Ancient sources: Homer mentions him in the Iliad, Hesiod in the Theogony and in Works and Days, Aeschylus in Prometheus Bound, and Horace in his Odes.

Trivia

A name with an echo. Cornutus, playing with the etymology of his name, describes him as a 'spokesman' and makes him responsible for animals producing sounds.
A third of the earth. The Sibylline Oracles say that Iapetus, Cronus and Titan each received a third of the earth as their inheritance, a division that Judeo-Christian texts attribute to the sons of Noah.
