horse-faced woman spirit of Central American folklore

Siguanaba

The Siguanaba is a spirit from the folklore of Central America and Mexico who appears as a beautiful woman with her face hidden, luring lone travelers. Her figure blends Mesoamerican roots, reflected in her Nahuatl-derived name, with European tales brought during the colonial era.

The myth

According to tradition, the Siguanaba appears at night to men travelling alone, often near rivers or roadsides, and leads them far from familiar paths. When they come close, she turns her face: it is a horse's face or a skull, and the shock sickens, drives mad or kills them.

In Guatemala she is linked to infidelity, punishing husbands who cheat. Some versions say she also lures children by imitating their mother, then abandons them lost and traumatised. Popular belief holds that making the sign of the cross or biting the edge of a machete breaks her spell.

Legacy

In Nicaragua and Costa Rica she is known as cegua, a word derived from the Nahuatl cihuatl, meaning "woman". In Mexico she is also called the "horse-faced woman", and some relate her to La Llorona.

Trivia

Salvadoran origin. In El Salvador, one story identifies her with Sihuehuet, a peasant woman who poisoned her husband, the prince Yeisun, and was cursed by the god Teotl to wander forever as the Sihuanaba.
Kinship with the lavanderas. In Asturias there are the lavanderas, ghosts who wash clothes by rivers on moonless nights. Some versions closely resemble the Latin American myth, as they also seduce men before revealing their true face.
