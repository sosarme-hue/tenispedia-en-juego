Thracian king and ally of Troy

Rhesus of Thrace

Rhesus is a mythical king of Thrace in Greek myth who appears briefly in the Iliad as an ally of the Trojans. His nighttime death and the theft of his horses made him a figure remembered by later tradition.

The myth

According to Homer, Rhesus was the son of Eioneus, a name that may be linked to the city of Eion at the mouth of the Strymon. Later writers gave him a more exotic parentage: his father was the river god Strymon and his mother one of the Muses, named variously as Euterpe, Calliope or Terpsichore. According to tradition, he was raised by fountain nymphs.

When Thrace was attacked by Scythia, Rhesus reached Troy later than the other allies. Dolon, a Trojan who had been spying for Hector, was captured by Diomedes and Odysseus and told them about the arrival of the Thracians and Rhesus's wealth: the finest horses and golden armour fit for gods. That same night, while the Thracians slept, the two Greeks slipped into the camp, killed Rhesus in his tent and carried off his horses. In the account, Rhesus died without ever entering battle.

His wife, Arganthone, went to the place where they had first lain together and stayed there calling for him day after day until she died of starvation. An anonymous tragedy titled Rhesus, sometimes attributed to Euripides, portrays a far more heroic Rhesus than Homer's, and in it Terpsichore mourns over her son's remains.

Legacy

A river in Bithynia also bears his name, and an attendant river god of the same name appears in Greek myth. A glacier in Antarctica and a Jovian asteroid are named after Rhesus.

Ancient sources: Homer's Iliad is the main source, and the tragedy Rhesus, transmitted among the plays of Euripides, develops his story.
