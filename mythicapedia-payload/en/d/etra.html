princess of Troezen, mother of Theseus

Aethra

Aethra is a princess of Troezen in Greek mythology, daughter of King Pittheus. As the mother of Theseus, her story links the origin of the Athenian hero with the god Poseidon and the Trojan War.

The myth

According to the tradition, King Aegeus of Athens, who had no children, visited Troezen, where his host Pittheus got him drunk and put him to bed with his daughter. Aethra then left the sleeping Aegeus, following Athena's instructions in a dream, and spent the night with Poseidon on the nearby island of Sphairia. The boy thus had two possible fathers, and Pittheus spread the story that Theseus was the son of the god, who was much revered in Troezen.

Before leaving for Athens, Aegeus hid his sandals, shield and sword under a huge rock and told Aethra that their son should lift it when he grew up to recover those weapons. Theseus did so, and after this test he became a great hero, with deeds such as killing the Minotaur.

Later, Theseus kidnapped Helen and left her in his mother's care. Helen's brothers, the Dioscuri, rescued her and took Aethra captive to Lacedaemon in revenge. There she became Helen's slave and followed her to Troy. Her grandson Acamas found her after the city fell, and at the request of Demophon, Agamemnon secured her freedom. According to Hyginus, Aethra took her own life out of grief over the deaths of her sons.

Worship and religion

Aethra is linked to the worship of Athena in Troezen. According to Plutarch, she dedicated a temple to Athena Apaturia, the Deceitful, on the island of Sphairia, and the maidens of the city offered their girdles to the goddess on their wedding day. There is no evidence of a cult dedicated to her alone.

Legacy

In art, the story of Aethra and the discovery of Aegeus's weapons was painted by Nicolas Poussin, Antonio Balestra and Nicolas-Guy Brenet. Richard Strauss turned her into Aithra, a sorceress and concubine of Poseidon, in his opera Die ägyptische Helena, though with significant changes to the ancient character.

Ancient sources: Her story appears in sources such as Hyginus and Plutarch, and in artworks such as the chest of Cypselus and a painting by Polygnotus in Delphi.
