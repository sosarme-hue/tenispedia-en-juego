underground beings of Galician, Asturian and Portuguese legend

Mouros

The mouros are supernatural beings of Galician, Asturian and Portuguese legend, said to dwell underground and guard gold and treasure. In folk belief they explained the hillforts, burial mounds and ancient ruins of the region, which peasants no longer understood.

The myth

According to Galician tradition, the mouros lived underground in dens and tunnels, where they mined gold and worked as goldsmiths. They were considered pagans and unbaptised, yet their lives resembled those of the peasants: they buried their dead, had their own priests and enjoyed wine, meat and dancing at night. The men were often dark-skinned, while the mouras, the women, had a reputation as sorceresses, with reddish hair and pale skin.

The mouros often offered brave lone travellers tests of courage or struck bargains: they paid gold in exchange for milk, wine or other goods they lacked. The deal demanded secrecy, because if a human revealed where the wealth came from, the gold turned into stones or charcoal and the pact ended.

Another version says the mouros fought Romans, Visigoths and other peoples before being driven out, a tale meant to explain the strength of the hillforts. Their name has been linked to Latin maurus, to Galician ouro ("gold") or, according to the philologist Isidoro Millán, to a Celtic root that gives Latin mortuus, "dead".

Legacy

The mouros appear in many Galician place names, usually marking hillforts, burial mounds, Roman mines or other ancient ruins.
