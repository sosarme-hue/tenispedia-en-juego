Egyptian creator god of the sun

Atum

Atum is the primordial god of Egyptian mythology, from whom, according to the stories, all other gods and the universe arose. He is linked to the evening sun and to the underworld, and he remained important throughout Egypt's history, although Ra eventually overshadowed him.

The myth

According to the Heliopolitan theology, Atum existed in the primordial waters of Nun, where he rose onto a primeval mound (the benben) and created himself. From there he brought forth Shu, god of air, and Tefnut, goddess of moisture. The texts offer different accounts of this birth: some say he spat them out of his mouth, others that they came from his lips or his hand, and some describe a self-generated act. Because the Egyptian word for hand is feminine, that hand was sometimes read as a female principle within the god.

Shu and Tefnut became the parents of Geb and Nut, earth and sky, whose children were Osiris, Isis, Nephthys, Seth and Horus. Memphite theology, by contrast, held that Atum was conceived from the heart of Ptah, who created him through speech and thought.

The Coffin Texts record a dialogue between Atum and Osiris about the end of the world: everything would return to the waters of Nun, and only the two of them would survive, as serpents. Atum is also the god who emerges as a serpent, the animal that renews itself each morning, and in the binary solar cycle he is contrasted with Khepri, the young sun of dawn.

Worship and religion

Atum was worshipped throughout Egypt, though his main centre of worship was Heliopolis. In the Old Kingdom he held a central place in belief, and was thought to lift the dead king's soul to the heavens. In the Middle Kingdom he appears in the Book of the Dead as a guide on the journey through the Duat. In the New Kingdom, the Divine Adoratrices of Amun, high priestesses at Thebes, acted as the Hand of Atum in temple rituals. Ra gradually eclipsed him, yet Atum continued to receive cult alongside the king.

Legacy

The only surviving trace of his great centre at Heliopolis is the obelisk of the Temple of Ra-Atum, erected by Senusret I and still standing in Cairo. His identity merged with other gods, such as Ra, with whom he formed Ra-Atum, and Amun, in the New Kingdom.

Ancient sources: He is frequently mentioned in the Pyramid Texts, and also appears in the Coffin Texts, the Book of the Dead and the Shabaka Stone.

Trivia

A god in the form of a serpent. In some texts Atum appears as a serpent, the form he will return to at the end of the creative cycle and which, according to the Book of the Dead, he took when emerging from the waters.
Priestesses as his hand. The Divine Adoratrices of Amun at Thebes acted as the Hand of Atum in temple rituals, a role tied to the Egyptian word for hand, which is feminine.
