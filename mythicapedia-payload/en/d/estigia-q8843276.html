river of the Greek underworld

Styx

The Styx is the main river of the underworld in Greek mythology, the boundary separating the world of the living from that of the dead. Its name, linked to hatred, gave rise to the adjective 'stygian'.

The myth

According to tradition, Styx was a goddess, the eldest daughter of Oceanus, personifying this river. Plato distinguishes the river from the lake into which it flows. Hesiod describes its waters as ancient, rising from a black cliff in Tartarus.

Among the infernal rivers, alongside Phlegethon, Lethe, Acheron and Cocytus, it converged into a great marsh, and souls crossed it in Charon's boat. Its most famous role is in oaths: a god who swore falsely by its waters lay breathless and voiceless for nine years, deprived of ambrosia and nectar. Later versions tell that Thetis dipped Achilles in it, making him invulnerable except for the heel she held.

Worship and religion

Rather than a cult of its own, the river had a ritual role in the oaths of the gods: Zeus sent Iris to fetch Styx water in a golden vessel.

Legacy

Dante places it in the fifth circle of Hell in the Divine Comedy, where the wrathful are drowned in its muddy waters. Its name gave rise to the adjective 'stygian', meaning infernal or hateful.

Ancient sources: Homeric poetry mentions it in divine oaths, and Hesiod's Theogony describes its waters in detail.

Trivia

Achilles' heel. The Roman version of Thetis explains why Achilles' heel was his only vulnerable spot.
A real Styx. Scholars placed a real Styx near Lake Avernus in the Bay of Naples, and Herodotus mentioned a spring in Arcadian Nonacris.
