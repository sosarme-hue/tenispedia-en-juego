Egyptian protective dwarf god

Ptah-Patek

Ptah-Patek, also called Pataikos, is a protective dwarf god of ancient Egyptian religion, mainly shown on amulets. His image spread through the Phoenician and Punic Mediterranean, reaching Etruria and, to a lesser extent, Greece.

The myth

The name Pataikos comes from Herodotus, who in his Histories applies it to the figureheads of Phoenician ships, protective dwarves. According to him, Ptah-Patek is a son or manifestation of Ptah, which makes the name mean "little Ptah". The link is explained by the fact that in Egypt dwarves were associated with metalworkers, a craft tied to Ptah.

He is shown as a misshapen, naked, green dwarf with a potbelly and an oversized head. Unlike Ptah, he carries no royal sceptre or symbols, and he often wears a scarab, a sign of solar regeneration. He protected against evil, dangerous animals and chaos, and assisted Taweret in childbirth alongside Bes.

Worship and religion

His popularity grew in the Late Period among Egyptians, who linked him with Bes from the 22nd Dynasty onwards. In the Hellenistic period he spread across the eastern Mediterranean. In childbirth spells, a small figurine of him was placed on the forehead of the pregnant woman.

Ancient sources: Herodotus, in his Histories, mentions him under the name Pataikos.

Trivia

Ship figureheads. Herodotus recalls that Phoenician ships carried figures of these protective dwarves on their prows.
Faience amulets. Many of his known representations are faience amulets found in Phoenician and Punic centres.
