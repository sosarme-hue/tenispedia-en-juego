world of humankind in Norse mythology

Midgard

Midgard is, in Norse mythology, the world of humankind, the inhabited realm known to people and one of the Nine Worlds. Its name, roughly «middle enclosure», places humanity at the centre of the cosmos, and its origin story shows how the Norse imagined the making of the world.

The myth

According to the myth, Midgard was made after Odin and his brothers Vili and Ve slew the primordial giant Ymir. The gods carried the corpse into the great void and shaped a habitable world from it: his flesh became the land, his blood the seas, his bones the mountains, his teeth the cliffs, and his eyebrows the wall that separated humans from the giants of Jotunheim.

Ymir's skull was held up by four dwarfs, Norðri, Suðri, Austri and Vestri, who stood for the points of the compass and formed the sky. His scattered brains became the clouds. Because the new world was still dark, the gods took sparks from Surtr's sword in Muspelheim: the largest became the Sun and Moon, and the rest became the stars.

The Sun and Moon travel across the sky in two chariots, chased by the wolves Sköll and Hati, which never catch them except during eclipses. The Sun is pulled by the white horse Skin, and the Moon by the black horse Hrim. After adding summer and winter, the gods judged their work complete and the world was ready for the first humans.

According to the Eddas, Midgard is not eternal. At Ragnarök, the final battle, the serpent Jörmungandr, also called the Midgard Serpent, will rise from the ocean and poison land and sea. The earth will sink into the water, but it will rise again green and fertile, and creation will begin anew.

Legacy

The word also had everyday uses: it appears in a Viking Age runestone poem from Fyrby, and its form survives in Danish, Swedish, Norwegian, Icelandic and Faroese. In Old English, middangeard occurs six times in the poem Beowulf, and in Middle English it developed into middellærd and middelerde. The name «Middle-earth» was popularized by J. R. R. Tolkien, an Old English scholar, in The Lord of the Rings, inspired by middangeard in the poem Crist A.

Ancient sources: The Norse Eddas describe its creation from Ymir's body and its destruction at Ragnarök; the word also appears in Beowulf, the Old High German Muspilli and the Old Saxon Heliand.

Trivia

A name with an English echo. The Germanic word for the inhabited world became middelerde in Middle English, the source of the name «Middle-earth» that Tolkien made famous.
A Viking runestone. Midgard appears in a poem on the runestone Sö 56 from Fyrby, showing the word was also used in everyday contexts, not only religious ones.
