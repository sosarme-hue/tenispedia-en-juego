five wisdom buddhas of Vajrayana Buddhism

The Five Tathāgatas

The Five Tathāgatas are five central buddhas of Mahayana and Vajrayana Buddhism, also called the five jinas or the five great buddhas. They are among the most recognizable subjects of Tibetan, Chinese and Japanese mandalas, and they explain how enlightenment appears in five complementary qualities.

The myth

According to Buddhist tradition, the Five Tathāgatas are emanations and representations of the five qualities of the Adi-Buddha, the primordial buddha associated with the dharmakaya. Some sources add a sixth buddha, Vajradhara, as the source of the five. Each occupies a cardinal point: Akshobhya in the east, Ratnasambhava in the south, Amitābha in the west, Amoghasiddhi in the north, and Vairocana at the center, although in some mandalas Akshobhya and Vairocana switch places.

Their origin lies in the Buddhist tantras. At first only two buddhas, Akshobhya and Amitābha, seemed to represent wisdom and compassion. In the Golden Light Sutra they appear under the names Dundubhisvara and Ratnaketu, which later changed to Amoghasiddhi and Ratnasambhava, while the central figure became Vairocana. Each family is linked to colours, symbols, animals and a female consort, and each presides over its own pure land.

Worship and religion

In Japan they are the main objects of realization and meditation in Shingon Buddhism, founded by Kōbō Daishi. In China they are venerated in many temples and invoked in rituals such as the Shuilu Fahui and the Yujia Yankou, as well as in prayers and chants. They appear together in the Mandala of the Two Realms, and their veneration remains alive across East Asian Buddhism.

Legacy

They are a common motif in Vajrayana mandalas, which work as concept maps for understanding the Dharma. The term «dhyani-buddhas» was first recorded in English in the early nineteenth century by Brian Houghton Hodgson, a British resident in Nepal, although it is not attested in traditional primary sources.

Ancient sources: They appear in the Golden Light Sutra (around the 5th century) under other names, and in the Sarvatathāgatatattvasaṃgraha (late 7th century) and the Vajrasekhara Sutra in their current form.

Trivia

A sixth buddha. Some sources, such as the Vajrasekhara Sutra, mention Vajradhara as a sixth buddha, seen as the source of the other five.
Swapped places. In the Guhyasamaja system, Akshobhya occupies the center of the mandala instead of Vairocana.
