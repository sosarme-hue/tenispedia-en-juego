spider trickster god of Akan folklore

Anansi

Anansi, also called Ananse, is a clever and wise spider from the Akan folklore of Ghana, whose figure spread across West Africa and the Caribbean. The protagonist of countless tales of wit and deception, his name gives its title to the whole genre of spider stories.

The myth

According to the tales, Anansi is the son of Nyame, the sky god, and Asase Ya. He acts on his father's behalf: he brings the rain that puts out fires and carries out other tasks. He may appear as a spider, as a human or as a blend of both. In his stories he outwits far more powerful rivals through cunning, though he is also greedy and selfish, and that turns his tales into warnings.

His family appears in several stories: his long-suffering wife Okonore Yaa, also known as Aso, and his sons Ntikuma, Tikelenkelen, Nankonhwea and Afudohwedohwe, each with a different physical trait. He also has a daughter, Anansewa, the heroine of tales such as the one Efua Sutherland recorded, in which Anansi sets out to find her a suitable suitor.

One of the best-known tales explains how he became the owner of all the stories in the world. Many Caribbean versions end with a proverb, and in the diaspora Anansi was reshaped without losing his essence.

Legacy

In the Caribbean, Anansi became a symbol of resistance for enslaved people, who saw in his trickery a way to survive. His figure inspired Joel Chandler Harris, who replaced Anansi with a rabbit in his tales, and Neil Gaiman, who wrote the novel Anansi Boys and made him appear in American Gods. The English rock band Skunk Anansie took its name from him, and in Cali, Colombia, the Ananse Festival of Afro cinema and video is held every year.

Trivia

A rabbit instead of a spider. Joel Chandler Harris rewrote several Anansi tales with a rabbit as the main character instead.
A proverb at the end. The Jamaican versions of these tales usually close with their own proverb, as in the story of Anansi and Brah Dead.
