Roman goddess of safety and well-being

Salus

Salus was the Roman goddess of safety, health and prosperity, for both individuals and the state. She is one of the oldest Roman goddesses and was later often identified with the Greek Hygieia.

The myth

Ancient sources present Salus mainly as a protective power of the state rather than as the protagonist of great stories. Her name, meaning safety or salvation, expresses that role. Some authors also call her Salus Semonia, which may link her to the group of gods known as the Semones, such as Semo Sancus Dius Fidius.

Over time her role widened to include personal health, and she was increasingly associated with Valetudo, the Roman name of Hygieia. She was also linked with Fortuna and Spes, although the steering oar she sometimes holds really belongs to Fortuna.

Worship and religion

Her temple stood on the Quirinal, on the hill called the Collis Salutaris, after it was voted in 304 BC during the Samnite Wars. It was dedicated on 5 August 302 BC, and the Augurium Salutis was held there each year for the preservation of the state. Her festival fell on 30 March, and her cult spread throughout Italy. Around 180 BC, sacrifices were offered to her alongside Apollo and Aesculapius.

Legacy

Her best-known image is of a woman feeding a snake, a motif that linked her to the snake of Aesculapius, an association documented on a coin from 55 BC. This iconography has lived on in medicine and in the pharmacy symbol.

Ancient sources: Ancient references to Salus appear in authors such as Macrobius, who places her at the head of a group of deities tied to agrarian cults, and in Livy, who mentions the sacrifices of around 180 BC.

Trivia

A goddess with several names. She was also known as Salus Semonia, and some scholars link this name to the Semones, an ancient group of gods.
A recent discovery. In 2020, during construction work in the port area of Mainz, a statue was found with an inscription naming the goddess as Salus.
