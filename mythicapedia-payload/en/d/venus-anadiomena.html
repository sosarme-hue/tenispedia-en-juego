representation of Aphrodite rising from the sea

Venus Anadyomene

Venus Anadyomene, or "Venus Rising from the Sea", is one of the most iconic representations of Aphrodite, the Greek goddess of love. It owes its fame to a lost painting by Apelles, and Western art returned to the subject again and again.

The myth

According to Greek mythology, Aphrodite was born as an adult woman from the sea off Paphos in Cyprus, and the sea perpetually renewed her virginity. The image captures that moment: the goddess standing and wringing out her hair as she emerges from the waves.

Apelles' painting was famous already in Antiquity. According to Pliny the Elder, it was painted for the temple of Asclepius on Kos, taken to Rome by Augustus as part of a tribute payment, and set up in the Temple of Caesar. Pliny describes it as already deteriorated, and tells the anecdote that Apelles used Campaspe, a mistress of Alexander the Great, as his model. According to Athenaeus, the idea was inspired by the courtesan Phryne, who often swam nude in the sea during the Eleusinian and Poseidonian festivals.

Legacy

The subject remained alive in Western art from Antiquity to the 19th century. Botticelli revisited it in The Birth of Venus, and Baroque and Rococo painters expanded it with cherubs, sea-nymphs and tritons around the goddess. In the 19th century Ingres, Cabanel and Bouguereau treated it, and Picasso incorporated it into Les Demoiselles d'Avignon (1907), a foundational work of Cubism.

Ancient sources: Pliny the Elder's Natural History describes Apelles' lost original painting.

Trivia

A scallop shell. Many Venus Anadyomenes include a scallop shell, which is read in some interpretations as a symbol of the female vulva.
A Venus fountain in Washington. The National Gallery of Art in Washington has a lifesize bronze of the subject, plumbed so that water drips from Venus's hair.
