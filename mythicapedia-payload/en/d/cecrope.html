Cecrops

Cecrops is, according to Greek tradition, the first legendary king of Athens and Attica, pictured with a man's upper body and a serpent's or fish's tail. He is regarded as a culture hero who taught the Athenians marriage, writing and ceremonial burial, and his story is tied to the origin of the city's name.

The myth

According to the myth, Cecrops was born from the earth itself, which made him an autochthon. His upper half was human and his lower half took the form of a serpent or fish tail, which is why he was called 'twice-born'. Some ancient writers linked this to marriage, others made him a foreigner from Sais in Egypt, and Strabo held that his name was not of Greek origin.

Cecrops married Aglaurus, daughter of Actaeus, and fathered three daughters: Aglaurus, Herse and Pandrosus. When Athena entrusted the infant Erichthonius to the three sisters in a box, they opened it and, terrified by the serpents guarding the child, threw themselves from the Acropolis. His reign, said by Eusebius to have lasted fifty years, saw Attica divided into twelve communities and laws on marriage.

In the best-known episode, Poseidon and Athena contested the patronage of the city. Athena planted an olive tree and Cecrops bore witness; the twelve gods awarded the city to her, and it took her name. Poseidon, in anger, flooded the plain and laid Attica under the sea.

Worship and religion

The Athenians raised an altar to him on the Acropolis and showed him on one of the pediments of the Parthenon. His tomb, the Cecropium, lay beside the porch of the Caryatids in the Erechtheum precinct.

Legacy

Cecrops gave his name to the Cecropia, the Acropolis's early name, and the Athenians were called Cecropidae during the reigns of the five kings who followed him.

Ancient sources: Strabo, Pausanias, Eusebius and Varro mention him and record the different traditions about his figure.

Trivia

Twice-born. The Greek epithet diphyes means 'of two natures' and was read both as his hybrid body and as a double citizenship, Greek and barbarian.
The women's vote. According to Varro, the women voted for Athena and, being one more than the men, gave her the win; Poseidon's revenge was to strip women of the vote.
