Hindu god, the Destroyer of the Trimurti and Supreme Being of Shaivism

Shiva

Shiva is one of the principal deities of Hinduism and the Supreme Being in Shaivism, one of its major traditions. Within the Trimurti he is the Destroyer who dissolves the universe and then renews it, alongside Brahma the creator and Vishnu the preserver. He is a pan-Hindu deity, widely revered in India, Nepal, Sri Lanka and Indonesia. Shiva's myth combines gentle and fearsome sides. He is the omniscient yogi who lives an ascetic life on Mount Kailash and, at the same time, the householder with his wife Parvati and their children Ganesha and Karttikeya. He is also regarded as the patron of yoga, meditation and the arts.

The myth

According to tradition, Shiva did not appear fully formed. His figure grew gradually as an amalgamation of several older Vedic and non-Vedic deities. The most visible root is Rudra, the storm god of the Rigveda, whom later texts called Shiva, meaning the auspicious one, a euphemism similar to the way the Furies in Greece were called the Eumenides. In the Puranas and the epics, Shiva appears as the creator, reproducer and dissolver of the universe.

In his best-known episodes, Shiva is an ascetic absorbed in meditation, a husband and father, or a fierce figure slaying demons. When the gods churned the ocean of milk to obtain the nectar of immortality, a poison capable of destroying the world emerged first. Shiva swallowed it to save creation, and the poison left his throat blue, which is why he is called Neelakanta. The third eye in his forehead reduces to ashes whatever it looks at when it opens. In the Shakta tradition, the goddess Devi is the creative energy and equal partner of Shiva, and in the Ardhanarisvara icon the two merge into one body, half man and half woman.

The Vaishnava and Shaiva traditions respect each other. Shaiva texts state that Vishnu is none other than Shiva, and the Mahabharata holds that Brahman, the ultimate reality, is identical to both.

Worship and religion

Shiva is usually worshipped in aniconic form as the lingam, a stone or marble monolith symbolising reproduction and regeneration linked to destruction. Originally twelve famous shiva-lingas were counted, among them Somanatha in Gujarat and Viswesvara in Benares. Today more than thirty million lingams are estimated to exist in India. His worship remains alive and forms part of the daily religious life of millions of Hindus.

Legacy

Shiva is considered the patron of yoga, meditation and the arts, and is depicted as Natarash, the king of the dance. Tradition credits him with teaching the three sources of knowledge: samkhya, which explains the structure of the world; yoga, the mastery of the subtle self through introspection; and tantra, initiatory rites and practices through which one can reach the secret nature of things.

Ancient sources: The Rigveda, where Rudra, the forerunner of Shiva, appears; the Mahabharata, the Puranas such as the Skanda Purana, the Devi Upanishad and the Devi Mahatmya also mention or praise him.

Trivia

A name meaning good omen. Shiva means auspicious or benevolent, and the name replaced Rudra, the terrible god of the Vedic texts, as a way of softening his figure.
An icon of union. The Ardhanarisvara is an image half man and half woman that blends Shiva and the goddess Shakti, and appears in many Hindu texts and temples.
