Roman goddess who personified the city and the state

Roma

Roma was the goddess who, in ancient Roman religion, personified the city and, more broadly, the Roman state. Her image expressed how Rome saw itself and helped justify its rule.

The myth

Accounts of Roma vary, since some traditions present her as a goddess and others as a heroine. In one, she was a Trojan captive who travelled with Odysseus and Aeneas toward Italy from the land of the Molossians. Weary of the journey, she urged the Trojan women to burn the ships near the Tiber. The settlers then established themselves on the Palatine, were well received by the local people and prospered, and chose to honour her by giving her name to the city.

Other traditions made her a daughter of Ascanius, a wife of Aeneas, a daughter of Italus and Leucaria, or a daughter of Evander. Some encyclopedias also name her a daughter of Aesculapius. Roman literature later counted her among the great mothers of the Roman people, alongside Venus and Cybele.

Worship and religion

Her official cult spread mainly through the Eastern provinces. The earliest certain cult was founded at Smyrna around 195 BC, and in 189 BC Delphi and Lycia instituted festivals in her honour. Her priesthood was male, perhaps reflecting the virility of Rome's military power. After Julius Caesar's death, the imperial cult linked her to the emperors as consort and protector, and Hadrian dedicated a great temple to her as Roma Aeterna in Rome. No living cult survives today.

Legacy

Her seated figure with shield and spear later shaped the depiction of Britannia, the personification of Britain. She appears frequently on coins, reliefs, arches and statues.

Ancient sources: The Greek poet Melinno wrote a hymn to her in Sapphic stanzas, calling her the daughter of Ares.

In art

Trivia

A coin from Locri. A silver coin from around 275 BC, minted at Locri, shows a seated woman with a shield and weapons labelled Roma.
