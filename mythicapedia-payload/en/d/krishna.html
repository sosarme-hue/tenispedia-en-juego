Hindu god of love and protection, eighth avatar of Vishnu

Krishna

Krishna is one of the most beloved deities in Hinduism, worshipped as the eighth avatar of Vishnu and, in some traditions, as the Supreme God in his own right. Associated with love, tenderness and protection, he appears in central texts such as the Mahabharata and the Bhagavad Gita.

The myth

According to tradition, Krishna was born in Mathura to Devaki and Vasudeva, but was raised by Nanda and Yashoda in Gokul to escape his maternal uncle, the tyrant Kamsa. The stories of his childhood, known as Krishna Lila, tell how he stole butter, enchanted the gopis with his flute, lifted Govardhan Hill to shelter his community from devastating rains, and defeated demons such as Putana and Sakatasura.

As an adult he defeated Kamsa, restored order in Mathura and founded the city of Dvaraka. In the Mahabharata he sided with the Pandavas and served as Arjuna's charioteer in the Kurukshetra War, where he delivers the Bhagavad Gita on duty, dharma and the meaning of action. His teachings state that he manifests whenever righteousness declines, to protect the virtuous and punish the wicked.

The narratives give him a large circle of consorts, among them Rukmini and Satyabhama, and sons such as Samba and Pradyumna. According to tradition, his end comes when he leaves the earth, although accounts differ between texts.

Worship and religion

Krishna is a pan-Indian god and his worship is practised across the subcontinent. His birth is celebrated each year at Krishna Janmashtami, in late August or early September. He is especially revered in Vrindavan, Mathura, Dwarka, Mayapur and Puri, where his Jagannatha form has a famous temple. He is also worshipped in many regional forms, such as Vithoba in Pandharpur or Guruvayoorappan in Kerala. His worship remains alive and is part of the faith of millions of Hindus.

Legacy

Krishna's stories have inspired classical dance forms such as Bharatanatyam, Kathakali, Kuchipudi, Odissi and Manipuri. His iconography, with the flute and the peacock feather, is among the most recognisable in Indian art. In the twentieth century his devotion reached the West largely through ISKCON, founded by Bhaktivedanta Swami.

Ancient sources: He appears in the Mahabharata, the Bhagavata Purana, the Brahma Vaivarta Purana and the Bhagavad Gita, and in many other Hindu philosophical and mythological texts.

Trivia

Name of colour. His name means 'black' or 'dark', which is why he is often depicted with dark or blue skin, although tradition describes him as dark-complexioned.
A hundred and eight names. A traditional hymn, the Sri Krisna astottara-shata namavali, lists 108 names of Krishna.
