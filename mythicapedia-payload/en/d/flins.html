Flins

Flins is a supposed god of death and resurrection attributed to the Slavic peoples of Lusatia, but it is an invention of chroniclers from the late Middle Ages and the Renaissance. Its story matters because it shows how a legend arose that still gives its name to a place and a tourism brand today.

The myth

According to the Cronecken der Sassen (1492), attributed to the goldsmith Konrad Bothe, Flins was a stone idol shaped like a dead man or a skeleton, holding a torch in one hand and carrying a lion on its shoulder. The lion would wake the dead with its roars, and the glowing stone symbolized resurrection.

In the sixteenth century, Christophorus Manlius expanded the tale: according to him, the idol was toppled by Christian troops in 1106 near Bautzen and thrown into the River Spree, but pagans rescued it, hid it on the Hochstein and finally moved it to a cave in the Jizera Mountains.

Worship and religion

The chronicle claims that warriors venerated it through a roughly carved stone, and that its cult was linked to a peak said to have given Flinsberg its name. No archaeological excavation has uncovered shrines, altars or remains connected with this god.

Legacy

The place-name actually comes from the Middle High German vlins, meaning flint or hard rock. After 1945, Polish historiography used the myth to reinforce the region's Slavic identity. Today Flins lends its name to hotels, craft beers and tourism campaigns in Świeradów-Zdrój.

Ancient sources: The Cronecken der Sassen (1492) by Konrad Bothe and De idolo Lusatiorum deiecto Flyns (1570) by Christophorus Manlius, the two sources behind the myth.

Trivia

A name with a geological root. The place-name Flinsberg comes from the Middle High German vlins, meaning flint or hard rock, a word used by local miners and woodcutters.
Another invented deity. Konrad Bothe also invented Krodo, a supposed Germanic or Saxon idol, showing that his accounts of pagan idols were fabrications.
