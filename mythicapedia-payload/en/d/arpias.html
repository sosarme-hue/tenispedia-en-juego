Harpies

Harpies are creatures of Greek and Roman mythology, half woman and half bird, often seen as personifications of violent storm winds. They matter because they embody sudden disappearance and theft, and they inspired poets, painters and writers for centuries.

The myth

According to Hesiod, the harpies were originally two sisters, Aello ("storm swift") and Ocypete ("swift wing"), daughters of Thaumas and the Oceanid Electra and sisters of Iris. Homer knows only one, Podarge ("fleet-foot"), while Virgil names one Celaeno. Later writers group them as three, and the names vary from source to source.

The most celebrated story involves Phineus, a Thracian seer punished by the gods. The harpies snatched food from his hands or fouled it so he could never eat. When the Argonauts arrived, Phineus promised to advise them on their voyage if they freed him from the creatures. The winged Boreads, Calais and Zetes, chased the harpies through the air. According to one tradition, one harpy fell into the river Tigris, which was renamed Harpys, and the other reached the Strophades, named after Aello's turn (strophe). Other accounts say the Boreads died, or that Iris or Hermes persuaded them to spare the harpies.

In the Odyssey, harpies carry off people and hand them over to the Erinyes, and Pherecydes places them as guardians of Tartarus. Roman and Byzantine writers stressed their ugliness, while Greek pottery often shows them as beautiful winged women.

Legacy

Dante places the harpies in the wood of the suicides in the seventh ring of Hell in the Divine Comedy. Ariosto uses them in Orlando Furioso, and William Blake drew on Dante for one of his works. In Shakespeare's The Tempest, Ariel stages a banquet scene that recalls the one in the Aeneid. The harpy eagle, a real American bird, takes its name from the monster. In everyday speech, "harpy" describes a shrewish or nasty woman, as when Benedick uses it of Beatrice in Much Ado About Nothing.

Ancient sources: Hesiod mentions them in the Theogony, Homer in the Iliad and Odyssey, and Virgil in the Aeneid, where Celaeno prophesies to the Trojans.

Trivia

A name meaning snatchers. The Greek name Harpyia means something like "snatchers" or "swift robbers", reflecting their role of carrying things off.
A river named after one. According to tradition, one of the harpies fell into the Tigris, which was therefore said to have been called Harpys.
