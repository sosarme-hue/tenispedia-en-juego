Ubume

Ubume are yōkai, the ghosts of women who died in childbirth, sometimes leaving a child behind still alive. They belong to Japanese folklore and are depicted as unsettling figures who ask passersby to hold their baby.

The myth

According to tradition, a woman who dies while pregnant, or in labor, leaving her child helpless, may return as an ubume. She usually appears as a pale woman in white garments, stained with blood below the waist, with long, tangled hair. She appears beside roads or riverbanks and asks a passerby to hold her child for a moment. If the person agrees, the apparition vanishes, and the bundle grows heavier and heavier until it turns out to be a stone, a statue of Jizō, a burial tower or a pile of dry leaves.

If the traveller runs away, he may fall ill with a fever that can prove fatal. In some tales, whoever helps receives extraordinary strength: one man washed his face and wrung out a towel, which tore in two and then in four, and he later became a powerful sumo wrestler. Other versions say the ubume tries to steal other people's babies or to nurse the child she carries, which explains her alternative name, the bird of mother's milk.

Legacy

Toriyama Sekien included her in his illustrated catalogue of yōkai, the Gazu hyakki yagyō (1776), where she stands by a river, wrapped from the waist down in a bloodstained cloth. That image is among the best-known depictions in the Japanese bestiary of ghosts.

Ancient sources: The Konjaku monogatarishū (12th century) tells of a samurai who, helping a woman by a river, finds that the baby turns into a bundle of leaves. The Kokon hyakumonogatari hyōban, the Kii zōdan shū (1687) and the Wakan sansai zue also mention her.

Trivia

Laundry left out overnight. According to the Kii zōdan shū, the ubume flies at night and leaves bloodstains on children's clothes, so people avoided leaving garments hanging out overnight.
A link with China. Japanese scholars equated her with the Chinese guhuoniao, a bird-like woman, and Ibaraki has a similar legend about the ubametori.
