Assyrian god of agriculture, later a demon

Nisroch

Nisroch is a deity linked to Assyria in the Hebrew Bible, whose name is most likely a scribal error for Nimrod. Over time he became a demon in European demonology.

The myth

The Bible tells that the Assyrian king Sennacherib was murdered by two of his sons while worshipping in the temple of Nisroch, an Assyrian sanctuary.

The Talmud offers another explanation: the name would come from the Hebrew word neser, the name of a wooden plank that Sennacherib supposedly found on his return from Judah. According to the sages, that plank had been part of Noah's Ark, and the king worshipped it as an idol.

Mesopotamian sources never mention a god called Nisroch. For this reason, most scholars think the name is a transcription error for Nimrod. Some researchers have proposed identifying him with Ninurta, god of agriculture, or with Nusku, god of fire, although these suggestions remain disputed.

Worship and religion

There is no evidence of a cult to Nisroch in Mesopotamian sources. His only link to religious practice is the Assyrian temple where, according to the Bible, Sennacherib worshipped him. If he were Ninurta, the sanctuary would have been the one at Kalhu.

Legacy

In the sixteenth century, the Dutch demonologist Johann Weyer listed him in his Pseudomonarchia Daemonum (1577) as the "chief cook" of Hell. John Milton made him one of Satan's demons in Book VI of Paradise Lost. In the nineteenth century, the archaeologist Austen Henry Layard called the winged, eagle-headed genies he unearthed at Kalhu "Nisrochs", and in 1906 Edith Nesbit turned him into an eagle-headed guide in The Story of the Amulet.

Ancient sources: The Hebrew Bible, in the Second Book of Kings, mentions him in the account of Sennacherib's murder, and the Talmud connects him with the plank from Noah's Ark.

Trivia

A wooden plank. The Talmud connects the name with neser, a plank that, according to the sages, had been part of Noah's Ark and that Sennacherib worshipped as an idol.
Misattributed sculptures. Austen Henry Layard called the eagle-headed winged figures he found at Kalhu "Nisrochs". Today specialists describe them as griffin-demons.
