Roman goddess of peace

Pax

Pax is the Roman personification of peace, said to be the daughter of Jupiter and Justice. Often linked with spring, she rose to prominence under Augustus, who used her image to legitimise a new order after the civil wars.

The myth

According to tradition, Pax was the daughter of Jupiter and Justice, and sister of Concordia and Disciplina. She was shown as a young woman crowned with olive, holding a cornucopia, a sceptre and a phiale, symbols of abundance and order.

Pax was barely important in the early days of Rome, when the pantheon relied on warlike deities such as Mars and Juno. Her Greek counterpart, Eirene, had been worshipped since the Bronze Age and gained prominence during Athens' rise and the Peloponnesian War. For the Romans, peace (pax, from pacisci, 'to make a pact') was a settlement that ended a war and often meant submission to Rome, rather than simply the absence of fighting.

Under Augustus, Pax became the emblem of the end of the civil wars. She was recognised as a goddess, and the Ara Pacis was dedicated to her in the Campus Martius. Vespasian later built her a temple after the chaos of the Year of the Four Emperors.

Worship and religion

Her festivals were held on 30 January and 30 March. Besides the Ara Pacis, she had an altar in the Forum of Peace, and Augustus tried to spread her cult to provinces such as Spain and Gaul. With the rise of Christianity and the invasions of the 4th and 5th centuries, her worship disappeared entirely.

Legacy

Her name entered Latin and modern languages as a symbol of peace. The period of stability in the empire is known as the Pax Romana, a term coined by later scholars.

Ancient sources: Ancient sources mentioning her are few and rely largely on Augustan evidence such as coins and the Ara Pacis.
