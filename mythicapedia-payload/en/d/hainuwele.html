Hainuwele

Hainuwele, 'the Coconut Girl', is a figure from the Wemale and Alune folklore of Seram Island in the Maluku Islands, Indonesia. Her story is an origin myth explaining the arrival of tuber crops.

The myth

A hunter named Ameta found a coconut caught on a wild boar's tusk and planted it. Cutting his finger while climbing the tree, his blood fell on a blossom, and nine days later a girl appeared there whom he named Hainuwele. She grew quickly, and valuable objects came out of her body, which she handed out at a dance instead of areca nuts.

Jealous, the men buried her alive on the last night. Ameta searched for her, learned what had happened through an oracle and dug up her body, which he cut into pieces and buried around the village. From these remains grew useful plants, including the tubers that have fed the people ever since.

Worship and religion

Jensen linked the myth to the Wemale maro dance, a ritual in which, in his view, many of its elements appear.

Legacy

Joseph Campbell first retold her story for English-speaking readers in The Masks of God, and Adolf E. Jensen used her case to introduce the concept of the Dema deity into ethnology.

Trivia

A meaningful name. Hainuwele means 'coconut branch', a name that recalls the tree from which the tradition says she was born.
Two human groups. According to the story, those who passed through Satene's spiral gate divided into the Patalima and Patasiwa, groups linked to the tribal divisions of the area.
