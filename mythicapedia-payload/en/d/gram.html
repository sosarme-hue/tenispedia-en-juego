Sigurd's sword in Norse mythology

Gram

Gram is, in Norse mythology, the magical sword with which the hero Sigurd killed the dragon Fafnir. Tied to the Völsung saga, it is one of the best-known weapons in the Germanic tradition.

The myth

Tradition holds that Völundr, the magical smith, forged the sword, and that the god Odin thrust it into the Barnstokkr tree in the hall of a wedding feast, promising it to whoever could pull it out. After many failed attempts, only Sigmund managed it. He used the sword in many battles until Odin shattered it during his final fight against King Lyngvi.

Hjördis, Sigmund's wife, kept the two halves for their son Sigurd. Raised by the dwarf smith Regin, Sigurd was to slay the dragon Fafnir, who guarded a treasure. Regin's first two swords broke at the first blow. For the third attempt, Sigurd handed him the pieces of Gram, and the reforged blade split an anvil in two. To test its edge, he let a tuft of wool drift downstream against it and watched it slice through. With the sword he killed Fafnir with one deep thrust, then avenged his father by slaying Lyngvi.

In the Völsunga saga, Gram was placed between Sigurd and Brynhild on their funeral pyre as a sign of chastity, and afterwards the sword vanishes from the narrative. In the Nibelungenlied the hero's sword is called Balmung.

Legacy

Richard Wagner called it Nothung, meaning 'the needful one', in his opera cycle Der Ring des Nibelungen, where the smith Mime fails to reforge it and Siegfried succeeds. Its name later appeared in many video games, series and trading cards. It is often noted that the story of a broken and reforged sword inspired Tolkien's Narsil in The Lord of the Rings.

Ancient sources: The Norse Völsunga saga is the main source, along with the Thidrekssaga and the medieval Nibelungenlied.

Trivia

Meaning of the name. Gram is an old Germanic adjective related to the word grim, meaning angry or fierce.
Parallel with Excalibur. Like Excalibur, Gram is fixed in a tree and only the destined hero can pull it free, in this case Sigmund.
