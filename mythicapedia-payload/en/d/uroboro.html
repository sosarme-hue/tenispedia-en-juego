Ouroboros

The ouroboros is a snake or dragon biting its own tail, forming a circle. Originating in ancient Egypt and later adopted by Greek magical tradition, Gnosticism and alchemy, it stands for the eternal cycle of life, death and rebirth, and for the unity of all things.

The myth

One of the earliest known motifs appears in the Enigmatic Book of the Netherworld, an ancient Egyptian funerary text found in the tomb of Tutankhamun, dating to the 14th century BCE. Two serpents holding their tails in their mouths surround a large divine figure that probably represents the union of Ra and Osiris, the sun reborn. Both serpents were seen as manifestations of Mehen, who protects Ra on his journey through the underworld, and the whole figure stood for the beginning and end of time.

In Norse mythology, the serpent Jörmungandr, one of the children of Loki and Angrboda, grows so large that it can encircle the world and grip its tail in its teeth. In the legends of Ragnar Lodbrok, a small lindworm given to a girl named Þóra grows until it coils around her bower and bites its own tail; Ragnar kills it and then marries her. Indigenous peoples of South America's tropical lowlands speak of an anaconda encircling the waters at the edge of the world.

In India, the Aitareya Brahmana, an early Vedic text, compares Vedic rituals to a snake biting its own tail. The serpent Shesha rests coiled over the cosmic ocean beside Vishnu, and the Yoga-kundalini Upanishad describes Kundalini as a serpent coiled with its tail in its mouth at the base of the body.

Worship and religion

In Egypt the symbol was used in funerary contexts, and in Roman times it often appeared on magical talismans, sometimes alongside other emblems. The 4th-century Latin commentator Servius knew of its Egyptian use and read it as the cyclical nature of the year. Shesha and Kundalini remain part of living Hindu traditions.

Legacy

In alchemy the ouroboros is one of the most characteristic emblems. The Chrysopoeia of Cleopatra the Alchemist shows it enclosing the words hen to pan, 'the all is one', and it is often linked to the philosopher's stone. Carl Jung saw it as an archetype and a basic alchemical mandala. The German chemist August Kekulé later said that a vision of snakes seizing their own tails helped him work out the ring structure of benzene. The ouroboros also appears on the seal of the Theosophical Society, founded in 1875, and in cosmology: Martin Rees used it to illustrate the scales of the universe, from the subatomic to the supragalactic.

Ancient sources: The Egyptian Enigmatic Book of the Netherworld, the Vedic Aitareya Brahmana, the Gnostic Pistis Sophia, Cleopatra's Chrysopoeia and the medieval Yoga-kundalini Upanishad all mention or depict it.

Trivia

Half white, half black. In Cleopatra's Chrysopoeia, the ouroboros is divided into a white half and a black half. Some have related this duality to Gnostic thought or to the Taoist yin and yang.
A son with a snake in his eye. In the legends of Ragnar Lodbrok, his son Sigurd is born with the image of a white snake encircling the iris of one eye and biting its own tail. For this reason he is named Sigurd Snake-in-the-Eye.
