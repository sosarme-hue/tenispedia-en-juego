Italic goddess of the woods, springs and liberty

Feronia

Feronia was an Italic goddess of woodlands, springs and untamed nature, worshipped by Etruscans, Sabines and Romans. Her link with liberty made her a protector of freed slaves.

The myth

According to tradition, Feronia was a goddess of springs and woods, especially wild ones far from human settlements. In Virgil's Aeneid, troops from her grove fight for Turnus against Aeneas. The Arcadian king Evander recalls that in his youth he killed Erulus, a son of Feronia who had a triple body like Geryon, and had to kill him three times.

Stories about her sanctuary at Terracina stress her wild character. According to Servius, a fire destroyed her grove, but the burnt wood turned green at once when locals tried to carry off her statue. Pliny says that towers built near the sanctuary in wartime were always destroyed by lightning.

Worship and religion

Her shrines stood in remote places, often beside springs. Strabo describes an annual rite in her grove below Mount Soracte, where participants walked barefoot over glowing embers without being burned. Freed slaves visited her temple to receive the cap that marked their new status, and first fruits of the harvest were offered to her. Her festival, the Feroniae, fell on November 13 during the Plebeian Games. No living cult survives, though in the 19th century Charles Godfrey Leland recorded traditions of a 'witch' named Feronia in Tuscany.

Legacy

The asteroid 72 Feronia is named after her. Her name comes from Latin ferus, 'wild', related to the English word feral. Dumézil compared her with the Vedic god Rudra, both linked to what has not yet been tamed.

Ancient sources: Varro, Servius, Strabo, Pliny, Livy and Virgil's Aeneid.

Trivia

Cap of freedom. Newly freed slaves could go to the Terracina shrine and receive on their shaved heads the pileus, a hat that symbolized their liberty.
Grove that turns green. According to Servius, after a fire destroyed her grove, the burnt wood sprouted again as soon as people tried to move her statue.
