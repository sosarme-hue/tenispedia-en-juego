Edo-period yōkai shaped like a blob of flesh

Nuppeppō

The nuppeppō is a yōkai from Japan's Edo period, depicted as a shapeless blob of flesh with wrinkles that cannot be told apart on its face and body. Its name comes from a derogatory slang term for a woman who wears far too much make-up.

The myth

Illustrated scrolls say little about it beyond its name and picture. The 1781 sharebon Shingoza Dehōdai Mōgyū adds that it sucks the fat of the dead and that in the past it came disguised as a doctor.

It is said to be passive and almost harmless. It wanders aimlessly through deserted streets, graveyards and abandoned temples, mostly at night near the end of the year. Its body odour rivals that of rotting meat, and its appearance is what alarms people most. According to one tradition, it first poses as a human, approaches someone in a friendly way and, once they let their guard down, reveals its true form.

Legacy

In Shōwa and Heisei-era literature it was placed near abandoned temples, based on a line by folklorist Morihiko Fujisawa, though it is suspected that he invented this, inspired by a background drawing in the Gazu Hyakki Yagyō.

Ancient sources: Edo-period illustrated scrolls such as the Gazu Hyakki Yagyō, the 1781 sharebon Shingoza Dehōdai Mōgyū, and the Chinese text Bái Zé Tú.

Trivia

A visitor at Sunpu. In 1609 a blob of flesh about the size of a small child appeared in the courtyard of Sunpu Castle. Tokugawa Ieyasu ordered it driven out, and the servants took it into the mountains.
A lost panacea. A scholar of pharmaceutics identified it with the Feng of old Chinese texts. He regretted not capturing it, since its flesh was considered a panacea that granted great power.
Like Frankenstein. It is sometimes described as built from the flesh of dead humans, in a way similar to Frankenstein's monster.
