youngest son of Priam in the Trojan War

Polydorus

Polydorus is the youngest son of Priam, king of Troy, and one of the most memorable victims of the Trojan War. His story belongs to the Greek tradition and varies considerably between sources, which makes him a good example of how fluid myths can be.

The myth

According to Homer, in the Iliad, Polydorus was the youngest son of Priam, born to Laothoe. His father would not let him fight, but the young man raced swiftly along the battle lines, and Achilles killed him with a spear. His death provoked the fury of Hector.

Other traditions, such as Euripides' tragedy Hecuba, make Hecuba his mother and tell how Priam sent him to Thrace with gold, under the protection of King Polymestor. After the fall of Troy, Polymestor killed him to keep the treasure. Hecuba, now a slave, uncovers the crime and avenges him by blinding the king and killing his sons. According to Hyginus, his sister Iliona raised him as her own son while her true son Deipylus died by mistake; after consulting the oracle, Polydorus learned the truth and took revenge on Polymestor.

In the Aeneid, Virgil tells how Aeneas, arriving in Thrace, uprooted a bleeding bush: it was the soul of Polydorus, who warned him to flee that land.

Legacy

His name was given to the asteroid 4708 Polydoros, a Jovian asteroid.

Ancient sources: Homer's Iliad, Euripides' Hecuba, Ovid's Metamorphoses and Virgil's Aeneid.

Trivia

A bush that speaks. In the Aeneid, the spears that killed Polydorus took root in the ground and turned into plants, so the bush bleeds when uprooted.
A ghost opens the play. In Euripides' Hecuba, the ghost of Polydorus delivers the prologue and explains who killed him and why.
