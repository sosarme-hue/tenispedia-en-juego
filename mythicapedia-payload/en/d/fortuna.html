Roman goddess of luck

Fortuna

Fortuna is the Roman goddess of luck and chance events, believed to bring both good and bad fortune. Her cult took root in Rome, and her image of the turning wheel survived in European culture for centuries.

The myth

In Roman tradition, Fortuna distributed good and bad fortune according to her whim, with no visible reason. She was considered a daughter of Jupiter, although no canonical mythical genealogy exists: an inscription in the sanctuary at Praeneste presents her this way, and one statue shows her being nursed by Juno.

Ancient authors described her as bald, blind, winged at the feet, standing on a wheel or globe, and holding a rudder or a cornucopia. Because of her unpredictability she was called the most capricious goddess of Olympus. Her darker side was marked with epithets such as Fortuna Dubia, Fortuna Brevis and Fortuna Mala, and in Rome she was also invoked as Fortuna Populi Romani, protector of the people.

According to Cicero and Sallust, good fortune depended on virtue: a man who lacked it attracted misfortune upon himself and upon Rome. Her name was also linked to the ancient Italic Vortumna, "she who turns the year".

Worship and religion

Roman writers disagreed on who introduced her cult to the city, whether Servius Tullius or Ancus Marcius. She had a temple in the Forum Boarium and a public sanctuary on the Quirinal, and the oracle at Praeneste used oak rods with various futures written on them, from which a small boy drew one at random. Her main festival, Fors Fortuna, was held around midsummer, with some sources placing it on 11 June and others on 24 June. As Annonaria she protected grain supplies, and she was also regarded as a promoter of motherhood.

Legacy

Boethius, in his Consolation of Philosophy, presented her as an instrument of divine will, and her wheel, with figures who rise, reign, fall and are left without a kingdom, became a common motif throughout the Middle Ages. Dante mentions her in the Inferno, and in Italy the saying "Luck is blind" remains alive in everyday speech.

Ancient sources: Ovid, in his letters from exile, describes her unsteady wheel; Seneca invokes her in his tragedy Agamemnon, and Cicero and Sallust mention her in their works.

Trivia

A wheel with four stages. The medieval Wheel of Fortune showed four figures labelled "I shall reign", "I reign", "I have reigned" and "I have no kingdom", representing rise and fall.
A boy and the oak rods. At the oracle of Praeneste, a small boy picked at random one of the oak rods bearing written predictions.
