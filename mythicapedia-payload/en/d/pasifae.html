Pasiphaë

Pasiphaë is a figure of Greek mythology, daughter of Helios and wife of King Minos of Crete. She is best known for her union with a bull, which gave birth to the Minotaur, and for the role she plays in Greek tragedy and Roman poetry.

The myth

According to the tradition, Minos claimed the favour of the gods as king of Crete. While making a sacrifice to Poseidon, he asked for a bull to rise from the sea, promising to sacrifice it, but when a magnificent animal appeared he swapped it for an inferior one. Poseidon, angered, made Pasiphaë feel an irresistible passion for that bull. Other versions blame Aphrodite, who was neglected by Pasiphaë, or even Zeus disguised as a bull.

Pasiphaë asked Daedalus for help, and he built a hollow wooden cow covered with real cowhide. Inside it, the queen mated with the bull, and from that union the Minotaur was born, a creature with a bull's head and a human body. To contain him, Daedalus built the labyrinth of Crete. In Euripides' lost play The Cretans, Pasiphaë defends herself before Minos as a victim of a madness sent by a god, and she places the real blame on the king.

Later traditions also present her as a sorceress who cursed Minos so that any woman who lay with him died. Procris offered him a remedy that let him lie with her safely. Ancient sources differ about her parentage: Diodorus gives Crete as her mother, while Plutarch makes Atlas her father.

Worship and religion

On mainland Greece, Pasiphaë was worshipped as an oracular goddess at Thalamae, near Sparta. Pausanias describes a small shrine beside a clear stream, flanked by bronze statues of Helios and Pasiphaë. According to Cicero, the Spartan ephors slept at her shrine to receive prophetic dreams that would guide their rule. There is no evidence that her cult survives today.

Legacy

Virgil places her among the sinful lovers in the Mournful Fields in the Aeneid, and mentions her in his Eclogue VI. Ovid portrays her in the Ars Amatoria with satirical wit, showing her jealous of the cows and eager to kill her rivals. Her name is linked to unrestrained passion and appears in lists of women ruled by desire, alongside Phaedra, Byblis, Myrrha, Scylla and Semiramis.

Ancient sources: Euripides (The Cretans), Apollodorus, Diodorus Siculus, Plutarch, Pausanias, Cicero, Ovid (Ars Amatoria), Virgil (Aeneid and Eclogues).

Trivia

A wooden cow. Daedalus built a hollow cow-shaped device covered with real hide so that the bull would approach Pasiphaë. This deception is central to the myth.
Dreams for governing. According to Cicero, the Spartan ephors spent the night in Pasiphaë's shrine to receive prophetic dreams that guided their political decisions.
