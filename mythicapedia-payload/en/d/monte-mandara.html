legendary mountain in Hindu mythology

Mount Mandara

Mount Mandara is a legendary mountain in Hindu mythology, described in the Puranas as the rod with which gods and demons churned the Ocean of Milk. Its role in that episode and its link to Shiva make it one of the most important mythical places in Hinduism.

The myth

According to the Samudra Manthana, or Churning of the Ocean, the devas and asuras wanted to obtain the amrita, the nectar of immortality, which lay in the depths of the primal ocean. They used Mount Mandara as the churning rod. Since they could not move it on their own, the celestial serpent Ananta helped carry it to the ocean.

The serpent Vasuki served as the rope: the asuras pulled its head and the devas its tail. As the mountain threatened to sink into the ocean floor, Vishnu took his second avatar, the tortoise Kurma, and served as a pivot beneath it. The churning produced fourteen treasures, among them the goddess Lakshmi, the horse Uchchaihshravas, the white elephant Airavata and Soma, the moon. Finally the vessel of amrita appeared, but so did the deadly Kalakuta poison, which Shiva drank to protect the universe.

Worship and religion

Mount Mandara is considered a residence of Shiva and Parvati, a site of intense spirituality. In the stories, Shiva playfully addresses Parvati on the mountain and inspires her to practise tapas, while Mandara itself performed austerities for five thousand divine years to win Shiva's favour. In South India, tortoise-shaped lamp bases and wedding platforms (kalyana mandapams) evoke the stability the mountain provided to the world.

Legacy

The episode is a common motif in Hindu and Buddhist art in India and Southeast Asia. The great 12th-century bas-relief at Angkor Wat in Cambodia shows hundreds of gods and demons pulling Vasuki, and Khmer art repeated the scene often, perhaps because their creation myth involved a naga ancestor. The Indologist Heinrich Zimmer read it as an image of the universe's cycles: creation, destruction and renewed creation.

Ancient sources: The Puranas, such as the Bhagavata, the Skandapurana and the Narada Purana, and the Mahabharata, which calls it the best of mountains; the Yoga Upanishads read the churning as a lesson in meditation.

Trivia

Angkor Wat relief. The 12th-century relief at Angkor Wat is the most famous depiction of the churning and forms part of this Cambodian temple's gallery.
Sirah Kentjong. In East Java, archaeological remains show the churning as part of hydraulic systems, with water gushing from the mountain peak.
