folkloric figure who trades baby teeth for coins

Tooth fairy

The tooth fairy is a folkloric figure of early childhood in Western and Western-influenced cultures. She collects the baby teeth children leave under their pillow and replaces them with a small payment, and she is the English-speaking counterpart of Spain's Ratoncito Pérez.

The myth

According to the folklore, when a child loses a baby tooth, it should be placed under the pillow or on the bedside table. While the child sleeps, the fairy visits and swaps the tooth for a small payment. The amount varies by country, family finances and what a child's classmates receive.

The figure takes many forms. The most common is a Tinkerbell-like fairy with wings and a wand, but she may also appear as a winged child, a pixie, a mouse, a bear or a little flying man.

Medieval England had its own tooth customs: children were told to burn their baby teeth to avoid searching for them in the afterlife, and people feared that a witch who obtained one could gain power over its owner. The earliest known record of the tradition itself, however, is a 1908 'Household Hints' item in the Chicago Daily Tribune.

Legacy

The figure has found its way into everyday life and commerce. Since 2011 the Royal Canadian Mint has sold special sets with a tooth fairy theme, and in 2020 the Royal Australian Mint issued 'Tooth Fairy Kits' with commemorative $2 coins. The phrase 'believing in the tooth fairy' describes an adult who is too gullible. Author Vicki Lansky advises parents to tell children that the fairy pays more for a healthy tooth than for one with decay, a way of encouraging good dental care.

Trivia

Payments that change. In the United States, the average payout per tooth rose from $1.30 in 1998 to $6.23 in 2023, although it fell to $5.01 between 2024 and 2025. The first tooth lost usually earns more than the later ones.
Throwing the tooth. In Japan, an upper lost tooth is thrown down to the ground and a lower one up into the air, so that the new tooth will grow in straight.
