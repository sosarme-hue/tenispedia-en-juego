Irish god of healing

Dian Cecht

Dian Cecht is the Irish god of healing and one of the Tuatha Dé Danann, the divine people of Irish Celtic mythology. He matters because he combines medicine, the magic of remedies and the family conflicts that run through the stories of his people.

The myth

According to tradition, Dian Cecht made a silver arm for King Nuada, who had lost his own arm in the first Battle of Magh Tuireadh, allowing him to keep ruling. After Nuada was replaced by Bres, a half-Fomorian tyrant, the people turned against Bres. He then asked Miach, Dian Cecht's son, for an arm of flesh and blood that would restore him to power. This feat roused his father's envy, and Dian Cecht ended up killing Miach.

Miach's sister Airmed mourned at her brother's grave, and from her tears and the earth, all the healing herbs of the world sprang up. Airmed sorted and catalogued them, but Dian Cecht, again driven by anger, scattered them and destroyed her work. The story says that no human now knows the properties of every plant.

Another tale tells how Dian Cecht killed three serpents found in the heart of the infant son of the Morrígan. He burned them and threw the ashes into a river, which boiled and gave the River Barrow its name. Sources disagree about his parentage: the Dindsenchas makes him a son of the Dagda, while the Book of Invasions lists several children, including Cu, Cethen, Cian, father of Lugh, and Miach.

Worship and religion

During the Second Battle of Magh Tuireadh he treated the wounded by soaking them in the healing well of Sláine, at Achad Abla. Nearby he gathered medicinal herbs at Lusmag and chanted spells with his sons Miach and Octriuil and his daughter Airmed. He boasted that he could heal anyone except those who had been beheaded. There is no record of a cult that survived to the present day.

Legacy

His name appears in the St. Gall incantations, which invoke a salve he left with his family. The early legal text Bretha Déin Chécht, on injuries and their compensation, is attributed to him, and the Senchas Már lists him among pre-Christian authors whose judgements were accepted.

Ancient sources: The Book of Invasions (Lebor Gabála Érenn), the Dindsenchas, Tochmarc Étaíne and the St. Gall incantations.

Trivia

The river that boils. The River Barrow is named after the ashes of the serpents Dian Cecht burned. According to the story, the water boiled and killed every living creature in it.
Scattered herbs. According to the story of Airmed, his jealousy meant that the full knowledge of medicinal plants was lost.
