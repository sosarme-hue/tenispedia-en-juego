bridge of judgement in Zoroastrianism

Chinvat Bridge

The Chinvat Bridge (Cinvatô Peretûm) is, in Persian Zoroastrianism, the bridge that separates the world of the living from the world of the dead. Every soul must cross it after death, and the crossing decides each soul's fate.

The myth

According to Zoroastrian tradition, the bridge is guarded by two four-eyed dogs, a motif that recalls Yama, the ruler of the underworld in Hindu tradition, who also has two such dogs. The bridge's appearance depends on the asha, or righteousness, of the person who sees it. If the person has been wicked, the bridge narrows until it is as thin as a knife's edge, and a demon drags the soul into the House of Lies (druj-demana), a place of punishment and suffering. If good thoughts, words and deeds prevailed in life, the bridge widens enough to be crossed.

In that case, Daena, a beautiful maiden who represents revelation and the deceased's good deeds, receives and guides the soul. A divine court made up of Mithra (covenant), Sraosha (obedience) and Rashnu (justice), assisted by Arshtat (justice), grants it the status of a righteous soul and leads it to the House of Song, paradise. Souls that cross successfully are united with Ahura Mazda. According to the Bundahishn, punishment is not eternal: after the final renewal of the universe (Frashokereti), condemned souls are purified and freed.

Legacy

Some scholars have identified the bridge with the rainbow or the Milky Way, an interpretation rejected by C. F. Keary and Ferdinand Justi, who describe it as straight rather than curved. The concept is compared to the Islamic As-Sirat. In visual culture, bridges have been identified on early medieval Sogdian funerary couches, one of them found at Xi'an. In literature, it appears in Dimitris Lyacos's Poena Damni trilogy and in Charles Olson's Maximus Poems. In Yazidism, the Silat Bridge at Lalish plays a similar symbolic role.

Ancient sources: The Yasna (chapter 71) and the Vendidad (fargard 19) describe the bridge, and the Vendidad (13,9) mentions its guardian dogs; the Bundahishn relates the fate of the wicked.
