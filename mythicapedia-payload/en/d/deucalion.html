son of Prometheus and survivor of the great flood in Greek mythology

Deucalion

Deucalion is, in Greek mythology, the son of Prometheus who survived the great flood sent by Zeus together with his wife Pyrrha, becoming the origin of a new humanity. His story matters above all for its parallels with the biblical account of Noah and with Utnapishtim in the Epic of Gilgamesh.

The myth

According to the myth, Zeus resolved to end the Bronze Age, punished for its vices, with a great deluge. Deucalion, advised by his father Prometheus, built a chest, boarded it with Pyrrha, and the pair survived the waters for nine days and nights while nearly all of humanity perished. The sources disagree on where the vessel came to rest: Mount Parnassus, Etna, Athos and Othrys are all named.

Once the flood ended, the couple made a sacrifice to Zeus Phyxios, protector of fugitives, and consulted the oracle of Themis at Delphi on how to repopulate the earth. The answer was to throw the bones of their mother over their shoulders. After reflection, they understood that the mother was Gaia and the bones were stones: the stones thrown by Deucalion became men and those thrown by Pyrrha became women. In another version, Zeus told them through Hermes to throw stones behind them after Deucalion put out a fire for fear of displeasing him.

Deucalion and Pyrrha had children, among them Hellen, ancestor of the Hellenes, and Amphictyon. Apollodorus names three sons, while Hesiod's tradition speaks of three daughters. Deucalion later moved to Athens, where he is said to have built an early sanctuary of Olympian Zeus. Tradition also linked him to the constellation Aquarius.

Worship and religion

In Athens, Deucalion's memory was kept through an early sanctuary of Olympian Zeus, near which his tomb was shown. According to Strabo, offerings of wheat flour mixed with honey were made each year at an Athenian chasm believed to have swallowed the flood waters. Lucian describes a temple of Deucalion in Syria, beside a chasm that received the waters, to which pilgrims brought sea water twice a year.

Legacy

The name Deucalion is borne by the trans-Neptunian object (53311) Deucalion, discovered in 1999. The flood story has been compared with the accounts of Noah and Utnapishtim, and some scholars have pointed to elements drawn from the Epic of Gilgamesh.

Ancient sources: Hesiod, Apollodorus, Ovid in the Metamorphoses, Pindar and Pausanias mention him in their accounts of the flood and his descendants.

Trivia

An asteroid named after him. The trans-Neptunian object (53311) Deucalion, discovered in 1999, was named in his honor.
Stones that become people. In Greek, laos (people) and laas (stone) have meant nearly the same thing in many languages, according to the tale of stones turned into people.
