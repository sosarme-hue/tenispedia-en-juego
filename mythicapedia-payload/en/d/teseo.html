Theseus

Theseus is the founding hero of Athens in Greek mythology, best known for killing the Minotaur in the labyrinth of Crete. He matters because he embodies the civilizing hero who clears the roads of bandits and unites Attica under Athenian rule.

The myth

According to tradition, Theseus was the son of King Aegeus of Athens and Aethra, daughter of Pittheus, king of Troezen. Other accounts make Poseidon his father, giving him a double birth, mortal and divine, a feature shared by many Greek heroes. Before leaving, Aegeus hid his sword and sandals under a great rock and told Aethra to send the boy to Athens once he was strong enough to lift it.

As a young man Theseus moved the rock, but rather than sail, he chose the more dangerous land route. Along the way he faced a string of bandits and monsters: Periphetes, who beat travellers with a bronze club; Sinis, who bent pine trees to tear his victims apart; the Crommyonian Sow; Sciron, who kicked travellers off the cliffs; Cercyon, whom he beat in wrestling; and Procrustes, who stretched or mutilated guests to fit his bed. Each died by the method he had used on others.

In Athens, Medea, Aegeus's wife, tried to poison him, but Aegeus recognised the sword and sandals and stopped the attempt. Theseus then captured the Marathonian Bull and, on his return, ended the tribute Athens owed Crete: young Athenians sent to be devoured in the labyrinth of the Minotaur. With the help of Ariadne, daughter of King Minos, he entered the maze, killed the Minotaur and found his way out by the thread. Some versions say he later abandoned Ariadne on Naxos. On his return he forgot to change the black sails for white, and his father, believing him dead, threw himself into the sea that bears his name.

Worship and religion

Theseus was worshipped as a hero in Athens. Plutarch says that after uniting Attica into one city he built a palace on the Acropolis, and Pausanias mentions a cult of Aphrodite of all the People on its southern slope. His figure was tied to Athenian political unity rather than to a famous temple, and this cult has disappeared.

Legacy

Theseus appears in the work of William Shakespeare, including A Midsummer Night's Dream and The Two Noble Kinsmen. The story of the labyrinth and the thread has inspired countless retellings in art and literature, and the Minotaur's maze is still a common image for an inextricable tangle.

Ancient sources: Plutarch's Life of Theseus draws on earlier accounts by Pherecydes, Demon and Philochorus, and Pausanias describes his Athenian cult.
