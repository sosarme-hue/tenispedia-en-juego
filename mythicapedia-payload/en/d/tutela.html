Roman goddess of guardianship

Tutela

Tutela was the Roman goddess of protection and guardianship, watching over homes, cities, ships and people. Her name gave rise to the legal concept of tutela, or guardianship.

The myth

The sources barely tell her story. She was less a character with a biography than a personification of protection: in early Roman religion every deity could hold a tutela, a sphere of guardianship, and an altar might be dedicated to an unnamed "tutelary god" so that the protector's identity stayed secret. The goddess may have grown out of that abstraction. Under the emperors, tutela became a form of authority, advertised as the tutela Augusti, and the Flavian dynasty promoted her cult. A coin of AD 71 shows her as a woman with two children.

Worship and religion

She received her own temples and rituals in the imperial period and became part of household worship alongside the Lares and Penates, often paired with the Genius. Her cult was very common in Hispania, where she was linked to local communities and the urbanisation of the western provinces.

Legacy

She gave her name to the legal institution of tutela, and she is considered the origin of several places called Tudela in Spain.

Ancient sources: She appears mainly in inscriptions, especially in Gaul, and only rarely in literature.

In art

Trivia

A coin with two children. On a coin of AD 71 she appears as a woman accompanied by two children.
Guardian of the prow. Her statue held a place of honour on the prow of ships to protect crews during voyages.
