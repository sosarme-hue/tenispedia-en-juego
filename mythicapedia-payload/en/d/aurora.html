Roman goddess of the dawn

Aurora

Aurora is the Roman goddess who personifies the dawn: each morning she rises and crosses the sky, announcing the coming of the Sun. The Romans identified her with the Greek Eos and linked her to older Italic beliefs about the goddess Mater Matuta.

The myth

In Roman tradition, Aurora renews her light every morning and travels across the sky to herald the sunrise. Her parentage varies by author: Hyginus makes her a daughter of Hyperion and Thea, sister of the Sun and the Moon, while Ovid calls her Pallantis, linking her to the Titan Pallas.

Aurora appears mostly in stories of love. One of her lovers was Cephalus, a hunter married to Procris. Aurora tested his loyalty by disguising him as a stranger; Cephalus eventually gave in, and Procris, discovering the deception, fled to Crete. Another lover was Tithonus, a Trojan prince for whom she asked Jupiter to grant immortality. She forgot to ask for eternal youth, so Tithonus grew old without dying and, in some versions, she turned him into a cicada.

According to the myth, the tears Aurora sheds as she flies across the sky, mourning her son Memnon, form the morning dew. Some authors also make her the mother of the winds, including Auster, Aquilo and Favonius, children of the star-father Astraeus.

Worship and religion

A dawn goddess was already part of Roman religion before the Greek myth arrived: this was Mater Matuta, with whom Aurora came to be identified as a second name. Her cult was practised in a temple in the Forum Boarium, where she was also honoured as a guide for sailors under the name stella matutina. Every 11 June, rites asking for her protection, known as the Matralia, were held.

Legacy

Aurora has been a frequent subject in European painting, with works by Guido Reni, Guercino and Bouguereau, and in literature, where Virgil, Ovid and Dante mention her. Shakespeare evokes her in Romeo and Juliet and Tennyson portrays her in his poem "Tithonus". In science fiction, Isaac Asimov named a world of his Spacer series after her, and Björk refers to her in the song "Aurora".

Ancient sources: Ovid mentions her in his Metamorphoses and Heroides, Virgil in the Aeneid and Hyginus in his Fabulae.
