Greek goddess of the Moon

Selene

Selene is the Greek goddess and personification of the Moon. Daughter of the Titans Hyperion and Theia, she drives the moon chariot across the night sky once her brother Helios has completed his journey.

The myth

According to tradition, Selene was born to the Titans Hyperion and Theia and grew up alongside her siblings Helios and Eos. Hesiod's Theogony places her in this line, but the Homeric Hymn to Hermes makes her the daughter of Pallas, and other authors present her as the daughter of Helios or of Zeus and Leto. Her name is linked to the Greek word selas, meaning light.

The best-known story is her love for Endymion, a shepherd of extraordinary beauty whom she watched from the sky as he slept in a cave on Latmos. In some versions she granted him eternal sleep so she could kiss him unnoticed, and from their union came fifty daughters, the menai, which Pausanias connects with the months of the Olympiad. Other lovers are also credited to her, including Zeus, father of Pandia according to a Homeric Hymn, and Pan, who seduced her while wrapped in white wool.

Selene could also be fierce. She punished the shepherd Lileo, who worshipped only her, by sending lions after him, and the young satyr Ampelus, who compared himself to her, died when a bull, stung by a gadfly she had sent, gored him. She is also said to have created the Nemean Lion, the monster Heracles later defeated.

Worship and religion

Pausanias reports that she was worshipped in Elis and Talamae. The Orphics linked her with Artemis and Hecate in a triad and invoked her as the bull-horned Moon. In later sources she was merged with Artemis, and in Rome with Luna. The sources say nothing about any modern cult.

Legacy

Her name inspired selenium, an element discovered in 1817 by Jöns Jacob Berzelius, who named it for its link with tellurium, itself named after Tellus, meaning Earth. The Japanese lunar probe SELENE, the asteroid 580 Selene and selenology, the science of the Moon, also recall her. Jules Verne called the Moon's hypothetical inhabitants Selenites, and selenite, a glistening variety of gypsum, takes its name from the Moon's glow.

Ancient sources: Hesiod's Theogony and the Homeric Hymns mention her, as do Pausanias and later sources.

Trivia

Fifty daughters. According to Pausanias, Selene had fifty daughters with Endymion, the menai, often read as the fifty lunar months of an Olympiad.
A probe with her name. The Japanese space agency JAXA named its lunar orbiter mission SELENE after the goddess.
