bird-woman of Russian folklore

Sirin

The Sirin is a creature of Russian folklore with the head and chest of a beautiful woman and the body of a bird, usually an owl. It is often compared to the Greek sirens, but its song has the opposite purpose: instead of luring sailors to their deaths, it rewards the virtuous with promises of future bliss.

The myth

According to tradition, the Sirin lived in the land of India, near Eden or along the Euphrates River. Her songs foretold future joys to the saints, and she is often shown wearing a crown or a nimbus.

For men, however, her voice was dangerous. Whoever heard it forgot everything on earth, followed her and ultimately died. To save themselves, people fired cannons, rang bells or made any other loud noise that might scare the bird away.

Legacy

The Sirin was a popular subject in Russian prints and illustrations, from nineteenth-century lubok engravings to a 1908 postcard, and the painter Viktor Vasnetsov depicted her beside the Alkonost in Birds of Joy and Sorrow (1896). Its meaning shifted in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, when it came to symbolize world harmony and heavenly happiness, since only very happy people were thought able to hear her. The name has also been used by the writer Vladimir Nabokov as a pen name and by a Moog synthesizer, and a 2024 album by the singer Marjana Semkina bears the title.

Trivia

Paired with the Alkonost. In folk tales the Sirin is linked to the Alkonost, the bird of joy. The Sirin cries in the apple orchard in the morning, and in the afternoon the Alkonost arrives to laugh, and her dew gives healing powers to the fruit.
A writer's pen name. The novelist Vladimir Nabokov published some of his works under the pseudonym Sirin.
