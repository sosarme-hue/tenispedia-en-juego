Persian god of the Moon

Mah

Mah is the Moon deity of Zoroastrianism in Persia. His Avestan name is Mångha, meaning "moon" or "month". Although he is not prominent in the Avesta, his crescent became a symbol of royalty during the Parthian and Sasanian periods.

The myth

The Avestan hymns describe the Moon's phases in great detail, and according to tradition Ahura Mazda causes its waxing and waning, while the Amesha Spentas spread its light over the earth. In the Bundahishn, written in the twelfth century, the Moon receives the chihr of Gawiewdad, the primeval bull that Jeh kills at Ahriman's urging, and keeps it as the prototype of all animals.

The Moon also works alongside Vohu Manah, guardian of cattle, and is linked with mental harmony and inner peace.

Worship and religion

Herodotus says the Moon was the protective deity of Iranian expatriates living in Asia Minor. In the Zoroastrian calendar, the twelfth day of the month is dedicated to it.

Legacy

His crescent was a symbol of royalty under the Parthians and Sasanians, and he appears alongside Mithra on Kushan coins.

Ancient sources: The Avesta, with two hymns dedicated to the Moon (the third Nyaish and the seventh Yasht), and Herodotus.

Trivia
