semi-legendary Swedish king in the Hervarar saga

Björn at Haugi

Björn at Haugi, «Björn at the Barrow», is a semi-legendary Swedish king who appears in the Hervarar saga. His figure blends epic tradition with possible historical traces, which makes him of interest to anyone studying the origins of Viking-age Sweden.

The myth

The Hervarar saga, an Icelandic work from the 13th century, tells that Björn was the son of Erik Björnsson and that he ruled Sweden in diarchy with his brother Anund Uppsale. At the end of the saga a short chronicle of Swedish kings, running from Ivar the Wide-Fathoming to Philip, was appended, and it names Björn with the epithet «at the Barrow» (Björn på Håga), from the Old Norse word haugr, meaning mound or burial barrow.

The account places his reign in the first half of the 9th century, since his nephew Eric Anundsson was a contemporary of Harald Fairhair of Norway. Other sources bring him closer to a royal court: the Landnámabók mentions a Swede called Þórðr knappr, one of the first settlers in Iceland, whose father was named Björn at Haugi, and the Skáldatal lists Björn alongside his court poet Bragi the Old and a second poet, Erpr lútandi.

The Icelandic scholar Jón Jóhannesson suggested that this Björn may have been a minor ruler in Norway around the late 9th century, and that tradition later moved him to Sweden, perhaps because of legendary associations or a possible Swedish ancestry. Even so, many accounts identify him with King Bern in Rimbert's Vita Ansgarii, who received Ansgar at Birka around 827. That identification is uncertain, because Rimbert only says that Birka lay within Bern's kingdom.

Ancient sources: The Hervarar saga (13th century), the Landnámabók, the Skáldatal and Rimbert's Vita Ansgarii, which mentions a king Bern around 827.

Trivia

A name with a mound. The epithet Haugi comes from the Old Norse word haugr, meaning mound or burial barrow.
A settler in Iceland. The Landnámabók mentions Þórðr knappr, a Swede among the first settlers of Iceland whose father was called Björn at Haugi.
