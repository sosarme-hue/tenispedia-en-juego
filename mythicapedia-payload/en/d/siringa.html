Arcadian nymph turned into reeds

Syrinx

Syrinx is a nymph of Greek mythology, a follower of Artemis, whose flight from Pan gave rise to the panpipes. Her story comes mainly from Ovid's Metamorphoses, where she is an Arcadian nymph who rejected the god.

The myth

According to Ovid, Syrinx was a famous nymph of the Arcadian mountains, admired for her beauty and devoted to Artemis, whom she imitated in hunting and in her vow of virginity. Several satyrs pursued her in vain, until Pan, his head crowned with pine needles, saw her returning from Mount Lycaeus and began to court her.

Syrinx fled him to the sandy river Ladon. Cornered, she asked her sister nymphs for help, and they turned her into a clump of reeds. When Pan arrived he could only embrace the reeds stirred by the wind, whose sound seemed to him like a sigh. He decided to make an instrument from them, the syrinx or panpipes, named after the nymph so that he could always speak with her.

The story also appears in the late author Nonnus, and Longus tells it differently: in his version Syrinx hides among the reeds and vanishes into the marsh, and Pan, making the pipes from them, keeps her with him forever.

Legacy

The tale inspired nineteenth-century poets such as Elizabeth Barrett Browning and Thomas Woolner, and the English word syringe derives from her name. Claude Debussy wrote the flute piece Syrinx in 1913, and Carl Nielsen, Benjamin Britten and Maurice Ravel also drew on the episode in their music. The name has also been taken by a Canadian progressive rock band and appears in Rush's song "2112".

Ancient sources: Ovid recounts her in Book One of the Metamorphoses, and Nonnus recalls her in his Dionysiaca.

Trivia

A word with history. The English word syringe, meaning a hypodermic device, derives from the nymph's name.
Another version of the escape. In Longus, Syrinx does not ask her sisters for transformation; she hides among the reeds and vanishes into the marsh.
