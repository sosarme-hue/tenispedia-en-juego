Hindu goddess of the severed head

Chhinnamasta

Chhinnamasta, 'she whose head is severed', is a Hindu goddess and one of the ten Mahavidyas, fierce forms of the mother goddess Devi within the Tantric tradition. Her iconography, in which she beheads herself and drinks her own blood, makes her one of the most unusual figures in the Hindu pantheon.

The myth

According to Tantric accounts, the goddess belongs to the Mahavidyas, a group of ten fierce manifestations of Devi. One version found in the Shakta Maha-bhagavata Purana tells how Sati, daughter of Daksha and first wife of Shiva, was insulted when she was not invited to her father's fire sacrifice. After Shiva refused to let her attend, Sati took on terrifying forms, becoming the Mahavidyas, who surround Shiva from the ten cardinal directions.

The legends specific to Chhinnamasta emphasise self-sacrifice, sexual dominance and self-destructive fury. She is shown nude, standing or seated on a divine couple in sexual union, holding her own severed head and a scimitar, while three jets of blood spurt from her neck and are drunk by herself and two attendants. Some interpreters read her as embodying both the life she gives and the death she brings.

Worship and religion

She is worshipped within the Kalikula sect of Shaktism, the Goddess-centred tradition of Hinduism. Although she is counted among the Mahavidyas, temples devoted to her are rare and are found mostly in Nepal and eastern India, where some western states know her as Jogani Maa. Her worship remains alive, especially among Tantric practitioners, who regard her as central to the awakening of kundalini.

Legacy

She is closely linked to Chinnamunda, the severed-headed form of the Tibetan Buddhist goddess Vajrayogini. The name Chhinnamasta is also used as a generic label for headless goddess icons in several shrines in eastern India.

Ancient sources: Texts such as the Shakta Maha-bhagavata Purana (c. 950 CE), the Devi-Bhagavata Purana, the Brihaddharma Purana, the Kamadhenu Tantra and the Maha-nirvana Tantra; in the Buddhist tradition, the Sadhanamala.

Trivia

An epithet of Renuka. In a legend linked to the goddess Renuka, she is given the epithet Chhinnamasta, though she does not behead herself: her son Parashurama does it.
A Buddhist origin. Buddhist texts tell how the sisters Mekhala and Kanakhala cut off their heads, offered them to their guru and then danced, with Vajrayogini herself dancing alongside them.
