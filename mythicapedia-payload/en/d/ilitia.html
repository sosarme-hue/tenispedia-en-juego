Greek goddess of childbirth and midwifery

Eileithyia

Eileithyia was the Greek goddess of childbirth and midwifery, usually described as the daughter of Zeus and Hera. She matters because she linked several local cults across the Greek world, from Minoan Crete to Argos and Olympia, and because women in labour called on her for centuries.

The myth

Hesiod, Apollodorus and Diodorus Siculus name her as the daughter of Zeus and Hera. Pausanias, however, cited a lost early poet, Olen, who called her «the clever spinner», linked her with the Fates and made her older than Cronus. Pindar places her beside the throne of the Moirai and addresses her as a goddess of births. Callimachus says she helped her sister Hebe in labour.

In the Iliad she appears alone or in the plural as the Eileithyiai, the harsh daughters of Hera who bring the pains of childbirth. The Third Homeric Hymn to Apollo tells how Hera kept Eileithyia from coming from the Hyperborean north, so that Leto could not give birth to Artemis and Apollo. The other goddesses sent Iris to fetch her, and as soon as she set foot on Delos the gods were born. Another tradition says that, at birth, she armed the giant Damasen with a shield.

Burkert notes that in classical times she is closely tied to Artemis and Hera but has little character of her own. An Orphic hymn to Prothyraia gives Artemis an epithet that makes the huntress also the helper of women in labour, showing how closely these figures overlapped.

Worship and religion

The cave of Amnisos, near Knossos in Crete, was regarded as her birthplace, and its stalactites hinted at her double role of bringing on labour and delaying it. Votive offerings have been found there, and her cult in Crete goes back to Minoan and Mycenaean times; she appears as E-re-u-ti-ja on tablets from Knossos. On the mainland, Pausanias described sanctuaries at Aigion, Argos, Corinth and Mases, and an altar at Olympia where a virgin priestess looked after a sacred serpent linked to Sosipolis. Women in labour invoked her and left offerings.

Legacy

In art she appears on vases showing the birth of Athena from Zeus's head, sometimes with two Eileithyiai with raised hands. A 4th-century BC mosaic from Durrës probably depicts her. Her Roman counterpart, Lucina, keeps the idea of bringing children into the light.

Ancient sources: Hesiod's Theogony, the Iliad, the Third Homeric Hymn to Apollo, Diodorus Siculus and the Odyssey, which mentions her cave at Amnisos.

Trivia

A cave with a double meaning. The cave at Amnisos had stalactites linked to the goddess's double role, as she could both hasten and delay labour.
A sacred serpent. At Olympia, a virgin priestess fed a serpent linked to the cults of Sosipolis and Eileithyia with water and barley and honey cakes.
