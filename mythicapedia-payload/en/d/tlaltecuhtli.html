Mexica goddess of the earth and fertility

Tlaltecuhtli

Tlaltecuhtli is an earth deity worshipped by the Mexica, the Nahuatl-speaking people of central Mexico. In myth she is a primordial sea monster whose dismembered body shaped the world, and she is linked to fertility and to the devouring of the dead.

The myth

According to Mexica tradition, before creation the world was a motionless ocean. Tezcatlipoca and Quetzalcoatl, in the form of serpents, attacked the sea monster Cipactli and tore her in two: one half became the sky and the other the earth.

Despite her defeat, the monster survived and demanded human blood. The gods decided her body would become the landscape: her skin grasses and flowers, her hair trees, her eyes springs and wells, her mouth rivers and caves. Accounts differ, and some describe her as a toad or lizard with jaws full of teeth.

In the tradition, Tlaltecuhtli devoured corpses and then gave birth to each person's soul, which set out for Mictlan, Tlalocan or the sun depending on the manner of death.

Worship and religion

Tlaltecuhtli received blood and human heart sacrifices so that she would keep nourishing the earth. People invoked her in prayers, and it was believed that she swallowed the sun at dusk, so eclipses caused anxiety and more sacrifices. Midwives called on her during difficult births. Her images were carved on the bases of sculptures and on sacrificial vessels, hidden from human view.

Legacy

She appears in the Aztec calendar as the second of the thirteen day signs, and some scholars suggest her face may occupy the centre of the Sun Stone. Modern researchers debate her gender, since her name is masculine while most of her depictions are female.

Ancient sources: Post-conquest Mexica manuscripts and the Histoire du Méxique, compiled in the mid-sixteenth century, record her myths and images.

Trivia

A masculine name. In Nahuatl, Tlaltecuhtli takes a grammatically masculine form, yet most of her representations are female.
Hidden from view. Many of her carvings were placed on the bases of sculptures to other gods, so they rested against the ground and were not visible to people.
