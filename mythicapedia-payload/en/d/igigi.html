celestial gods of ancient Mesopotamia

Igigi

The igigi were lesser gods of ancient Mesopotamia, linked to the heavens and to the hardest labour of the divine world. In some texts the name also referred to the council of the celestial gods.

The myth

According to the Akkadian poem Atrahasis, the igigi were younger, lower-ranking gods who served the Anunnaki, the senior deities. Their task was to dig and clear irrigation channels for a garden paradise, work that wore them down. Exhausted, they rebelled against Enlil's rule: they set their tools on fire and surrounded his great house at night. When the Anunnaki council learned that the toil on the canals was the cause of the unrest, it decided to create humans to take over the farm work.

The Enuma Elish tells of a similar revolt, and in that version the leader of the rebels was sacrificed so that humanity could be created. The accounts differ in their details, so they are best read as variants of one tradition.

Worship and religion

Topographical texts mention Edur-kuga, 'House of the Sacred Brick', a sanctuary of the igigi in Babylon, located in the district of TE.E.KI. Their name also appears in personal theophoric names, showing that it was invoked in everyday life. The sources say nothing about a broad cult or about any survival today.

Ancient sources: The Akkadian poems Atrahasis and the Enuma Elish, which recount their rebellion.

Trivia

A name with numbers. In Sumerian, the name written as í-gì-gì can be read as a sum of numerals adding to 7, the number of the Great Gods, or as a product of 600, which in some traditions was the total number of gods.
Watchers. The root igi means 'to look', and the term has been linked to 'the watchers', the same word the Bible uses for the Nephilim.
