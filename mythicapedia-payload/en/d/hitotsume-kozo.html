one-eyed yōkai of Japanese folklore

Hitotsume-kozō

The hitotsume-kozō is a yōkai of Japanese folklore that takes the form of a bald child, sometimes resembling a young monk, with a single eye in the centre of the face. It belongs to a group of comparatively harmless supernatural beings who provoke more surprise than real fear.

The myth

According to popular tales, the hitotsume-kozō appears suddenly to startle people, especially outdoors, though it rarely causes any real harm. It enjoys silence, so it often scolds noisy people. Some stories place it indoors, such as the one in which a merchant waiting in an Edo mansion saw a child of about ten rolling a hanging scroll up and down; when he objected, the boy turned to reveal a single eye, and the man fainted. Other accounts tell of a girl in Aizu-Wakamatsu who was stared down by a one-eyed child asking for money.

In the traditions of central and eastern Japan, people placed bamboo baskets outside their doors to ward it off. In the Kantō region it was said that on certain nights of the lunisolar calendar the hitotsume-kozō visited homes with a notebook, recording each household's faults so that misfortune could follow.

Worship and religion

In folk religion the hitotsume-kozō was linked to the dōsojin, protective deities of roads and villages. In some places its notebooks or dōsojin figures were burned at the start of the year to avert misfortune. The folklorist Yanagita Kunio interpreted it as a fallen mountain god.

Legacy

It appears in kaidan collections, in rakugo performances and in Edo-period illustrated catalogues such as the Hyakki Zukan. Its cute and comical image has made it a familiar figure in Japanese illustrated folklore.

Ancient sources: It appears in kaidan literature and in Edo-period works such as the Hyakki Zukan and the Bakemono Zukushi.

Trivia

Bamboo baskets. To drive it away, some families hung or placed bamboo baskets beside their doors.
A fallen god. Yanagita Kunio proposed that the hitotsume-kozō was originally a mountain god who had lost his divine status.
