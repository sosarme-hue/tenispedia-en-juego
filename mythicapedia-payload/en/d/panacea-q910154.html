Greek goddess of healing and the cure-all remedy

Panacea

Panacea is, in Greek mythology, the goddess of healing, a daughter of Asclepius, and her name gave rise to the word used for any remedy supposed to cure every ill. The idea of a single medicine that cures everything has run through centuries of alchemy, folk medicine and commerce.

The myth

According to the tradition, Panacea was one of the daughters of Asclepius, god of medicine, along with her sisters Hygieia, Iaso, Aceso and Aglaea, each responsible for one aspect of health. Panacea was the goddess of the cure, and she was said to possess an elixir or potion able to heal any human malady.

Her name, of Greek origin (pan, all, and akos, remedy), eventually came to mean the remedy itself. In the Middle Ages, physicians and alchemists sought a panacea in complex preparations such as theriac, which sometimes combined more than fifty ingredients, and linked the quest with the philosopher's stone and the elixir of life. After contact with the Americas, people hoped to find one among the unfamiliar plants of the New World.

Legacy

In the seventeenth century, the Dutch doctor Giles Everard argued that tobacco was the long-lost ancient panacea, and in 1659 an English work appeared with the term in its title. In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, so-called patent medicines were marketed as panaceas; Benjamin Brandreth's Vegetable Universal Pills were even mentioned in Herman Melville's Moby-Dick. Today the word carries a negative sense, linked to quackery, and in literature it also refers to any solution claimed to solve all the problems of a matter.

Trivia

Brandreth's pills. Benjamin Brandreth sold his purgative pills as a cure for many illnesses, and his name became so well known in the United States that it appeared in Moby-Dick.
A medieval panacea. One medieval recipe for a panacea included crushed gemstones and gold powder, distilled for eight days with many other medicinal components.
