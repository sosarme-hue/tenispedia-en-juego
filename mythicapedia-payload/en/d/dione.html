Greek goddess, mother of Aphrodite

Dione

Dione is an ancient Greek goddess of pre-Olympian origin, best known as the mother of Aphrodite in Homer's Iliad. Her name is linked to that of Zeus, and her role as his cult consort at Dodona makes her a key figure for understanding the roots of the Greek pantheon.

The myth

According to tradition, Dione lives on Olympus and, with Zeus, is the mother of Aphrodite. Homer does not explain how that union came about or give Dione's own ancestry. Later writers differ: the mythographer Apollodorus makes her the thirteenth Titan, daughter of Gaia and Uranus, and describes her as one of Zeus's lovers rather than an earlier wife. Hesiod leaves her off his list of Titans and has Aphrodite born from sea foam after Uranus was castrated, though he does name a Dione among the Oceanids. Hyginus counts her among the children of Earth and Aether, and other sources make her the mother of Dionysus.

In Book V of the Iliad, during the Trojan War, Diomedes wounds Aphrodite's hand as she protects her son Aeneas. Dione comforts her by recalling other gods wounded by mortals and tends to her injuries. Zeus, while urging her to leave the battlefield, calls her his daughter. Dione's other known role comes at the birth of Apollo: she attends Leto through a nine-day labour prolonged by Hera, and with the other goddesses she celebrates the god's arrival.

Worship and religion

In the first century BC, Strabo recorded her worship in a sacred grove near Lepreon, on the western coast of the Peloponnese. She was mainly honoured as Zeus's consort in his temples, above all at the oracle of Dodona, which Herodotus considered the oldest in Greece. Votive inscriptions show joint dedications to the two, and some scholars suspect Dodona was first a centre for an earth goddess. Herodotus gives two accounts of the oracle's founding, one involving Egyptian priestesses and the other black doves. Apollodorus of Athens records "Dione" as a regional epithet of Hera at Dodona.

Legacy

Her name shares a root with Zeus, which modern philology links to the Indo-European dyeu-, "sky, bright god". After Linear B was deciphered, some scholars identified with her a goddess called Di-u-ja, named in Mycenaean tablets. She also appears on the 2nd-century BC frieze of the Great Altar of Pergamon, among Aphrodite's Olympian family. Diana shares the distant root but has no historical connection to her.

Ancient sources: Homer's Iliad, Hesiod's Theogony, the writings of Apollodorus, Hyginus's Fabulae and Herodotus's Histories.

Trivia

Divine queen. Her name, Δῖώνη, is usually read as "divine queen". In its older form it is linked to Zeus's genitive Diós, which is why she is seen as a feminine form of the god.
Doves at Dodona. According to the priestesses of Dodona, two black doves flew to Libya and to Dodona and commanded the founding of oracles to Zeus.
