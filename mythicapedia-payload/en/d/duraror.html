stag of Norse mythology

Duraþrór

Duraþrór is one of four stags that, according to Norse mythology, feed on the branches of the world tree Yggdrasil. His name appears in the Poetic Edda and in Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda.

The myth

According to the poem Grímnismál, part of the Poetic Edda, four male stags graze among the branches of the world tree, Yggdrasil. They crane their necks upward to chew at the foliage, and the morning dew gathers in their antlers and forms the rivers of the world. Their names are Dáinn, Dvalinn, Duneyrr and Duraþrór.

Snorri Sturluson summarizes the stanza in the Gylfaginning section of the Prose Edda, where he uses the word barr, usually applied to the needles of fir and pine trees. This has led to debate over whether the tree was a conifer, although today barr is generally understood to mean foliage in general. In 1824, Finnur Magnússon proposed that the stags stood for winds: based on their names, Dáinn and Dvalinn would be calm breezes and Duneyrr and Duraþrór heavy winds, perhaps linked to snoring. These are modern interpretations, not statements from the ancient texts.

Ancient sources: He appears in stanza 26 of Grímnismál in the Poetic Edda, and in Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda, in the Gylfaginning.

Trivia

Dwarf names. Dáinn and Dvalinn are also dwarf names in Norse mythology, a link scholars point to when interpreting the stags as winds.
One stag?. Finnur Jónsson proposed that there was originally a single stag, later multiplied into four, and some identify it with Eikþyrnir, mentioned earlier in the same poem.
