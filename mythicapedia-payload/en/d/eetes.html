king of Colchis, father of Medea

Aeëtes

Aeëtes is the ruler of Colchis in Greek myth, son of the sun god Helios and father of Medea and Circe. He is the main antagonist in the story of Jason and the Argonauts and the Golden Fleece, and his figure links the Greek tale with the lands of the Black Sea.

The myth

In Hesiod's Theogony, Aeëtes was the son of Helios and Perseis, an Oceanid, and brother of Circe. Other authors give different mothers, including Ephyra, Antiope or Asterope. Apollonius of Rhodes says his first wife was Asterodeia, a Caucasian nymph, with whom he fathered Absyrtus, and that he later married Idyia, mother of Medea. Other versions name Neaera, Clytia, Ipsia or Eurylyte as his consort.

In the myth he is the king who welcomes Phrixus, son of Athamas, who reached Colchis riding the golden-fleeced ram, and gives him his daughter Chalciope in marriage. Phrixus gives the fleece to the king, who hangs it on a sacred tree dedicated to Ares.

When Jason comes to claim the fleece, Aeëtes sets him tasks: yoking fire-breathing bulls, ploughing a field of Ares and sowing dragon's teeth. Medea helps him with her magic, and Jason prevails. Aeëtes then orders him to overcome the dragon that never sleeps, which Medea also makes possible. As the Argonauts flee with Medea, the king pursues them, but Medea kills and dismembers her half-brother Absyrtus and throws the pieces into the sea. Aeëtes stops to gather his son's remains, and the fugitives escape.

The tradition says the king of Corcyra, Alcinous, rules that Medea should not be returned, and that Aeëtes eventually loses his kingdom to his brother Perses. Medea comes to his aid with her son Medus, who defeats Perses and restores the throne to his grandfather.

Legacy

Aeëtes appears in ancient writers such as Arrian and Strabo, who treat him as a real Colchian ruler, though scholars regard him as probably mythical. His name was linked to the Greek word for eagle, and in tradition he was credited with founding Aea, a realm on the eastern shore of the Black Sea.

Ancient sources: Hesiod, in the Theogony, describes him as son of Helios and Perseis; Apollonius of Rhodes narrates his marriages to Asterodeia and Idyia and his role in the voyage of the Argonauts.
