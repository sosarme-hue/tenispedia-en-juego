Greek personification of Time

Chronos

Chronos, also spelled Chronus, is the personification of time in Greek mythology, a primordial figure discussed by pre-Socratic philosophers and later writers. His name was easily confused with that of the Titan Cronus, father of Zeus, and during the Renaissance he gave rise to the image of Father Time.

The myth

In the Orphic tradition, the unaging Chronos was engendered by earth and water, and he appears alongside Ananke, Necessity. In the version attributed to Hieronymus and Hellanicus, he takes the form of a three-headed dragon, with the head of a bull, a lion and, in the middle, the face of the god himself, and wings on his shoulders. The two beings join, and from Chronos come Aether, Chaos and Erebus, while in the primordial waters the cosmic egg is incubated, from which Phanes emerges.

Pherecydes of Syros, in his lost work Heptamykhos, speaks of three eternal principles: Zas (Zeus), Chthonie (the earth) and Chronos. For him, Time acts as a demiurge, shaping fire, air and water, and after establishing order he defeats Ophioneus in a struggle and drives him into the depths of the sea. Epicurus describes Time and Necessity as one indissoluble entity without parents. Euripides makes him the father of Aion, and other texts link him with Night, from whom Day and the Fates were born.

The tradition admits that a coherent family is hard to construct for a god so close to philosophy. Boccaccio imagines him as a son of Saturn and reads him symbolically. The Egyptians associated him with the constellation of the Serpent-Bearer, a man holding a serpent that bites its own tail, a symbol of the cycle of time.

Legacy

Chronos was the Greek name of the planet Saturn, the outermost of the seven celestial bodies visible to the naked eye. Its observable period of about thirty years made it seem the guardian of time, which is why it was pictured as a long-bearded old man. In the Renaissance he merged with the Titan Cronus and became Father Time, with his scythe. Greco-Roman mosaics show him holding the zodiac wheel. Boccaccio and ancient astrologers debated the so-called Great Year, the cycle in which the planets return to their starting positions.

Ancient sources: Euripides, Epicurus, Plutarch, Cicero and Nonnus mention Time, while the Orphic texts and Pherecydes of Syros in his Heptamykhos place him among the first principles.

Trivia

Two gods, one name. Cicero explained that Saturn, called Cronus in Greek, bore that name because Krónos is the same as chrónos, meaning a span of time. The similarity of the names has caused confusion ever since.
Different spellings. Some authors write his name as Jronos, reflecting the Greek letter Chi with a Spanish sound, while others prefer Chronos or even the Latin form Tempus.
