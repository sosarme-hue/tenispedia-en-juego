heraldic serpent devouring a human, symbol of Milan

Biscione

The biscione (“big grass snake”) is a heraldic serpent devouring or emerging with a human child, a historic emblem of Milan and of the Visconti and Sforza dynasties. Its origin is unknown, though several legends are told about it.

The myth

According to a tradition recorded by Galvano Fiamma, Ottone Visconti won the emblem by defeating a Saracen in single combat. The enemy carried it as his weapon, showing a serpent devouring a man on both shield and helmet.

Other accounts link it to a bronze serpent that, according to tradition, Archbishop Arnulf II of Arsago brought to Milan from Constantinople in the 11th century. The name comes from biscia, meaning grass snake, itself from bistia, a corruption of the Latin bestia, beast.

Legacy

The biscione still appears on Milan's arms and on the Alfa Romeo logo, which pairs it with the city's flag. Inter Milan uses it as its emblem, and Silvio Berlusconi adapted it for the Mediaset and Fininvest logos. It also appears on the coats of arms of Sanok and Pruzhany, honouring Bona Sforza.

Ancient sources: Bonvesin da la Riva's De magnalibus urbis Mediolani, written in the late 13th century, records it as a symbol of the Visconti.

Trivia

A fish and a prophet. In some depictions of the Hindu deity Matsya, his upper body emerges from the mouth of a fish, a image comparable to the biscione. In early Christian catacomb art, the prophet Jonah appears swallowed by a serpent-like fish.
Eagles and vipers. An inscription on the Filarete Tower of Sforza Castle reads “Eagles joined with vipers”, referring to the marriage of Francesco Sforza to Bianca Maria Visconti, daughter of the last Visconti duke.
