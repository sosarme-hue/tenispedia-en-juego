Norse end-of-the-world battle

Ragnarök

Ragnarök is the foretold final battle of Norse mythology, in which gods, giants and monsters will perish and the world will be destroyed by fire and flood. It matters because it presents a vision of gods who know their fate in advance and cannot escape it.

The myth

According to Norse tradition, Ragnarök will be preceded by Fimbulvetr, a winter of three consecutive seasons without summer, when conflict will break out and mortals will die. The wolves Sköll and Hati will devour the sun and moon, the stars will vanish, and the earth will tremble until mountains fall and the bonds holding Loki and his son, the wolf Fenrir, break.

The Völuspá, in the Poetic Edda, describes the serpent Jörmungandr rising from the ocean and poisoning sky and earth, while the giants sail in the ship Naglfar and the fire beings of Muspelheim, led by Surtr, cross toward Vígrid. Heimdall sounds his horn, Odin consults Mímir, and the einherjar march with the gods.

In the battle, Odin falls to Fenrir, Thor kills Jörmungandr but dies of its venom after nine steps, Freyr is defeated by Surtr, and Loki and Heimdall kill each other. Víðarr avenges his father by setting his foot on the wolf's jaw. After the fire, the earth rises again from the sea, and surviving gods gather at Iðavöllr with the humans Líf and Lífþrasir.

Legacy

The word gave rise to the phrase Götterdämmerung, 'Twilight of the Gods', popularised by Richard Wagner in the last opera of his Der Ring des Nibelungen (1876).

Ancient sources: The Völuspá and other poems of the Poetic Edda, and Snorri Sturluson's 13th-century Prose Edda, which describes its events in detail.

Trivia

A name with several readings. The name is read as 'fate of the gods' or 'twilight of the gods', depending on how its second element is understood, a term philologists still debate.
A foretold ending. The gods know from prophecy what will happen and know they cannot prevent it.
