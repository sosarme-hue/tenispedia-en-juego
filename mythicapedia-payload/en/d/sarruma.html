Hurrian, Hittite and Luwian mountain god

Šarruma

Šarruma is a mountain god of Anatolian origin, worshipped first by the Hurrians and later adopted by the Hittites and Luwians. His name is linked to the Akkadian word šarru, 'king', though its etymology remains unclear.

The myth

According to tradition, Šarruma was the son of the weather god Teshub and the goddess Hepat, and brother of the goddess Inara. He was shown as a warrior riding a tiger or a panther and wielding an axe, although some early images show him as a bull.

His only appearance in a mythological text is in a Hurrian tablet from Ugarit, where he is called the messenger (sukkalu) of Kumarbi. His usual role was that of a protector and intercessor between worshippers and Teshub and Hepat.

Worship and religion

The Hurrians worshipped him in south-eastern Anatolia and northern Syria. After he entered the Hittite pantheon, King Tudhaliya IV chose him as his personal guardian god, and he appears in the procession of gods at the rock sanctuary of Yazılıkaya. In the Iron Age he was still worshipped in Syria and southern Anatolia. There is no evidence of a living cult.

Legacy

His name appears in Luwian personal names attested as late as the Hellenistic period in Cilicia and Lycia.

Ancient sources: Hittite, Hurrian and Ugaritic texts, such as the Ugarit text RS 1.007, where he appears as the messenger of Kumarbi.

Trivia

A royal seal. The seal of the crown prince Mursili III showed Šarruma embracing him, reflecting the god's close link to the Hittite royal family.
A dream of shrines. Queen Puduhepa, wife of Hattusili III, dreamed that she promised to build him twelve shrines in the mountains.
