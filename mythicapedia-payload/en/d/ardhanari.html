Hindu deity, composite form of Shiva and Parvati

Ardhanari

Ardhanari, also known as Ardhanarishvara, is an androgynous Hindu deity that joins Shiva, on the right side, and his consort Parvati or Shakti, on the left, in a single body. It expresses the union of the masculine and feminine principles of the universe and the idea that God's feminine energy is inseparable from God himself.

The myth

The Puranas and iconographic treatises tell of Ardhanarishvara, whose name means "the Lord who is half woman". According to tradition, the figure draws on older Vedic ideas, such as the twin pair Yama-Yami or the primordial creator described as a bull who is also a cow. The Brihadaranyaka Upanishad tells how Purusha split himself into male and female halves, which then came together to produce all life. The Shvetashvatara Upanishad names Rudra, the forerunner of Shiva, as maker of all and root of Purusha and Prakriti, hinting at his androgynous nature.

The Shiva Purana gives the hymn "I am one, but I become many", which expresses Shiva as both the universe and the souls. Advaita thinkers see the form as consistent with non-duality, while Dvaita thinkers see it as compatible with the difference between God and souls. The Rigveda, as quoted, describes God as male who is also female.

Worship and religion

Ardhanarishvara is a widespread form in Indian religious art and appears in most Shiva temples, although very few temples are dedicated specifically to this deity. Some sources connect it with an attempt to unite the Shaiva and Shakta sects. Its worship remains alive within Hinduism.

Legacy

The figure has been widely sculpted in India, especially in Chola bronzes and at the reliefs of Ellora and Elephanta. The sources also note its presence in Japan, where it appears as Aldernalisuvara.

Ancient sources: The Puranas, the Shiva Purana, the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad, the Shvetashvatara Upanishad, the Rigveda and the Matsya Purana, together with treatises such as the Shilparatna and Agamic texts.

Trivia

Four arms. In the four-armed form, a right hand may hold an axe or a trident, and a left hand a mirror or a lotus, depending on the source.
Right and left halves. Except in a few cases, the right half represents Shiva and the left Parvati. In some Shakta images the feminine half occupies the right side.
