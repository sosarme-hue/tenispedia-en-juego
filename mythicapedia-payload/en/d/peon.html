healer god of Greek mythology

Paean

Paean is a minor god of Greek mythology, the divine physician of the gods, who appears in Homer's Iliad and Odyssey. His name means "healer" and in time it became an epithet shared by other healing deities, such as Apollo and Asclepius.

The myth

According to Homer, Paean was the physician of the Olympian gods. When Heracles wounded Hades with an arrow at Pylos, the god of the underworld went in distress to the house of Zeus, and Paean soothed his pain with medicines and healed him, since he was not of mortal make. The same happened to Hera, wounded in the breast by the hero, and to Ares, wounded by Diomedes during the Trojan War. Paean sprinkled the war god with pain-killing remedies, and the wound closed as swiftly as fig juice curdles white milk.

In the Odyssey, Helen receives from Polydamna, wife of Thon in Egypt, wise drugs that ease sorrow, and the text says the Egyptians are unmatched healers because they trace their lineage to Paeeon. Hesiod, according to a scholium, named him as a deity independent of Apollo. Later, Paeon became an epithet of Apollo, in his role as a god able to bring disease and also to cure it, and then of Asclepius, the healer god par excellence.

Worship and religion

The sources do not document temples or festivals dedicated to Paean. His healing role was mainly associated with Apollo, who was propitiated as a healer, and the name Paean was used as an opening invocation in ritual songs. Helios was also invoked as Paion, probably because of his identification with Apollo.

Ancient sources: Homer's Iliad and Odyssey mention him as a divine physician, and Hesiod names him as a deity independent of Apollo.

Trivia

A Mycenaean tablet. A Linear B tablet found at Knossos contains the form pa-ja-wo-ne, which some scholars consider an ancestor of Paean. It is believed that in Mycenaean Greek it named Apollo.
A name from ritual. The name is linked to the ritual cry iē paiōn, understood as the opening invocation of a song.
