Roman god of the newborn's first cry

Vaticanus

Vaticanus, also known as Vagitanus, was a minor god of Roman religion who presided over the first cry (vagitus) of newborn babies. His name is linked to the Latin verb vagire, to cry, and he belongs to a group of childbirth deities whom Roman authors recorded.

The myth

Ancient sources preserve little beyond his function. Various authors discussed his name: Varro, quoted by Aulus Gellius, connected the god with the sound a baby makes at birth, explaining that the opening syllables of his name echo that wail.

Vaticanus is also associated with the hill that later gave its name to the Vatican, although the link between the place name and the god is disputed. According to Varro, the god was honoured with an altar at the lower end of a new road, where a voice from heaven had been heard, just as with the god Aius.

Augustine of Hippo, in The City of God, uses Vaticanus as an example of how the names of the gods reveal their functions: the god who presides over infants' cries. He adds that Vaticanus is the one who opens the newborn's mouth so that it cries.

Legacy

The name of the Vatican, the Roman seat of the Holy See, comes from the Vatican hill and has been linked to the god, although the etymology remains uncertain.

Ancient sources: Varro, in his work on divine matters, as quoted by Aulus Gellius, and Augustine of Hippo in The City of God.

Trivia

Disputed name. Ancient writers already debated whether the correct form was Vaticanus or Vagitanus, and some modern scholars think Vagitanus was a vulgar Latin form.
A god for the first cry. According to the texts, Vaticanus was distinct from Fabulinus, the god invoked for the first articulate speech: one presided over the cry and the other over speech.
