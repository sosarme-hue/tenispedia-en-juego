Greek goddess of the hunt and the wild

Artemis

Artemis is one of the oldest and most widely worshipped deities of ancient Greece, mistress of the hunt, the wilderness and youth. Daughter of Zeus and Leto and twin sister of Apollo, she is one of the twelve Olympian gods, and the Romans knew her as Diana.

The myth

According to the Homeric and Hesiodic tradition, Artemis was the daughter of Zeus and Leto, and Aeschylus calls her the child of Leto. Other accounts differ: Pausanias makes her a daughter of Demeter, and Herodotus names Dionysus and Isis as her parents, with Leto serving only as her nurse. Scholars explain these variants by the identification of the Greek goddess with local or foreign deities. Her birthplace is also described differently; in some versions she was born on Ortygia.

In the stories of the hunt, Artemis roams forests and mountains accompanied by nymphs. The hunter Actaeon saw her bathing naked, and the goddess turned him into a deer so that his own hounds would devour him. In another myth, Callisto loses the goddess's company after breaking her vow of virginity, is transformed into a bear, and is later placed among the stars as Ursa Major. The epic tradition also tells how Artemis held back the winds that trapped the Greek fleet at Aulis, after King Agamemnon killed a deer sacred to her. She demanded the sacrifice of his daughter Iphigenia, though in most versions she spares the girl and leaves a deer in her place. During the Trojan War she supported the Trojans and challenged Hera in battle.

The goddess punishes the loss of virginity, insults and neglected sacrifices harshly, sometimes with wild beasts or death. At the same time, the myths present her as a protector of young people and of girls before marriage, and as a divine midwife who can help in childbirth or, if offended, bring about the death of the mother.

Worship and religion

Artemis was one of the most venerated deities in Greece. Her great temple at Ephesus, in Ionia, was counted among the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World before it was burnt to the ground, and her local cult remained popular until late antiquity. In Sparta she was revered as a warrior, with dances, processions of singing girls, ritual flogging of boys and competitions for cheese. Her sanctuaries were often located near springs or wetlands, and women offered clothing to her on the Acropolis to ask for a happy childbirth. Her role in female rites of passage, especially at her cult at Brauron, made her a guardian of the transition from childhood to adult life.

Legacy

Her Roman equivalent, Diana, carried her image into the western tradition. In the Hellenistic age she was identified with Selene, the goddess of the moon, which is why she is sometimes shown with a crescent moon above her head. In art she is represented with a bow, arrows and a quiver, often accompanied by deer, animals sacred to her. In literature she stands out as a fierce defender of her virginity. Her name has given rise to many linguistic hypotheses, although its etymology remains uncertain.

Ancient sources: Homer in the Iliad and the Odyssey, the Homeric Hymns, Hesiod, Aeschylus, Herodotus, Pausanias, Callimachus and Plato mention her, and the Mycenaean tablets from Pylos already record her name.
