Norse god of mischief and deception

Loki

Loki is a figure of Norse mythology, known as the master of deception among the Æsir. Born to giants, his ambiguous place between gods and jötnar makes him one of the most complex characters in the Eddas.

The myth

According to the tales, Loki is the son of the giant Farbauti and Laufey, and he has two brothers, Helblindi and Býleistr, about whom little is known. Despite his origin, he moved freely among the gods, and Odin even regarded him as a blood brother. His pranks and tricks often got the Æsir into trouble, but he also solved their problems: he cut off Sif's hair and later replaced it with the help of the dwarves, and he rescued Iðunn from the giant Þjazi.

Tradition also presents him as a great shapeshifter, able to take the form of a salmon, a fly or a mare. From that last union Sleipnir, Odin's eight-legged horse, was born. With the giantess Angrboda he fathered Fenrir, the serpent Jörmungandr and Hel. With Sigyn he had Narfi and Váli.

His decisive moment comes with the death of Baldr, which Loki engineers. The gods then bound him to three rocks, with a serpent dripping venom onto his face. The sources foretell that he will break free at Ragnarök and fight the gods at the head of the forces of the giants.

Worship and religion

There is no evidence of an ancient cult of Loki, and the texts barely assign him a religious role. Today some neopagan groups who call themselves Lokeans venerate him, although other pagan groups oppose this.

Legacy

Richard Wagner included him in his opera Das Rheingold under the name Loge. His name has been linked to the fire giant Logi, which gave him a reputation as a fire spirit, though scholars consider this a linguistic confusion. He also appears in many modern works of popular culture.

Ancient sources: He appears in the Poetic Edda, in Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda, in skaldic poetry and in the Hyndluljóð.

Trivia

Name and cobweb. Some scholars connect his name to a Germanic root linked to loops and knots, which would tie him to the net and to his role at Ragnarök.
An eating contest. In the Prose Edda, Loki competes with a jötunn named Logi in an eating contest, and Logi devours even the bones and the plate.
