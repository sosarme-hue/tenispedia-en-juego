Old Norse poem about Fródi's magic mill

Grottasöngr (Song of Grótti)

Grottasöngr, or the Song of Grótti, is an Old Norse poem that explains why the sea is salty. It survives in a manuscript of Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda, alongside the story that gives it context.

The myth

According to the tradition, the Danish king Fróði, during the so-called peace of Fróði, bought two enslaved giantesses, Fenja and Menja, in Sweden. He tied them to a magic mill called Grótti and made them grind gold, peace and happiness for him, giving them almost no rest.

In revenge, they sang a song that summoned an army led by the sea-king Mysing. He attacked Fróði at night, killed him, and carried off the mill and the giantesses, forcing them to grind salt. When his ship sank under the weight of the salt, the sea rushed through the millstone and formed a whirlpool, and ever since the sea has been salty.

Legacy

In Sweden, Viktor Rydberg modernised it in his poem Den nya Grottesången, which criticised the harsh working conditions in nineteenth-century factories. Johannes V. Jensen included it in his novel The Fall of the King, and Ann Leckie drew inspiration from it for The Raven Tower.

Ancient sources: It survives in a manuscript of Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda and appears in many later editions of the Poetic Edda.

Trivia

Origin of the word maelstrom. The word maelstrom, now used for a great sea whirlpool, is explained in the sources as coming from mal (mill) and ström (stream).
Witches of Orkney and Shetland. In Orkney and Shetland folklore, the giantesses appear as two witches, Grotti Finnie and Grotti Minnie, who create the Swelkie whirlpool in the Pentland Firth.
