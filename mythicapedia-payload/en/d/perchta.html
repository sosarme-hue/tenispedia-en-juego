Alpine Germanic folklore goddess

Perchta

Perchta, also called Berchta or Percht, is a female figure of folklore in the Germanic and Slovenian Alps. She supervised spinning and rewarded or punished children and young people according to their behaviour during Christmas.

The myth

According to tradition, Perchta roamed the fields in midwinter and entered homes during the twelve days between Christmas and Epiphany, above all on the last night, known as Rauhnacht. She left a silver coin in the shoe or bucket of well-behaved children. Lazy children and liars had their bellies slit open and stuffed with straw and stones. Spinning women who had not finished their flax were also at risk, and so was anyone who ate anything other than the fish and gruel of her feast night.

She was depicted either as a beautiful woman dressed in white or as an old woman with a hooked nose, and many old descriptions give her a single enormous foot, like a goose's or a swan's. Jacob Grimm read this as a sign of her higher nature and her ability to take animal form.

Worship and religion

According to medieval documents, some followers left food and drink for her and her retinue in exchange for wealth and abundance. This cult was condemned in Bavaria in 1468. Today her figure survives mainly in the festive folklore of the region.

Legacy

Her figure lives on in the mask parades of the Alps, where her followers, the Perchten, are played by men in animal costumes. In Italy, the Befana, who brings sweets or coal to children on the night of 5 to 6 January, is her closest equivalent.

Ancient sources: Medieval and ecclesiastical texts such as the Thesaurus pauperum (1468) and Thomas Ebendorfer's De decem praeceptis (1439), which condemned her cult; no pagan texts of her own mention her.

Trivia

A goose or swan foot. Several medieval languages have a Bertha with a large foot. Grimm linked it to the flat foot of the spinning women who worked the loom's treadle.
A day named after her. According to John B. Smith, Perchta may personify Epiphany, whose day, 6 January, was sometimes called Perchta's Day.
