legendary king of Iranian mythology

Kai Kobad

Kai Kobad (also Kay Qobad, in Avestan Kauui Kauuāta) is a legendary king of Iran's national history and, according to tradition, the founder of the Kayanian dynasty. His figure, passed down mainly through the Shahnameh, embodies the ruler who protects the land, organizes farming and defends the borders.

The myth

In the tradition preserved in the Shahnameh, Kai Kobad was a descendant of Manuchehr and lived in the Alborz mountains, from where Rustam brought him to Estaxr, the capital. When the Pishdadi dynasty weakened under Nowzar, who lost the royal glory by oppressing the Iranians, the country fell to the non-Iranian general Afrasiab, who killed Nowzar in battle.

Kai Kobad then led the Iranian forces. Rustam defeated Afrasiab and nearly captured him, and the enemy army was routed. For this feat, and because he possessed the royal glory, the Iranians elected him king, and the descendants of Nowzar, Zou, Garshasp and Gastham pledged him allegiance. According to the Shahnameh, his son was Kay Kavus.

His reign is described as prosperous: he had the fields cultivated, regulated river and spring water for irrigation, set the boundaries of the countries and collected tithes from crops to support the army. He is also portrayed as haughty and often at war with the Turks. Al-Tabari reports that at his coronation he spoke of subduing Turkish lands and caring for his own country, and he compares him to the Pharaoh for his self-aggrandizement.

Legacy

His name appears in the Shahnameh, the great Persian national epic, and is part of Iran's historical memory. The epithet 'Kay' was shared by other figures, and the name 'Kay Qobad' also designates the Sasanian king Kavad I, which causes confusion.

Ancient sources: The Shahnameh, the Bundahishn and the chronicles of Al-Tabari.

Trivia

Confusion with Kavad I. In the Shahnameh, the 'Kay' epithet does not always mean Kayanian origin: the Sasanian king Kavad I is also called Kay Qobad.
A harsh childhood. The Bundahishn says he was abandoned as a child on a river and froze on the door-sills; Auzobo found him, raised him and gave him his name.
