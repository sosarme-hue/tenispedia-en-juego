the first human couple in Norse mythology

Ask and Embla

Ask and Embla are, in Norse mythology, the first two human beings, created by the gods from two trees. Their story explains the origin of humankind and bears some resemblance to the account of Adam and Eve in the Bible, although its materials and motifs belong to Germanic and Scandinavian tradition.

The myth

According to the myth, the gods found two driftwood trunks on a beach, one of ash and one of elm, and shaped them into human form. The gifts they received vary from source to source. In the Poetic Edda, stanza 17 of the Völuspá tells how Hœnir, Lóðurr and Odin came upon the pair, who were almost helpless, and gave them three gifts whose meaning scholars still debate. In the Prose Edda, chapter 9 of Gylfaginning, it is Vili, Vé and Odin who create them: one gives breath of life, another movement and intelligence, and the third shape, speech, hearing and sight. The three gods also provide them with clothing and names.

The pair are then given a home within the walls of Midgard, the world of humans, and become the ancestors of all humanity. The name Ask means ‘ash tree’ and belongs to the man. The woman’s name, Embla, is less certain: ‘elm’ and ‘vine’ are the two main proposals, and philologists continue to disagree about which is correct.

Modern scholars have compared the story with other Indo-European myths of first couples, such as Yama and Yamī in the Vedas, and have noted that origin myths in which humans come from wood seem concentrated in Europe, Iran and Asia Minor, while the Near East more often uses clay or blood. The linguist Gunlög Josefsson proposes that Embla may mean something like ‘fire-maker’, linking it to the old practice of kindling fire with a fire plough. Others, with considerable caution, connect the story with the wooden Braak Bog Figures from Germany, which have been read as possible early divine images.

Legacy

The pair appear in A. S. Byatt’s novel Possession (1990), where a poem titled “Ask to Embla”, attributed to the poet R. H. Ash, quotes their names and story. In art, Stig Blomberg made a sculpture of the two in Sölvesborg, in southern Sweden, in 1948, and Dagfin Werenskiold depicts them on two of the sixteen wooden panels in Oslo City Hall. They also inspire part of the main story of the mobile game Fire Emblem Heroes, alongside other deities and tales from Norse mythology.

Ancient sources: The Völuspá and Snorri Sturluson’s Prose Edda, both medieval, record their creation; the Poetic Edda was compiled in the 13th century from earlier traditional sources.

Trivia

An echo among the Vandals. Paul the Deacon, writing in the 7th century, mentions the Vandal kings Assi and Ambri, whose names may derive from the same root as Embla.
A poem inside a novel. The poem “Ask to Embla” belongs to the fictional character R. H. Ash in A. S. Byatt’s Possession, which won the Booker Prize in 1990.
