Pandion I

Pandion I is, in Greek myth, the fifth legendary king of Athens, son of Erichthonius and the naiad Praxithea. He matters mainly as a link in the Athenian royal line and through the stories of his daughters, Procne and Philomela, whose tale survives in many versions. Myth portrays him as a rather unremarkable ruler. According to Apollodorus, the gods Demeter and Dionysus arrived in Attica during his reign.

The myth

Tradition says Pandion married Zeuxippe, his maternal aunt, and had two sons, Erechtheus and Butes, and two daughters, Philomela and Procne. Some accounts also make Cephalus, the lover of Eos, and Teuthras, father of Thespius, his descendants.

His reign was marked by a border war with Labdacus, king of Thebes. To secure help in the fighting, he married his daughter Procne to Tereus. During his rule, according to Apollodorus, Demeter was welcomed at Eleusis by Celeus, and Dionysus by Icarius, who received a vine from the god and learned to make wine.

He is said to have died of grief on learning that his two daughters had died. Erechtheus succeeded him on the throne, while Butes received the priesthoods of Poseidon Erechtheus and Athena.

The sources do not agree. Roman mythographers distinguished this Pandion from a second Pandion, perhaps to fill out the chronology of Athenian kings, while Greek sources know only one. M. L. West proposes reconstructing the original Pandion as a son of Cecrops.

Legacy

His name gave rise to that of the Attic tribe Pandionis, and he is often identified with the eponymous hero of that tribe.

Ancient sources: Apollodorus, in his Bibliotheca, records his reign, the war with Thebes and the story of Procne; Hesiod and Sappho name him as father of the swallow, the double of Philomela.
