prodigious fox of Greek mythology

Teumessian fox

The Teumessian fox is a prodigious fox of the Theban cycle, fated never to be caught. Its story, tied to the hound Laelaps and to Cephalus, explains a pair of stone figures and, in other versions, two constellations.

The myth

According to a fragment of the Epigoni summarized by Photius, the gods sent the fox to punish the descendants of Cadmus, and the Thebans excluded that house from the throne. Its name comes from the people of Teumessus. No source gives it parents. The text never specifies its sex; the feminine is a convention of modern translations.

Cephalus, son of Deion, owned the hound Laelaps, which never let its quarry escape. After accidentally killing his wife Procris and being purified, he loosed it against the fox; when they met, both were turned to stone near Teumessus. Apollodorus says the dog was a gift from Minos to Procris and that Zeus petrified them.

Other tellings vary the origin: Dionysus may have sent the beast, or Artemis may have given the hound to Procris. Antoninus Liberalis adds that the Thebans had to offer a child every thirty days. Hyginus explains the stone by a paradox: a dog that always catches and a fox that is never caught. Ovid places the fox after the death of the Sphinx and ends both animals as marble statues.

Legacy

In his 1921 translation of the Bibliotheca, Frazer called it the «Cadmean vixen», after Cadmus. In ancient astronomy, the hound and the fox are linked to Canis Major and Canis Minor.

Ancient sources: Fragment of the Epigoni (cited by Photius), Apollodorus, Pausanias, Antoninus Liberalis, Hyginus and Ovid.

Trivia

A translator's name. Frazer called it the «Cadmean vixen» in his 1921 translation, although the Greek texts do not use that name.
A fox without a sex. The Greek texts never specify the animal's sex, although modern translations usually use the feminine.
