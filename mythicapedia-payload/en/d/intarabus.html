Gallic god of the Treveri

Intarabus

Intarabus was a Gallic god worshipped by the Treveri and some neighbouring peoples, in what are now Belgium, Luxembourg, western Germany and eastern France. He is known from nine inscriptions.

The myth

The surviving texts tell almost no stories about Intarabus, so nearly everything we know comes from inscriptions and a statuette. He is shown as a beardless, long-haired man in a tunic, draped in a wolf skin, with one raised hand that may once have held a spear.

He may have been the protective deity of one of the three pagi of the Treveri. He is usually invoked alone, without being merged with a Roman god or accompanied by goddesses. One inscription from Trier calls him Mars Intarabus.

Worship and religion

At Mackwiller there was a sanctuary linked to a local spring, dedicated to Narius Intarabus from the 1st century CE. In the 2nd century CE a mithraeum was built there, and inscriptions show the shared worship of Mithras and Narius Intarabus. In the second half of the 3rd century the mithraeum was replaced by a Gaulish-style temple enclosing the sacred spring, which Helmut Birkhan reads as a return to older Celtic local deities. Indications of a theatre at Echternach and an aedicula at Ernzen dedicated to him also survive.

Ancient sources: Nine Gallo-Roman inscriptions, including one from Trier that calls him Mars Intarabus and one from Mackwiller that gives him the epithet Narius.

Trivia

Bronze statuette. A small bronze statuette found at Foy-Noville shows the god wearing a wolf skin, and its base is inscribed with his name in the dative case.
A temple replaces a mithraeum. At Mackwiller, the cult of Mithras gave way to a Gaulish-style temple enclosing the sacred spring, a sign of a return to local deities.
A name between rivers. His name is etymologically obscure, although Xavier Delamarre interprets it as entar-abus, meaning "between rivers".
