hero and king of Mesopotamian mythology

Gilgamesh

Gilgamesh is a hero of ancient Mesopotamian mythology and the protagonist of the Epic of Gilgamesh, an epic poem written in Akkadian in the late second millennium BC. He may have been a real king of the Sumerian city of Uruk who was deified after his death, and his story blends royal power with a deeply human fear of mortality.

The myth

According to the tradition, Gilgamesh is the son of the goddess Ninsun and a priest named Lillah, although the Sumerian King List places him as the successor of Lugalbanda on the throne of Uruk, where he reigned for 126 years before passing power to his son Ur-Nungal. Sumerian legends portray him as a ruler of extraordinary strength who confronts the forces of the world.

The Akkadian epic tells how the people of Uruk, oppressed by their king, ask the gods for help, and the gods create Enkidu, a wild man. After an evenly matched struggle, the two become friends and set out together on journeys and exploits, such as defeating the giant Humbaba (Huwawa in Sumerian) and slaying the Bull of Heaven, which the goddess Inanna (Ishtar in Akkadian) sends against them after Gilgamesh rejects her love. The gods punish Enkidu with death for that victory.

Grief-stricken, Gilgamesh sets out to seek immortality and visits Utnapishtim, the survivor of the Flood, who explains that this gift was granted only once. To console him, Utnapishtim reveals a plant that restores youth, growing in the depths of the sea. Gilgamesh finds it, but a serpent steals it while he bathes. The hero returns to Uruk, understanding that immortality lies beyond his reach. Sumerian stories add other episodes, such as his revolt against Agga of Kish or the loss of the pikku and mikku, and a fifth poem recounts his death and funeral.

Worship and religion

Some Sumerian texts indicate that Gilgamesh came to be worshipped as a god in various places in Sumer, and King Utu-hengal of Uruk adopted him as his patron deity in the 21st century BC. The kings of the Third Dynasty of Ur called him their "divine brother" and "friend", and some prayers on clay tablets invoke him as a judge of the dead in the Underworld. No evidence of a surviving cult today is recorded.

Legacy

The Epic of Gilgamesh, rediscovered in the Library of Ashurbanipal in 1849, caused great controversy in the nineteenth century because of its similarities with passages of the Hebrew Bible. Since the late twentieth century, Gilgamesh has become an increasingly prominent figure in modern culture. The epic is considered to have influenced the Iliad and the Odyssey, and Gilgamesh served as a prototype for later heroes such as Heracles. His story still moves readers through its universal longing to escape death and its celebration of friendship.

Ancient sources: The Epic of Gilgamesh, composed in Akkadian by the scribe Sîn-lēqi-unninni during the Middle Babylonian period, and five Sumerian poems; the Greek writer Aelian, in the 2nd century AD, also records an anecdote about his birth.

Trivia

A birth saved by an eagle. According to Aelian, Gilgamesh's grandfather kept his mother under guard to prevent pregnancy, because an oracle had said his grandson would overthrow him. The guards threw the child from a tower, but an eagle caught him mid-fall.
An ending still under debate. According to press reports, in 2001 the Assyriologist Giovanni Pettinato allegedly read a different ending on tablets from Iraq, in which Gilgamesh buried himself alive with eighty members of his court.
