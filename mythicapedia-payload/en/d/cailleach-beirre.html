Celtic goddess of winter

Cailleach

The Cailleach is a divine hag of Gaelic myth, linked to winter, storms and the shaping of the landscape. Her name means 'old woman' or 'hag', and she is especially remembered in Ireland, Scotland and the Isle of Man, where she is often blended with Brigit.

The myth

In the stories, the Cailleach is a giantess with blue-grey skin, one eye in the middle of her forehead, bear's teeth and boar's tusks. She wears grey, carries a quiver of golden arrows and a elder-wood bow, and is said to have shaped mountains and valleys as she strode across the land, dropping rocks from her creel.

Accounts differ. Some show her hunting wolves, boars and deer; others show her guiding hunters to their prey. With a rowan-free holly wand she withers the leaves in early autumn and turns to stone when spring arrives. She is said to wash her great plaid in the Gulf of Corryvreckan, and snow covers the land when she is done. According to Mackenzie, on the longest night of the year she drinks from the Well of Youth and grows younger day by day.

The most widespread belief identifies her with Brigit, goddess of fire, poetry and spring. In some versions, the winter hag turns to stone or sails to Avalon to eat from the tree of eternal youth, returning as Brigit for Imbolc. In others, the Cailleach holds Brigit captive until the festival frees her. Tradition also gives her many children and says her descendants became whole peoples.

Worship and religion

In Scotland and Ireland, the first farmer to finish the grain harvest made a corn dolly representing the Cailleach from the last sheaf. The last farmer to finish had to keep it through the year, so no one wanted that burden. Her ritual role survives mainly in folklore and in spring festivals such as St Brigid's day.

Legacy

She lives on in place names and landscape: Hag's Head on the Cliffs of Moher and the Loughcrew megalithic tombs are linked to her. In Ireland and Scotland the word is also used for 'witch', and some have suggested a link with the Sheela na Gig carvings.

Ancient sources: The 8th- to 9th-century Irish poem The Lament of the Old Woman names her and describes her many foster-children and her lineage.

Trivia

The corn dolly day. The last family to finish the harvest had to keep the Cailleach figure all winter. Nobody wanted to be that farmer.
A name with many readings. Some scholars link the word to a meaning like 'sharp' or 'shrill' rather than 'veiled one'.
