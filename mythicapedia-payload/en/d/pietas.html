Roman goddess of piety and duty

Pietas

Pietas is the divine personification of piety, one of the most cherished virtues of ancient Rome, especially loyalty to parents, ancestors and the homeland. She often appears on Roman coins and is linked to the hero Aeneas.

The myth

Pietas is, above all, a human virtue rather than a character in stories. The distinguishing virtue of the founding hero Aeneas, whom Virgil calls pius in the Aeneid, is piety, and in the fall of Troy he carries his father Anchises on his back out of the burning city. That image was later repeated in coin designs.

The goddess Pietas embodies the sacred nature of this virtue. The Greek equivalent is Eusebeia. According to Cicero, piety urges us to do our duty to our country, our parents and other blood relations, and whoever has it fulfills his obligations to both the gods and other people.

Roman tradition held that the Romans were the most pious of humans and that the gods therefore helped them. For this reason, after defeating a people they invited that people's gods to Rome to be honored, in a rite known as evocatio.

Worship and religion

Pietas had a temple in Rome, the Temple of Piety, vowed by the consul Manius Acilius Glabrio at the Battle of Thermopylae and completed by his son; it held a gold statue of the father. Nearby stood a sacred site linked to the story of an imprisoned woman who survived on her daughter's breast milk. Sacrifices and libations symbolized piety in civic religion.

Legacy

Pietas appears on Roman coins from denarii issued by Marcus Herennius, and later on imperial issues, including those under Hadrian. The stork was her symbol, admired for caring for its parents. Metellus Pius took the cognomen Pius to highlight his filial piety, starting a practice of using 'Pius' in Roman nomenclature.

Ancient sources: Virgil links her virtue to the hero Aeneas in the Aeneid, and Cicero defines piety in his moral writings; Petronius calls the stork pietaticultrix.

Trivia

The stork. The Romans believed the stork returned to the same nest every year and cared for its parents in old age, making it a symbol of filial piety.
Aeneas and Anchises. The image of a son carrying his father on his back appears on Roman coins and in the scene of Aeneas fleeing Troy.
