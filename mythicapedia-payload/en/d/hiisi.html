Finnic spirit of wild places

Hiisi

Hiisi are a class of spirits in Finnish and Finnic mythology. They first denoted sacred sites and later became malevolent beings, similar to trolls or giants. They matter because they embody the contrast between wild nature and cultivated fields.

The myth

According to tradition, hiisi dwell in the wild corners of the landscape: cliffs, crevasses, caves, boulder fields and lonely hills. Originally the word denoted sacred places. After Finland and Estonia were Christianized, between the 12th and 13th centuries, their nature grew darker until it came to resemble that of the Christian devil.

In oral folklore they are malicious spirits, sometimes tiny and sometimes gigantic, who travel in noisy processions, attack anyone who does not make way and slip through open doors to steal. Anyone chased by them should seek refuge in a cultivated field, since a hiisi cannot enter ground sanctified by cultivation. In the Kalevala they appear as a chief with a retinue, and one of Kaleva's twelve sons bears the name too. According to the epic, the twelve sons were transformed into the constellations.

Worship and religion

According to Bishop Mikael Agricola, in his list of Finnish pagan gods, Hiisi was a lord of forest game and furs, sharing this role with Tapio. Reindeer hunters in Kainuu offered worship to him every first weekend of July until the 1960s, when the celebration turned into dancing and drinking.

Legacy

His name survives in place names such as Hiisimäki and the village of Hiitola, and in hiidenkiuas, the Finnish term for prehistoric stone-cairn graves. In Finnish magic songs it served as a prefix for animals and ailments: 'Hiisi's hound' denotes a disease and 'Hiisi's horse' a fine steed.

Ancient sources: Bishop Mikael Agricola mentions him in his list of Finnish pagan gods, and later collected folk spells invoke him frequently.

Trivia

Giants throwing rocks. In a tale from Vesilahti, giants threw rocks into the sea to stop people from travelling by boat to church. The cape of Hiidennokka was said to have been formed this way.
Cauldrons of coins. According to local tradition, fishermen caught cauldrons full of coins that had rolled down the cliff at Hiidennokka.
