Greek god of the east wind

Eurus

Eurus is the Greek god of the east wind, one of the four principal wind gods known as the Anemoi, alongside his brothers Boreas, Zephyrus and Notus. He matters because he is one of the least featured winds in ancient stories, rarely having a myth of his own.

The myth

According to the tradition, the winds lived on the island of Aeolia, where Zeus had made Aeolus their keeper. Aeolus gave Odysseus a bag holding all the winds except the gentle Zephyrus, who was to carry the ships home. Odysseus's crew, thinking it held treasure, opened it and released Eurus and the others, who blew the ships back to Aeolia. Later, Eurus and Notus stranded Odysseus for a month on Thrinacia, the island of Helios, and after he left Calypso, Poseidon unleashed all four winds to drown him.

Outside the Odyssey he barely appears. Hesiod, who makes the beneficial winds children of Eos and Astraeus, does not name him and speaks instead of Argestes. It was Nonnus of Panopolis, in his Dionysiaca in the fifth century AD, who made him a son of Eos and Astraeus and showed him serving nectar to Demeter at his father's home.

Worship and religion

The earliest evidence is two Mycenaean tablets in Linear B, KN Fp 1 and KN Fp 13, which mention a "priestess of the winds". In later times traces of his worship appear alongside the Winds: a sanctuary in Titane in Corinthia, a tradition in Sparta that called him the "saviour of Sparta", an altar in Coronea, and remains in Attica. There is no evidence that this cult survives today.

Legacy

On the great Pergamon Altar, which depicts the battle of the gods against the Giants, the four winds appear in the shape of horses pulling Hera's chariot. For the Romans, Eurus was identified with the god Vulturnus, linked to dry, warm weather, and he was occasionally called Africanus.

Ancient sources: Homer's Odyssey, which names him in the episode of Aeolus, and the Dionysiaca of Nonnus of Panopolis.

Trivia

A nearly forgotten wind. Hesiod discusses the principal winds without naming Eurus, and the Dionysiaca is one of the few sources that gives him a family.
Saviour of Sparta. In Sparta he was remembered as the city's saviour, an unusual title for a wind.
