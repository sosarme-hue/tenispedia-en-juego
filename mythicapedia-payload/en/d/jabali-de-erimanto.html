Erymanthian Boar

The Erymanthian Boar is a monstrous beast from Greek mythology whose capture was one of the labours of Heracles. It matters because the episode combines the hero's strength, the authority of Eurystheus and the story's ties to Arcadia.

The myth

According to tradition, the boar lived on Mount Erymanthus in Arcadia, from where it descended to ravage the lands of Psophis, which is how it got its name. It was bigger and stronger than any other boar.

Eurystheus ordered Heracles to bring it alive to Mycenae as the fourth of his twelve labours. After the death of the centaur Pholus, the hero chased it with shouts until it left its thicket, then wore it out by driving it into deep snow. He trapped it, bound it in chains and carried it on his shoulder to Mycenae. When Eurystheus saw him arriving with the beast, he was terrified and hid in a bronze jar.

Accounts differ. Some authors place it as the third labour rather than the fourth, and others say Heracles left the boar tied at the entrance to the Mycenaean assembly so he could join the Argonauts at Jason's request, against Eurystheus's plans.

Legacy

Some accounts claimed that boar tusks preserved in Apollo's sanctuary at Cumae were those of the Erymanthian Boar, a claim the historian Diodorus of Sicily regarded as wholly improbable.

Ancient sources: It appears in Apollonius Rhodius, Pindar, Apollodorus, Pausanias, Ovid, Seneca and Statius, among other Greek and Latin authors.

Trivia

A disputed labour. Not all authors agree on the order of Heracles' labours. Some place the capture of the boar third.
A bronze hiding place. Eurystheus hid in a bronze jar when he saw the hero arrive carrying the boar on his back.
