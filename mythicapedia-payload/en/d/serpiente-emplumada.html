Mesoamerican feathered serpent deity

Feathered Serpent

The Feathered Serpent is a deity found in many Mesoamerican cultures, known as Quetzalcoatl among the Aztecs and Kukulkan among the Maya. It joins the earthbound serpent with the sky-reaching feather, making it a symbol of the dual nature of Mesoamerican gods.

The myth

Its earliest associations were with water. Over the centuries it took on powers of wind, rain and knowledge, and split into local forms worshipped in different communities.

Among the Mixtecs it was known as Nine Wind. After rising to the sky and receiving the insignia of the creator gods, it came down to teach people the foundations of civilization, and its figure legitimized the ruling lineages.

Worship and religion

Its worship was especially visible at Teotihuacan, where feathered serpents decorate the Temple of Quetzalcoatl. The Mixtecs venerated it in caves, while its role among the Olmecs is less clear.

Legacy

Its image marks the architecture and pottery of Mesoamerica, and it appears in Aztec codices and in the accounts of the Spanish conquistadors.

Ancient sources: The Florentine Codex records Quetzalcoatl among the Aztecs, the Popol Vuh names Tepeu Q'uq'umatz as creator of the cosmos, and the Codex Vindobonensis documents Nine Wind among the Mixtecs.

Trivia

Linked to Venus. Quetzalcoatl was associated with the planet Venus, which Mesoamerican peoples followed closely in the sky.
Creator in the Popol Vuh. In the Popol Vuh, the K'iche' god Tepeu Q'uq'umatz appears as the creator of the cosmos.
