Etruscan goddess of dawn, divination and childbirth

Thesan

Thesan is the Etruscan goddess of the dawn, linked also to divination and childbirth. The Etruscans identified her with the Greek Eos and the Roman Aurora, and she appears often on decorated bronze mirrors.

The myth

In Etruscan tradition, Thesan brought the light of day, and she was shown with wings and sometimes nude. Her name is tied to words meaning "dawn", "illumination" and "divination", ideas the Etruscans joined: just as dawn lights what was dark, divination brings clarity to the future. Because she appeared at the start of the day, she was also linked to childbirth, paralleling the beginning of a newborn's life.

The Etruscans borrowed Greek stories about Eos, whom Aphrodite punished with an insatiable desire for young mortals. On mirrors, Thesan carries off Cephalus, a hunter married to Procris, holding him in her arms. Another tale concerns Memnon, her son by Tithonus, killed in the Trojan War; Eos's grief for him was said to explain the morning dew. In another scene she appears with Tinia (Zeus) and Thetis, pleading for the lives of their sons, Memnun and Achle.

Worship and religion

Thesan was worshipped at Pyrgi, the harbour of Caere, where a temple was dedicated to her and dawn-themed antefixes were excavated. The Liber Linteus records offerings to her alongside the sun god Usil.

Legacy

Her image appeared on Etruscan mirrors, pottery and tomb paintings, and she has served as a model for comparing other Mediterranean dawn goddesses.

Ancient sources: The Etruscan Liber Linteus mentions her alongside Usil; her stories largely come from Greek tradition about Eos, transmitted through the mirrors.

Trivia

Bronze mirrors. Many of her scenes are known from Etruscan bronze mirrors with engraved reliefs on their backs.
Tears of dew. According to the story, Eos weeps every morning for her son Memnon, and her tears form the dew.
