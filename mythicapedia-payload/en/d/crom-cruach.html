pre-Christian Irish god of fertility

Crom Cruach

Crom Cruach was a pagan god of pre-Christian Ireland, linked to fertility and, in some interpretations, to the sun. Medieval Christian writers describe him as a god who received human sacrifice in exchange for good harvests and livestock.

The myth

According to a 12th-century dinsenchas poem in the Book of Leinster, his cult image, a gold figure surrounded by twelve stone figures, stood on Magh Slécht in County Cavan. There, firstborn children were offered in return for milk and grain, a rite the poem dates back to the time of Érimón.

Tradition holds that the High King Tigernmas died with three quarters of his army while worshipping him on the eve of Samhain. Later, Saint Patrick was said to have destroyed the image with a sledgehammer, or with his crozier in the Tripartite Life of the 9th century, although these episodes do not appear in the saint's own writings.

Worship and religion

The sacrifice of firstborn children and the requests for good harvests suggest a cult tied to fertility. A festival called Domhnach Crom Dubh, Crom Dubh's Sunday, was associated with him, though the sources do not say whether it survived.

Legacy

A decorated stone found at Killycluggin in 1921 has been linked to his cult image, and a street in Drumcoo is named Crom Crúaich Way. He also appears in an episode of the series Robin of Sherwood, titled 'Cromm Cruac'.

Ancient sources: The 12th-century dinsenchas poem in the Book of Leinster, the 9th-century Tripartite Life of Saint Patrick, and Jocelin's Life and Acts of St Patrick.

Trivia

A name with several meanings. Crom can mean 'bent' and cenn 'head'. Cruach refers to a pile or heap, of grain or hay, so the name has been read as 'head of the heap' or 'bloody head'.
The Killycluggin Stone. It was found broken into several pieces and was later reconstructed. Today it is kept in the County Cavan Museum, and a replica stands near the find spot.
