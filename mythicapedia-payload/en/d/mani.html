Norse god of the Moon

Máni

Máni is the personification of the Moon in Norse mythology and the brother of Sól, the Sun. He appears in the Poetic Edda and the Prose Edda, and his name simply means “Moon” in Old Norse.

The myth

According to the tradition, Máni and Sól are the children of Mundilfari, a man whose children were so beautiful that he named them “Moon” and “Sun”. The gods saw this as arrogance and placed the siblings in the sky, where Máni guides the Moon’s path and controls its waxing and waning.

Máni is followed across the heavens by the siblings Hjúki and Bil, whom he took from the earth while they were fetching water from a well. This episode appears in the Gylfaginning, the part of the Prose Edda written by Snorri Sturluson.

Wolves chase the heavenly bodies: Hati Hróðvitnisson pursues Máni, and at Ragnarök both will be devoured. The Vafþrúðnismál explains that they must travel the sky every day so that people can count the years. In the Alvíssmál, the dwarf Alvíss lists the Moon’s names in each world: “moon” for humans, “fiery one” for the gods, and “the wheel” in Hel.

Legacy

Scholars connect the story of Máni, Hjúki and Bil with the Man in the Moon, a figure in folklore from Scandinavia, England and northern Germany, in which a man with a pole and a woman with a bushel are seen on the lunar surface. Rudolf Simek also notes that a stanza of the Völuspá says the Æsir set up the Moon to reckon the year, which he links to a Germanic way of measuring time by the Moon and by nights rather than days. The Prose Edda also gives the Moon a long list of names in the Skáldskaparmál, including “year-counter” and “shiner”.

Ancient sources: The Poetic Edda (Völuspá, Vafþrúðnismál, Grímnismál and Alvíssmál) and Snorri Sturluson’s Prose Edda, especially the Gylfaginning and the Skáldskaparmál.

Trivia

Eleven names for the Moon. In the Alvíssmál, the dwarf Alvíss gives a different name for the Moon depending on the world: humans call it “moon”, elves call it “year-counter” and dwarves call it “the shiner”.
The Man in the Moon. Scholars compare the story of Máni, Hjúki and Bil with the popular belief in the Man in the Moon, found in Scandinavia, England and northern Germany.
