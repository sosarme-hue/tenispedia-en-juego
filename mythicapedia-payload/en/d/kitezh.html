legendary city beneath Lake Svetloyar in Russian folklore

Kitezh

Kitezh is a legendary city of Russian folklore which, according to the tradition, sank beneath the waters of Lake Svetloyar to escape the Mongol invasion. Its story, strongly shaped by Christian belief, became popular among educated Russians and has inspired operas, paintings and literature.

The myth

According to the legend, Prince Yuri II, Grand Prince of Vladimir, first built a town called Little Kitezh on the Volga and later founded Great Kitezh on the shores of Lake Svetloyar. It was home mostly to monks and Christian clergy, and its white stone walls and golden domes made it famous.

When Batu Khan's Mongols captured Little Kitezh, the prince fled into the forest. A traitor showed the invaders the secret paths to the lake. Arriving at the town, its unarmed people began to pray. Countless springs then burst from the ground, the water rose, and the city sank beneath the lake, leaving only a cathedral dome with a cross as the last sight. Other versions, such as the opera by Rimsky-Korsakov, say the city became invisible in the mists.

Legacy

Rimsky-Korsakov's 1907 opera The Legend of the Invisible City of Kitezh and the Maiden Fevroniya was based on the story, and painters Mikhail Nesterov and Konstantin Gorbatov also depicted it. Werner Herzog searched for the city in the documentary Bells from the Deep (1993), and Anna Akhmatova wrote a poem about it. The lake is sometimes called the Russian Atlantis.

Ancient sources: The earliest known reference appears in the Kitezh Chronicle, an anonymous manuscript linked to the Old Believers, and in an apocryphal work titled A Tale and Lament of the Hidden City of Kitezh.

Trivia

Russian Atlantis. Lake Svetloyar is sometimes called the Russian Atlantis because of the legends about the sunken city.
Path of Batu. The road to the lake is still called the Path of Batu, after the Mongol leader of the legend.
