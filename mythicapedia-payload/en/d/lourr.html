Norse god, one of the Æsir

Lóðurr

Lóðurr is a god in Norse mythology, counted among the Æsir, who appears almost only in the Völuspá, where he helps animate the first human beings. His name and identity remain open to scholarly debate.

The myth

In the Völuspá, from the Poetic Edda, Lóðurr takes part in the scene where the gods shape and give life to the first humans. He is credited with bestowing certain gifts whose exact meaning is unclear, and the phrase litu góða, traditionally rendered as «good looks», suggests that the gods made them handsome.

Outside that passage he is hardly mentioned in the ancient texts. His name also appears in skaldic poems, where «friend of Lóðurr» serves as a kenning for Odin, which fits his creative role. Snorri Sturluson, in the Prose Edda, leaves him out and credits the creation of humans to the sons of Borr, so his figure remains vague.

Legacy

His later presence is very slight. Some scholars have linked his name to Old Norse words meaning «fruit», «land» or «people», but the etymology is speculative and no consensus exists. Ursula Dronke, Jan de Vries and Georges Dumézil identified him with Loki, and the Icelandic scholar Haukur Þorgeirsson has defended the same identification based on the fifteenth-century rimur.

Ancient sources: The Völuspá, in the Poetic Edda, where he appears only once, and some skaldic poems that use his name as a kenning for Odin.

Trivia

Nordendorf fibula. A brooch from around 600 CE bears the runic inscription logaþore wodan wigiþonar. Some read it as naming a third god alongside Odin and Thor, and Lóðurr or Loki have been proposed, though the etymological basis is weak.
Loki in a Faroese ballad. The Loka Táttur, a ballad from the Faroe Islands, shows Odin, Hœnir and Loki as a trio, a rare appearance of Norse gods in folklore.
