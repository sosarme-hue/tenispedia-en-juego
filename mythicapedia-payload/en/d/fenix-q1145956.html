eponymous hero of Phoenicia in Greek mythology

Phoenix

Phoenix is a hero of Greek mythology and the eponym of Phoenicia, the land that tradition says he settled and named after himself. He matters because he links the legendary origin of the Phoenicians with the discovery of purple dye.

The myth

According to tradition, Phoenix was a son of King Agenor, and depending on the version his mother was Telephassa, Argiope, Antiope, Damno or Tyro. He was the brother of Europa, Cadmus and Cilix. When Zeus carried off Europa, Agenor sent his sons to find her, but the search failed. Phoenix eventually settled in a land that he named Phoenicia after himself.

The sources disagree about his family. In some early accounts Europa is his daughter rather than his sister, and Cadmus is his son. He is also credited with several wives, including Cassiopeia, Alphesiboea and Perimede, and with descendants such as Adonis, Cepheus and Phineus. One account says that Zeus, taking the form of Phoenix, seduced Cassiopeia.

The version recorded by John Malalas, a Byzantine chronicler, makes Phoenix the king of Tyre. In it, a philosopher called Heracles the Tyrian discovers purple dye when he sees a shepherd's dog chewing a small sea snail. Heracles gives the dyed wool to the king, who makes it a royal mantle and forbids anyone else to wear it, except himself and his successors.

Ancient sources: John Malalas, in his Byzantine chronicle; the Suda, which names Ogygus as a possible father; and the poet Moschus, who calls him the husband of Telephassa.

Trivia

The origin of purple. According to Malalas, purple dye was discovered when a dog was seen with its mouth stained after eating a sea mollusc. Heracles the Tyrian made the discovery.
A disguised father. One account in the sources says that Zeus took the form of Phoenix to seduce Cassiopeia, and that their union produced Anquinus.
