Greek personification of doom and death

Moros

Moros (Μόρος, "fate") is, in Greek mythology, the personified spirit of impending doom, the inevitable fate that drives mortals to death. He appears rarely, mostly in epic and tragic poetry, and Roman writers knew him as Fatum.

The myth

In Hesiod's Theogony, Moros was born from Nyx alone, alongside the grim Ker and Thanatos. Roman authors instead made him the son of Nyx and Erebus, so accounts of his father disagree. In Hesiod, the three siblings express three kinds of death: Moros is mortality as the general destiny of humans, Ker is violent death on the battlefield, and Thanatos is a natural, peaceful passing. As a child of Nyx, Moros is also brother to the Moirai, the Fates who portion out each life.

In the tragedies of Aeschylus, Moros functions more as a common noun than as a character. In Prometheus Bound, Prometheus claims he gave mortals hope so they would stop dwelling on death before their time. In The Suppliants, Moros is a terrible avenging god who does not release his victim even in Hades. A fragment of Aeschylus adds that neither battle nor staying safe at home can escape one's appointed fate. In later epic he appears alongside the Keres and Eris, delighting in slaughter.

Legacy

In Christian tradition, the Book of Revelation places him beside Thanatos as one of the riders of the pale horse.

Ancient sources: Hesiod (Theogony), Aeschylus (Prometheus Bound, The Suppliants and a fragment), later epic poetry, and Roman poets, who called him Fatum.

Trivia

Siblings of death. Moros shares his origin with Thanatos and the Keres, who represent different aspects of death.
A gift to humanity. In Prometheus Bound, Prometheus says he gave humans hope so they would ignore the inevitability of Moros.
Rider of the pale horse. In Revelation, Moros appears alongside Thanatos as a rider of the pale horse.
