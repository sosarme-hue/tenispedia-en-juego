first sign of the Chinese zodiac

Rat

The Rat is the first sign of the twelve-year cycle of the Chinese zodiac and is associated with keen intelligence, cunning and prosperity. Its place at the head of the cycle is explained by a popular story about a race of animals called by the Jade Emperor.

The myth

According to the most widespread popular tale, the Jade Emperor, ruler of Heaven, Earth and Hell, organised a contest to fix the order of the animals in the calendar. Among the candidates were the cat and the rat. The rat, wary of the cat's good looks, did not wake it, and the cat overslept and was shut out for good; that is why, the story goes, cats have hunted mice ever since.

During the contest, the rat stole a march by playing the flute on the back of the ox, which came second for allowing it. Impressed, the Emperor placed the rat at the head of the cycle. Chinese tradition also dates the first year to 2637 BCE, when the Prime Minister of the Yellow Emperor is said to have devised the sixty-year cycle, although the dates vary between sources. The race itself is a modern popular story rather than an ancient text.

Worship and religion

In the Indian town of Deshnoke, the temple of Karni Mata houses some 20,000 rats, believed to be reincarnations of the goddess and of her followers, the sadhus. Priests and pilgrims feed them grain and milk, and eating food touched by one of them is considered a blessing. In Hindu mythology the rat is the vehicle of the god Ganesha. In China the animal is still respected, although traditional astrology focused far more on the Heavenly Stems and Earthly Branches than on the animals themselves.

Legacy

In the West, calling someone a "rat" is a negative metaphor for a despicable person. The animal often appears in decorations for Chinese New Year, and on Chinese lunar coins and postage stamps from various countries. In Japanese it is called nezumi. In English, rat years are sometimes called mouse years, although Chinese makes no such distinction.

Trivia

Sacred rats. At the temple of Karni Mata, devotees consider it a blessing to eat food that the rats have touched.
One animal, two names. In English, rat years are sometimes called mouse years, though Chinese uses a single term for both.
