Andean deity of central and southern Peru

Staff God

The Staff God is a religious icon of the Peruvian Andes, shown facing forward and holding a staff in each hand. Its imagery spread across the region for millennia and shaped cultures such as Chavín, Wari and Tiwanaku, and it may have influenced Inca religion.

The myth

According to Andean tradition, the Staff God could turn anything into a staff and take on its power. The transformed object was then shared with its followers, providing sustenance to the peoples.

Its exact identity is unclear. Some scholars believe it was a forerunner of the main Inca gods such as the Sun, Moon and Thunder, or that it may be linked to Viracocha and Illapa, though there is no conclusive evidence. Other researchers argue that many of the figures associated with it may instead be ritual practitioners rather than deities.

Worship and religion

Its worship is attested from around 2250 BCE, based on gourd fragments found in the Pativilca River Valley. It spread to the Central Andes during the Middle Horizon (600-1000 CE).

Legacy

Its figure appears on the Raimondi Stela at Chavín de Huántar, on the Sun Gate at Tiwanaku, and on Wari and Tiwanaku textiles and ceramics.

Trivia

Oldest god image in the Americas. A depiction found in 2003 on gourd fragments, dated to around 2250 BCE, is the oldest known image of a deity on the continent.
Symbols of complementarity. An artifact from Chavín shows it holding Spondylus and Strombus shells, linked to female and male symbols respectively.
