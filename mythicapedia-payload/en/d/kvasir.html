Norse god of wisdom

Kvasir

Kvasir is a god of Norse mythology born from the saliva that the Æsir and the Vanir shared when they made peace. His blood, mixed with honey, produced the Mead of Poetry, which is why he is seen as a symbol of wisdom and inspiration.

The myth

According to Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda, after a war, both groups of gods sealed their peace by spitting into a vat. From that mixture they created Kvasir, so wise that he could answer any question, and he travelled the world teaching people.

Two dwarfs, Fjalar and Galar, invited him to their home and killed him. They collected his blood in three containers, mixed it with honey and produced a mead that turned whoever drank it into a poet or scholar. Later, Suttung, son of the murdered dwarfs' victims' kin, hid the brew inside a mountain under the watch of his daughter Gunnlod. Odin, after working a summer for Baugi, Suttung's brother, seduced the young woman into giving him three sips, drank it all and escaped as an eagle.

Kvasir also appears in the Prose Edda as the first to enter Loki's house, where he saw the shape of the net the god had woven in the ashes of the fire. That clue allowed the gods to catch Loki.

Legacy

In skaldic poetry, “Kvasir's blood” became a metaphor (a kenning) for poetry; the Icelandic skald Einarr skálaglamm used it in the 10th century. A Norwegian search engine bears his name. Scholars have linked him to the making of fermented drinks and to peacemaking customs among ancient peoples.

Ancient sources: Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda and Heimskringla (13th century), along with skaldic poetry such as Einarr skálaglamm's Vellekla.

Trivia

A net in the ash. In the Prose Edda, Kvasir recognises the shape of Loki's net in the ashes of the fire, something the other gods had not noticed.
A pact of spit. The peace between the Æsir and Vanir is sealed by spitting together into a vat, a gesture scholars compare with reconciliation customs of ancient peoples.
