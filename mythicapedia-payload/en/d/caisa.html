goddess of chess, muse of the game

Caïssa

Caïssa is a muse of chess, presented as a Thracian dryad, but she appears in no ancient source: she is a literary invention of the sixteenth and eighteenth centuries dressed in Greek clothes. She became popular as a symbol of good luck over the board.

The myth

The myth did not exist in antiquity. Her first appearance is in the Latin poem Scacchia Ludus, published in 1527 by Marco Girolamo Vida, where a chess dryad named Scacchia attends a game between Apollo and Mercury before the gods. Later, in 1763, William Jones wrote the English poem Caïssa in heroic couplets. In his version, Caïssa rejects the god of war, Mars, who asks Euphron, god of sport, for help to win her favour. Euphron invents chess as a gift.

In the legend that spread afterwards, Caïssa foresees the armies of the future and creates a game where intelligence and courage decide the outcome. The pieces receive their own gifts and curses: the pawns, bishops and king have limits, and the queen, made in the image of a goddess, is the most powerful. Caïssa hides the game, which falls to India. Years later she decides to protect it as its muse and leave it on Earth for good.

Legacy

Chess commentators cite her as a metaphor for luck. Garry Kasparov mentions her in his five-volume My Great Predecessors with the phrase 'Caïssa was with me'. The program Kaissa, which won the first World Computer Chess Championship in 1974, took its name from her.

Trivia

A computer named after her. The program Kaissa won the first World Computer Chess Championship in 1974.
Towers that became castles. Vida's description of rooks as towers on elephants led to the modern English name 'castle' for the rook and to the term 'castling'.
