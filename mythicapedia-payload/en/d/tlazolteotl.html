Aztec goddess of lust, purification and forgiveness

Tlazolteotl

Tlazolteotl is a goddess of the Aztec (Mexica) tradition, of Huastec origin, linked to sex, carnality and moral transgression. She matters because she unites contradictions: she inspires sin and sexually transmitted disease, yet she can also forgive and cleanse it.

The myth

According to Aztec stories, Tlazolteotl stirred forbidden desires, but she could also forgive and wash away sin. She was believed to cause disease, especially sexually transmitted infections, though she could also cure them. She was credited with inventing weaving and embroidery, and codices show her wearing spindles and a cotton headband.

Tradition also presents her as a mother. According to the Florentine Codex, she gave birth to the maize god Cintéotl with Piltzintecuhtli, which made her the female embodiment of procreation. Another legend links her to Xólotl and makes her the patron of the state of Texcoco. The Codex Matritense describes her with four sisters, known collectively as Ixcuina, although accounts of who they are vary.

Worship and religion

Tlazolteotl was one of the main deities honoured at the Ochpaniztli festival, held from 2 to 21 September with cleaning, sweeping, repairs, the scattering of maize seed, dances and military ceremonies. Her priests heard the confessions of penitents, who could receive this mercy only once in their lives. After choosing a suitable day by the ritual calendar, the priest imposed penances ranging from fasts to offerings or ritual songs and dances.

Ancient sources: The Borgia, Borbonicus and Matritense codices and Sahagún's Florentine Codex mention her.

Trivia

Her festival's name. Ochpaniztli means 'sweeping', and the festival's rituals of cleaning linked it to purification.
Symbolic excrement. Because sin was symbolised as dirt, she was believed to consume it and purify it; images show ochre marks of divine excrement around her mouth and nose.
