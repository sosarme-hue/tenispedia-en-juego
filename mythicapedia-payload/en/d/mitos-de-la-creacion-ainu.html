origin stories of Japan's Ainu people

Ainu creation myths

These are the traditional accounts of how the world began, told by the Ainu, an indigenous people of Japan. They share features with Japanese creation myths and with earth-diver stories found in Central Asia and among Native American cultures, yet they keep a distinct identity of their own.

The myth

According to Ainu tradition, time is divided into three stages: mosir sikah ohta (when the universe was born), mosir noskekehe (the centre of the world) and mosir kes (the end of the world, about which no detailed ideas are recorded).

The English missionary John Batchelor recorded a story in which, before the first kamuy created the world, there was only a vast swamp inhabited by a large trout. The creator placed the world on the fish, and when it swallows and spits out seawater, the tides result. In another version, the creator deity Kotan-kar-kamuy sends down a water wagtail to make habitable land in the watery world. The little bird flutters over the waters, pushing them aside and packing the earth firm with its feet and tail, raising the islands where the Ainu would later live. At first there was no vegetation. Humans were created later, and since there was no way to make fire, Kamuy-huci came down to give it to them.

Other traditions hold that the first ancestor was a bear or a wolf, since the Ainu are hairier than many other East Asian peoples. An alternative version tells of Kamuy sending a heavenly couple, Okikurumi and Turesh, to earth; their son, considered by some the first Ainu, taught the people the skills needed to survive.
