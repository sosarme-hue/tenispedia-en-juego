Achaean hero of the Trojan War, builder of the Trojan Horse

Epeius

Epeius is a hero of Greek mythology, son of Panopeus, who led thirty ships to the Trojan War. He is best remembered as the builder of the wooden horse through which the Achaeans entered the city.

The myth

According to tradition, Epeius was a man of prodigious strength, sometimes described as a giant. In the Iliad, at the funeral games for Patroclus, he won the boxing match against Euryalus. Later, at the funeral games for Achilles, he fought Acamas, son of Theseus, to a stalemate.

Most traditions credit Odysseus with the idea of the horse, although the Little Iliad says Epeius acted on the instructions of Athena, who had appeared to him in a dream. In the story, he felled timber on Mount Ida and built a hollow structure with openings in its sides, so tall that it could not pass through any of Troy's gates. Its trapdoor had a catch that only he could undo. Thirty warriors, fully armed, hid inside, and Epeius chose the other twenty-nine himself.

After the war, he sailed to Italy. According to Virgil, he founded the cities of Metapontum and Pisa there.

Worship and religion

According to one tradition, at Metapontum he dedicated to Artemis the tools he had used to build the horse. The Aristotelian Corpus, however, says the tools were kept as sacred offerings in a sanctuary of Athena near Metapontum. There is no evidence of a cult devoted to Epeius himself.

Legacy

In his Description of Greece, Pausanias describes a fresco by Polygnotus at Delphi showing the sack of Troy, in which Epeius is depicted naked, tearing down the city's walls.

Ancient sources: Homer's Iliad, the Little Iliad, Apollodorus's Bibliotheca and Pausanias's Description of Greece mention him; Virgil records his founding of cities in Italy.

Trivia

A fresco at Delphi. Pausanias described a painting by Polygnotus at Delphi in which Epeius appeared naked, tearing down the walls of Troy.
Sacred tools. The tools he used to build the horse were kept as offerings at sanctuaries near Metapontum, according to different traditions.
