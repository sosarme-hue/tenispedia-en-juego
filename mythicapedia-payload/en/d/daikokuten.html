Japanese god of fortune, abundance and trade

Daikokuten

Daikokuten is a syncretic Japanese deity of fortune, luck and wealth, descended from the Buddhist deity Mahākāla, who was himself linked to Shiva. He is the patron of farmers, cooks and those who work with money, and forms one of Japan's most popular divine pairs with Ebisu.

The myth

Mahākāla, whose name means "Great Black" or "Great Time", was originally an epithet of Shiva as the time that destroys all things. Buddhism adopted him as a protector of the dharma, but also as a terrifying figure who roams the forests at night with ghouls and demons. According to Buddhist accounts, he subdued the ḍākinīs, flesh-eating female demons, and made them feed only on the hearts of those close to death.

As the cult reached Japan through the esoteric Tendai and Shingon schools, the god gradually became a jovial, benevolent figure, often smiling, while his darker traits faded. Tradition credits Saichō, founder of the Tendai school, with bringing his cult to Japan. Legend says Mahākāla appeared to him as an old man on Mount Hiei and offered to guard the monastic community that would become Enryaku-ji.

In the medieval period the god was identified with the native kami Ōkuninushi, since the first two characters of that name can be read as "Daikoku". His sack, his rice bales, the golden mallet Uchide no Kozuchi and the mice that accompany him became part of his iconography.

Worship and religion

His cult spread through Japanese temples and shrines, where he was honoured as a giver of abundance, harvests and good fortune. A popular custom, Fuku-nubisi, holds that stealing small figures of gods at year-end markets brings luck, provided the thief is not caught. His veneration remains alive in Japan, especially around the New Year.

Legacy

He is prominent in Japanese art, where he is shown seated on rice bales, carrying a sack and a magic mallet, often accompanied by mice. As a deity of fortune, his image has spread to charms and lucky objects.

Trivia

A name with a double reading. The first two characters of Ōkuninushi's name can be read as "Daikoku", which helped identify him with Daikokuten in the medieval period.
A god who went from fearsome to smiling. Early Japanese images show him wrathful or stern, while later artworks almost always depict him smiling.
