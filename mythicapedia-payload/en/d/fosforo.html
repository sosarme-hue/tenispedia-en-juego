personification of the Morning Star in Greek and Roman mythology

Phosphorus

Phosphorus was the name by which the Greeks and Romans personified the Morning Star, the planet Venus seen before dawn. The name means "light-bringer", and in Latin the star was called Lucifer.

The myth

According to Hesiod, Phosphorus was a son of Astraeus and Eos, the goddess of dawn, though other writers make him a son of Cephalus and Eos, or of Atlas alone. Accounts also differ over his offspring: Ovid, who treats Phosphorus and Hesperus, the Evening Star, as identical, makes him the father of Daedalion and Ceyx. The grammarian Servius names him father of the Hesperides or of Hesperis, and a scholium mentions Stilbe, daughter of Eosphorus, who bore Autolycus to Hermes.

The tradition rests on what people could see in the sky: Venus is visible only for a short time before sunrise or after sunset, never high in the night. Some myths of morning-star deities who proudly strive for the highest place among the gods and are cast down may have this origin. The Greeks eventually accepted that Phosphorus and Hesperus were the same body, yet they continued to treat them as distinct mythological figures.

Legacy

In the philosophy of language, the sentence "Hesperus is Phosphorus" became famous: Gottlob Frege used it to illustrate the distinction between sense and reference, and Saul Kripke used it to argue that some necessary truths can be discovered a posteriori. In Latin, Lucifer designated the Morning Star in both prose and poetry. In the Bible, the Vulgate uses lucifer to translate the Hebrew helel in Isaiah 14, a passage later read as a reference to Satan, although modern translations usually render it as "morning star" or "day star".

Ancient sources: Hesiod and Ovid mention him in their works, and the grammarian Servius includes him in his commentaries on Virgil.
