first woman in Greek mythology

Pandora

Pandora is the first mortal woman in Greek mythology, created by Hephaestus on Zeus's orders as a punishment for humanity after Prometheus stole fire. She is best known for the jar she opened, which released evils upon mankind.

The myth

According to the myth, Zeus, angry that Prometheus had given fire to humans, decided to give them a deadly gift. He ordered Hephaestus to shape a maiden from earth, and each god contributed something: Athena dressed her and taught her weaving, Aphrodite gave her charm and a consuming longing, and Hermes gave her a deceitful nature and the power of speech. She was named Pandora, "all-gift", because every Olympian had presented her with a gift.

Hesiod describes her in the Theogony without naming her, as the origin of the race of women and a "beautiful evil". In Works and Days he tells her story in more detail: Prometheus warned his brother Epimetheus not to accept any gifts from Zeus, but Epimetheus took her as his wife. Pandora lifted the lid of a great jar that held the evils, which spread across the world, and only Hope remained inside, because the lid closed before it could escape.

Other versions claim the jar actually held blessings that flew back to heaven, leaving only hope among men. The image of the jar with good and evil already appears in the Iliad. According to the Catalogue of Women, Pandora was also the mother of Graecus, though it is unclear whether this is the same figure.

Legacy

The story of the jar changed over time. In the sixteenth century Erasmus of Rotterdam translated the Greek word pithos (a jar) as pyxis (a box), and from this came the phrase "Pandora's box", now used for any source of unforeseen trouble. Her story has inspired poets, playwrights, painters and sculptors throughout history.

Ancient sources: Hesiod's Theogony, Works and Days and Catalogue of Women, as well as later mythographers such as the Bibliotheca and Hyginus.

Trivia

Another name. A white-ground kylix in the British Museum has the name Anesidora inscribed beside her figure.
A jar, not a box. Before the Renaissance the container was correctly translated as a jar, and was said to hold blessings for humanity.
