mythical king of Dardania and Troy

Tros

Tros was, according to Greek tradition, the king of Dardania and the founder of the Trojan kingdom, which took its name from him. He links Dardanus and Erichthonius with Anchises and Aeneas, and he was the father of Ganymede, the youth carried off by Zeus.

The myth

According to Homer, Tros was the son of Erichthonius and the father of three outstanding sons: Ilus, Assaracus and Ganymede, the most beautiful of mortal men. The gods took Ganymede up to serve as cupbearer to Zeus and to live among the immortals. Apollodorus gives another version: Erichthonius married Astyoche, daughter of the river Simoeis, and from this union Tros was born, who took Callirrhoe, daughter of the river Scamander, as his wife. With her he had a daughter, Cleopatra, besides the three sons. Other authors name a different wife, Acallaris, daughter of Eumedes.

When Zeus carried off Ganymede, Tros grieved for his son. To console him, the god sent Hermes with a pair of fine horses, swift enough to run over water and over standing ears of grain, and promised that the boy would be immortal. These horses, of the same breed as those of Zeus, became the finest in the world. Anchises secretly crossed them with his mares without Laomedon's knowledge, and Aeneas used them in war; later Diomedes yoked them to his chariot.

According to the tradition, Tantalus, king of the Phrygians, abducted Ganymede out of desire, and for this Tros declared war on him. Ilus, son of Tros, later drove Tantalus out of Paphlagonia. The Dardanians came to be called Trojans after Tros, and the region was called the Troad.

Ancient sources: Homer (Iliad), Apollodorus (Bibliotheca) and Dionysius of Halicarnassus, who place him in the genealogy of the Trojans.

Trivia

The horses of Zeus. The horses Zeus gave to Tros were so swift that, according to the myth, they could run over water and over standing grain. Later, Diomedes yoked them to his chariot.
The name of the Trojans. The Dardanians came to be called Trojans, and the region the Troad, after Tros, according to the ancient sources.
