Yoruba god of thunder, lightning and justice

Shango

Shango (Ṣàngó) is an orisha of the Yoruba pantheon associated with thunder, lightning, fire, justice, dance and virility. According to tradition, he was the third Alaafin of the Oyo Empire and was deified after his death. He remains central to Candomblé, Santería and other Afro-diasporic religions.

The myth

According to Yoruba tradition, Shango was the son of Oranyan and had several wives, among them Oya, Oshun and Oba. He ruled Oyo with force and ambition, and his violent temper led him into conflict with his own brother. One version holds that his palace was destroyed by lightning by accident, which ended his reign.

After his death he became an orisha. In one well-known story, Obatalá ordered the male children of his line put to death, and Elegguá hid the newborn Shango with his elder sister Dadá, who raised him. Later Shango healed his father, which led to the pardon of Orula, and in gratitude Shango carved from a ceiba tree the divination board that Orunmila received.

Worship and religion

Shango is one of the most widely venerated orishas. He receives amalá, a stew of cornmeal, okra and other foods, and his ceremonies use batá drums and necklaces of red and white beads in groups of four or six. His followers avoid beans, and his priests wear red cloth. In Nigeria his name is still invoked at coronation ceremonies, and his worship continues in Cuba, Brazil, Haiti and Trinidad.

Legacy

He appears in music, with tracks by Babatunde Olatunji, Hugh Masekela and Santana, and in literature, including Duro Ladipo's play Ọba kò so and Aimé Césaire's A Tempest. He also features in novels and television, such as American Gods, where he appears as a character.

Ancient sources: Yoruba oral traditions, transmitted mainly through the itãs of Ifá, are his principal source; no ancient written text describes him directly.

Trivia

Double-headed axe. His characteristic weapon is the oxê, a double-bladed axe that identifies him in iconography and on altars.
Thunderstones. Stones formed by lightning strikes are considered sacred by his worshippers and are kept at shrines.
