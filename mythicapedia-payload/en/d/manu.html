the first human being and first king in Hindu tradition

Manu

In Hindu tradition, Manu is the first human being and the first king to rule the Earth. He is the hero of a story of a universal flood, and the figure from whom the Sanskrit words for humankind derive. He appears in Vedic, epic and Puranic texts.

The myth

According to tradition, Manu was the son of Vivasvat, the sun god, and of a mother named Saranyu, which is why he is also called Vaivasvata. Another of his names, Satyavrata, means 'he who keeps his vow of truth'. His name is linked to manas, 'mind', so Manu would be the thinking being. The Mahabharata, in the Adi Parva book, presents him as a wise and virtuous man and the progenitor of a dynasty, from which the brahmins and kshatriyas descend, and who are therefore called manavas, 'humans'.

His ten sons, among them Ikshvaku, Nabhaga and Ila, founded royal lineages. The Mahabharata also mentions Vena, a wicked king, and fifty other sons who, it is said, perished fighting one another. From Ikshvaku came the ikshvaku tribe, a branch of the solar dynasty.

The best-known episode is the flood. According to the Bhagavata Purana, the fish Matsya, an avatar of Vishnu, appeared to Manu, then king of Dravida, while he was washing his hands in a river, and asked for his protection. The creature grew so large that it had to be moved from a copper vessel to a pond, then to a lake, and finally to the ocean, and then it warned him of the coming flood. Manu built a boat in which he took his family and animals of every kind, and the fish towed it safely through the waters. This story resembles the flood accounts of Sumerian mythology, which precede the Noah's ark story.

Puranic traditions place Manu as the ruler of a manvantara, a period of about 4,320 million years. Fourteen manus succeed one another during a kalpa, which equals one day of Brahma, and according to the text we live in the seventh, ruled by Vaivasvata.

Legacy

Manu's name gave rise to the Sanskrit words for human beings, manuṣya and mānava, 'children of Manu'. He is also credited with the Manusmriti, known as the Laws of Manu. In the nineteenth century, some British politicians who had conquered India spread the idea that this text was the sacred law of the Hindus, something some present-day Indian scholars believe was meant to make Hindus easy to ridicule. Hindus regard it as a smriti, a text of secondary authority compared with the shruti, such as the Vedas.

Ancient sources: Manu appears in the Rig Veda, the Vajasaneyi Samhita and the Shatapatha Brahmana, as well as in the Mahabharata and the Bhagavata Purana, and the Manusmriti is attributed to him.

Trivia

A fish that keeps growing. In the story, the fish Matsya grows without stopping: first it fits in a copper vessel, then a pond, a lake and the ocean. It is the avatar of Vishnu who guides Manu through the flood.
A Manu for each period. Each manvantara has its own Manu, and fourteen succeed one another in a kalpa. The texts name several of them, such as Svayambhuva, the first, and Vaivasvata, the seventh.
