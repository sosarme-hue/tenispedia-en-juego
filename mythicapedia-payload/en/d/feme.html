Greek goddess of fame and rumour

Pheme

Pheme (Φήμη) or Ossa (Ὄσσα) is the Greek personification of fame, renown and rumour. Her Roman counterpart, Fama, shared her nature and was depicted in a similar way.

The myth

Ancient authors portray her in different ways. Homer shows her as a messenger of Zeus in the Iliad, alongside Iris, and in the Odyssey she runs through the city announcing the slaughter of Penelope's suitors. Hesiod describes her as a mischievous figure whose gossip never dies away, while Sophocles makes her a daughter of Elpis, hope.

Virgil turns her into a monstrous winged being, a daughter of Earth, covered in feathers, eyes and tongues, who never sleeps and prefers to spread misfortune. Ovid places her in a bronze palace with thousands of openings, where rumours, true or false, come and go without rest.

Worship and religion

She had an altar in the Athenian agora, documented from the 5th century BCE and in use until the imperial era. It may have been set up to celebrate news of a victory by Cimon over the Persians in Pamphylia, which travelled with astonishing speed, or, according to another tradition, the Greek victory at Mycale. She had no widespread cult and no mythology of her own.

Legacy

Her name passed into Latin as fama and from there into modern languages, including English fame. In Elizabethan theatre, Rumour was a common character, and Shakespeare invokes him in the second part of Henry IV.

Ancient sources: Homer (Iliad and Odyssey), Hesiod (Works and Days), Sophocles, Virgil (Aeneid), Ovid (Metamorphoses) and Statius (Thebaid).

Trivia

Rooted in speech. Greek pheme is related to phanai, 'to speak', and Latin fama to fari, with the same meaning. English fame comes from the Latin word.
Messenger of Zeus. In the Iliad she appears alongside Iris as one of Zeus's messengers, tasked with urging warriors to take up arms.
