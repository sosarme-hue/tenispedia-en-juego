Greek goddess of fortune and luck

Tyche

Tyche is the Greek goddess of luck, chance and fortune, the personification of the fate that governs the prosperity of individuals and, above all, of cities. Her cult grew during the Hellenistic period and later spread to Rome, where she was identified with the Latin Fortuna.

The myth

Ancient accounts give her no independent story comparable to those of the major gods; Tyche is largely an abstraction given a personal form. Genealogies vary between authors. Some made her one of the Oceanids, daughter of Oceanus and Tethys; others named Zeus or even Prometheus as her father. In one source Plutus, the god of wealth, is her son, though he is more usually said to be the son of Demeter and Iasion.

Tyche distributes prosperity at her whim: she showers some with abundance from her horn and strips others of what they own. Her choices are unpredictable, and she is shown playing with a ball to suggest that fortune rises and falls. If a recipient of her favours grows proud and shares nothing with the gods or the needy, Nemesis steps in to punish the arrogance.

Aesop presents her in moral fables. In the story of the farmer, she reminds him that his treasure comes from her, not from the earth; in the story of the traveller asleep by the well, she wakes him before he falls, warning that had he fallen he would have blamed Fortune rather than his own carelessness. An Orphic hymn invokes her as mistress of the roads and ruler of the varied lives of mortals.

Worship and religion

Every city had its own Tyche, shown wearing a mural crown like the protective deities of cities. At Itanos on Crete she was venerated as Tyche Protogeneia, and in Alexandria the Tychaeon, a temple that the rhetorician Libanius considered one of the most splendid of the Hellenistic world, stood in her honour. In Athens she received offerings as Agathe Tyche alongside other gods. Her cult did not survive as a living religion, though it was briefly revived in the final years of public paganism, before the temples were definitively closed in the late fourth century.

Legacy

Her image, with the mural crown, rudder and cornucopia, recurred on Hellenistic coins and in Greco-Roman and medieval art. In the Middle Ages she was shown blindfolded beside the wheel of fortune, a symbol that remained part of Western culture. Polybius turned her into a historical principle explaining Rome's rise, and the same idea appears in Plutarch's essay on Roman fortune. In astronomy, the constellation of Virgo has sometimes been identified with her figure.

Ancient sources: Pausanias and Aesop mention her, and Pindar invokes her as protector of cities and of athletic victories; Polybius and Plutarch reflect on her role in history.

Trivia

Palamedes' dice. According to Pausanias, Palamedes invented the first pair of dice and offered them to Tyche.
A goddess of cities. Each city venerated its own Tyche, and late Roman art shows sets of the empire's greatest cities.
