Greek hero of the Seven against Thebes

Hippomedon

Hippomedon is one of the Seven against Thebes in Greek mythology, the band of champions who marched against the city to restore Polynices. He is remembered for his great size and strength in battle, and for the different gates and fates that ancient authors assign to him.

The myth

The ancient sources disagree about his parentage. Some make him a son of Talaus, father of Adrastus; others name Aristomachus, also a son of Talaus, or Mnesimachus. If his father was Mnesimachus, his mother was Metidice, daughter of Talaus, which made him the son of Adrastus's sister. He was said to have lived in Mycenae or near the lake Lerna in the Peloponnese.

In Aeschylus' tragedy Seven Against Thebes he is a huge and powerful warrior whose shield bears a fire-breathing Typhon. He attacks the gate of Athena Onca, where he is confronted by Hyperbius, son of Oenops. According to the Bibliotheca, however, he is killed by Ismarus, son of Astacus. In Euripides' Suppliants he is portrayed as indifferent to comfort and feasting, preferring hardship and spending long hours training for combat. In Phoenician Women his shield shows Argus Panoptes, and he assaults the Ogygian Gates.

His longest appearance is in Statius' Thebaid. He fights over the body of Tydeus and, when the attempt to win it back fails, keeps killing until he wades into the river Ismenus. Among his victims is the young Crenaeus, son of Pan and the nymph Ismenis. His mother asks the river god for vengeance, and Ismenus raises the waters and nearly drowns the hero. Hippomedon begs not to die that way, and Hera persuades Zeus to spare him. Yet he leaves the river without armor, and a volley of enemy missiles kills him. Hypseus takes his helmet as a trophy and announces his death to the allies, shortly before he falls to Capaneus.

Ancient sources: Aeschylus (Seven Against Thebes), Euripides (Suppliants and Phoenician Women), the Bibliotheca attributed to Apollodorus and, from the Roman period, Statius' Thebaid.

Trivia

The ruins of his house. In Pausanias' time the foundations of his house on Mount Pontinus, near Lerna, were still shown to visitors.
A death without armor. In Statius, Hera persuades Zeus to spare Hippomedon from the river, but he dies after leaving the water unarmored and being struck by missiles.
