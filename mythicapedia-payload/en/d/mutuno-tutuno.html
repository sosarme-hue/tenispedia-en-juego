Roman phallic marriage deity

Mutunus Tutunus

Mutunus Tutunus (or Mutinus Titinus) was a phallic marriage deity of ancient Rome, equated in some respects with the Greek Priapus. He is known above all for the pre-wedding rites attributed to him in the sources and for the modern debate over his identity.

The myth

According to tradition, his shrine stood on the Velian Hill from the founding of Rome until the 1st century BC. Sources say that before weddings, Roman brides straddled his phallus to prepare for intercourse, an act Church Fathers read as an obscene loss of virginity. The apologist Arnobius claims matrons were «taken for a ride» on the god's «awful phallus», while other texts suggest the rite taught brides not to be ashamed of sex.

The grammarian Festus is the only classical Latin author to mention him in detail, and much of the information comes from Christian writers whose view of the rite was hostile or biased.

Worship and religion

According to Festus, the shrine was demolished to make a private bath for the pontifex Domitius Calvinus, despite being revered as one of the oldest in the city. Robert Palmer linked his cult to that of Father Liber and the Liberalia, and suggested that Julius Caesar sacrificed to Mutunus in the form of Liber on the day he was assassinated. Under Augustus, the god was supposedly Hellenized as Bacchus Lyaeus.

Legacy

His name appears in the satirical verse of Lucilius and Horace, in an epigram by Martial, in the Corpus Priapeorum and in graffiti from Pompeii. A denarius minted by Quintus Titius around 90-88 BC is thought by some numismatists to show an aged, bearded Mutunus, though the identification is not unanimous.

Ancient sources: Festus, the 2nd-century grammarian, is the only classical Latin source to mention him in detail; the other references are Christian, from Arnobius and the Church Fathers.

Trivia

A god with a slang name. Mutto and mūtōnium were slang words for penis in Latin, and Mutto was even used as a Roman cognomen.
A winged phallus. The winged phallus was a common decorative motif in Rome, possibly serving as protection against the evil eye.
