Slavic god of abundance and war

Svetovit

Svetovit (also Svantovit or Sventovit) is a god of the Polabian Slavs, worshipped above all on the island of Rügen in the Baltic Sea. He was seen as a lord of abundance and war, and one of the highest-ranking gods in the Slavic pantheon.

The myth

Medieval chroniclers describe his statue with four heads, each facing a different point of the compass, and a horn and a sword in its hands. Some pre-Christian Slavic accounts present him as the creator of the earth and of humankind, though surviving texts say little about his origins. According to tradition, at night the god rode out to fight his enemies.

Scholars read his four faces as identifications with other Slavic deities, and debate whether he was a local form of the god Perun. His name has several possible meanings: holy lord, holy victor, or the one who dawns.

Worship and religion

His main temple stood at Cape Arkona on Rügen. Chronicles describe a large wooden statue and a horn of plenty that was refilled with mead every year. The temple held the tribe's treasure, guarded by three hundred mounted warriors. After the harvest a great festival was held in his honour, and the chief priest divined the future by watching the behaviour of a white horse sacred to the god. His cult was destroyed by the Danes in 1168. Among the Serbs a partial echo survives in the feast of Vidovdan, linked to St Vitus.

Legacy

His name appears in Balkan place names, such as Mount Vid on the Croatian island of Brač and the peak Suvid in the Dinaric Alps. He also features in Poul Anderson's science fiction story Delenda Est, where he remains the chief god of Littorn, a stand-in for Lithuania.

Ancient sources: Saxo Grammaticus, in the Gesta Danorum, and Helmold, in his Chronicle of the Slavs, describe his temple and statue.

Trivia

Mix-up with St Vitus. For a long time it was believed that his cult came from St Vitus. Today it is considered more likely that the saint was used to replace the god.
A festival that survives. The Serbian feast of Vidovdan, linked to St Vitus, keeps traces of the old veneration of the god.
