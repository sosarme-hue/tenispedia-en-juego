mountain nymph of Phrygia, first wife of Paris

Oenone

Oenone is a mountain nymph of Mount Ida, in Phrygia, best known as the first wife of Paris, the Trojan prince. Ancient authors told her story with different endings, which makes her one of the most tragic figures of the Trojan cycle.

The myth

According to tradition, Oenone was a naiad, daughter of the river god Cebren, whom some writers also call Oeneus. Her name links her to wine, and she was known for her skill with healing herbs and, above all, for prophecy, an art that some accounts say she learned from Rhea.

When Paris was a humble herdsman tending cattle on Mount Ida, he fell in love with her. They lived together as a married couple, and Paris swore he would never leave her. Oenone, however, prophesied that he would eventually abandon her for a beautiful woman from Europe. They had a son, Corythus. When Paris set out to take Helen from Sparta, Oenone tried to dissuade him and told him that if he was ever wounded, he should return to her, since only she could heal him.

During the siege of Troy, Paris was struck by an arrow from Philoctetes, shot with the bow of Heracles, and returned to Ida. Oenone, bitter, refused to heal him, and Paris died, in Troy or on the mountain depending on the version. Accounts differ on her own end: some say she hanged herself upon finding his body; others say she threw herself onto his funeral pyre; and a fragment of Bacchylides speaks of a leap from a cliff.

Legacy

Ovid imagined a reproachful letter from Oenone to Paris in his Heroides, a text later extended by spurious additions. In English literature, Thomas Heywood wrote the short poem Oenone and Paris (1594), Aphra Behn translated her letter, and William Morris and Lawrence Binyon also treated the theme. Tennyson wrote about her in two poems, the second, The Death of Oenone, published in 1892. In Racine's Phèdre, the name Oenone belongs to the nurse of the heroine, who also takes her own life.

Ancient sources: Ovid in the Heroides, Apollodorus in the Library, Parthenius of Nicaea, Quintus Smyrnaeus in the Posthomerica, and Bacchylides.

Trivia

An island with her name. Oenone was also the ancient name of an island, which later took the name Aegina, daughter of the river god Asopus.
A death with no single version. According to some sources, Oenone killed the messenger who brought her news of Paris' death, while other versions do not involve her in his death at all.
