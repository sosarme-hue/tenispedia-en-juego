goddess of Roman Africa and personification of the continent

Africa

Africa was a goddess of fertility and abundance worshipped by the Berbers, who in Roman North Africa became a minor deity and later the personification of the continent. Her image, with an elephant-skin headdress and a cornucopia, remains recognizable on coins and mosaics.

The myth

According to Berber tradition, Africa was a deity associated with caves, which were her main places of worship. Her name may be linked to the Berber word ifri, meaning "cave", and to the Afri, a Libyan people believed to be ancestors of the modern Berbers.

The Romans adopted her as the personification of their province of Africa, roughly modern north-eastern Algeria, Tunisia and coastal Libya. Ancient texts describe her only briefly: Pliny the Elder, in his Natural History, notes that no one in Africa did anything without first calling on her. Her image includes a cornucopia, ears of corn, a military standard and, in some depictions, an elephant's head, a feature coins linked to Alexander the Great.

Worship and religion

The Berbers worshipped her in caves, and at Timgad she was the main deity of a great sanctuary, where she was honoured as Dea Patria, goddess of the homeland. Some scholars doubt the Romans regarded her as a true goddess, since no inscription calls her Dea.

Legacy

During the Renaissance she was a standard figure in the Four Continents imagery, often shown with a dark complexion, curly hair and a broad nose.

Ancient sources: Pliny the Elder, in his Natural History, mentions her as the one invoked before any undertaking in Africa.

Trivia

Invoked before every undertaking. Pliny the Elder wrote that in Africa no one began anything without first calling on the goddess.
An elephant headdress. The elephant headdress first appears on coins of Alexander the Great, and later on those of Ptolemy I of Egypt.
