Sicilian shepherd of Greek mythology, inventor of pastoral poetry

Daphnis

Daphnis is a Sicilian herdsman in Greek mythology, credited with inventing pastoral poetry. Some traditions make him a son of Hermes, while others present him as the god's beloved, and his story is a model of a short-lived youth whose love ends in tragedy.

The myth

According to tradition, his mother, a nymph, abandoned him as an infant beneath a laurel tree, which gave him his name, from the Greek daphnē, "laurel". Herdsmen found him and raised him, and he became a shepherd himself, tending flocks on Mount Etna. Some sources make him Hermes' son, others his lover. Pan taught him the panpipes, and the Muses inspired his poetry. He accompanied Artemis on her hunts and sang his songs for her.

He is said to have civilised the herdsmen, teaching them to honour the gods and spreading the worship of Dionysus. A nymph, whose name varies by author (Echenais, Xenea, Nomia or Lice), fell in love with him and made him swear he would never love another woman, under threat of blindness. He gave in after being made drunk by a princess, and the nymph blinded him or turned him to stone. In the best-known version, Hermes carried him up to heaven and a spring rose on the spot; other accounts say he fell from a cliff while wandering blind.

Worship and religion

The Sicilians offered an annual sacrifice at the spring that bore his name, in memory of his early death.

Legacy

He inspired Theocritus, whose first Idyll narrates his death, and Virgil, who recalled him in his Fifth Eclogue. He gave his name to a poetic genre, the pastoral.

Ancient sources: Theocritus in his Idylls and Virgil in his Eclogues record him; Ovid and Diodorus Siculus also mention him.

Trivia

A namesake priestess. Pausanias mentions a mountain nymph called Daphnis, to whom Gaia supposedly entrusted the prophecy at Delphi.
