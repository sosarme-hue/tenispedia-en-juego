three-bodied giant of Greek mythology

Geryon

Geryon is a giant of Greek mythology who dwelt on the island of Erytheia in the far west, where he guarded a magnificent herd of cattle. He is best known for the tenth labour of Heracles, and he matters because later Greeks linked his story with the Iberian Peninsula.

The myth

According to Hesiod's Theogony, Geryon was the son of Chrysaor and Callirrhoe, daughter of Oceanus. He had one body with three heads and was extremely violent. His two-headed hound Orthrus, brother of Cerberus, and the herdsman Eurytion guarded his cattle on the island. Aeschylus gave him three bodies instead, and Stesichorus, in his poem the Geryoneis, described him as winged, with six hands and six feet.

Eurystheus ordered Heracles to bring back this herd as his tenth labour. The hero crossed the Libyan desert, and, exasperated by the heat, shot an arrow at Helios. The admiring god lent him the golden cup of the Sun to reach Erytheia. There Heracles killed Orthrus and Eurytion, and when Geryon pursued him, he struck him with an arrow dipped in the blood of the Lernaean Hydra beside the river Anthemus. The return journey was troubled: in Italy the thief Cacus stole part of the cattle, and Hera sent a gadfly that scattered the herd and a flood that forced the hero to pile stones into the river.

Diodorus Siculus gives another version, in which Geryon is a king of Iberia with three sons, whom Heracles defeats in battle. Other authors place him in Italy, in Liguria, or even in Ambracia in Greece.

Worship and religion

Diodorus Siculus records that after Heracles' victory, the Iberians established a custom of sacrificing their finest bull each year in honour of Heracles, identified with Melqart. The sources do not document a cult of Geryon himself, although they mention an ancient veneration of his figure in the peninsula.

Legacy

In Dante's Divine Comedy, Geryon appears as the Monster of Fraud, with a serpent's body, claws and a scorpion's sting, but the face of an honest man. Medieval chroniclers of the peninsula turned him into a local hero: Alfonso X tells how Heracles cut off his head and built a tower on it, a tale linked to the Tower of Hercules in A Coruña, a lighthouse that still works today.

Ancient sources: Hesiod's Theogony gives the earliest description, Stesichorus' Geryoneis presents him as winged, and Apollodorus, Aeschylus and Diodorus Siculus preserve other versions of the myth.

Trivia

A tower over a head. Alfonso X tells how Heracles raised a tower over Geryon's head. The present Tower of Hercules in A Coruña is a working lighthouse rebuilt on a Roman one.
A monster with a human face. In Dante's Divine Comedy, Geryon has a beast's body and a scorpion's tail but the face of an honest man, and he carries Virgil and Dante on his back down to the Circle of Fraud.
