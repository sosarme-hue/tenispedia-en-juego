Chinese sea goddess, protector of sailors and fishermen

Mazu (Matsu)

Mazu, also known as Matsu, is a sea goddess of Chinese folk religion, honored by sailors and fishermen along China's coasts. She is the deified form of Lin Moniang, a shamaness from Fujian said to have lived in the late 10th century, and her worship remains strong in Taiwan and among overseas Chinese communities in Southeast Asia.

The myth

According to tradition, Lin Moniang was born in Meizhou, in Putian County, into a family of fishermen surnamed Lin. Her name means "silent girl" because she did not cry when she was born. Later sources link her with Guanyin: one version says she was born miraculously after her mother dreamed of the goddess, and another that she became a devout Buddhist after gazing at a statue of Guanyin in a temple.

She was said to be an excellent swimmer who stood on the shore in red garments to guide fishing boats home. A Taoist master, or an immortal in other versions, supposedly gave her an amulet that let her heal the sick, exorcise demons and avert disasters.

Her principal legend tells of how she saved relatives during a typhoon, when she was about sixteen. While weaving she fell into a trance, and her spiritual power began to save the men from drowning, but her mother woke her and she dropped one brother into the sea. Versions differ: some say her father was lost and she searched for three days, others that everyone came home safely. Tradition places her death in 987, after which she was revered. Her iconography shows a young woman in red accompanied by two demons, Thousand-Mile Eye and Wind-Ear, whom she subdued and who now serve her.

Worship and religion

Her cult began in the Song dynasty and spread along the coasts of Zhejiang, Fujian, Guangdong, Hainan and Taiwan, as well as among Chinese communities across Southeast Asia. People believed she roamed the seas protecting her devotees through miraculous interventions, and today many regard her as a powerful and benevolent Queen of Heaven. Around 1,500 Mazu temples exist in 26 countries. Her festival is a major event in Taiwan, with the largest celebrations at her temples in Dajia and Beigang. In emergencies, devotees traditionally prayed to her as Mazu rather than by her formal titles, since it was believed she would answer more quickly.

Legacy

Her official titles include Tianfei, "Princess of Heaven", conferred in 1281, along with others granted by imperial courts over several centuries. In Macau she is popularly known as A-Ma, and in Hokkien as Mazupo, a name widely used in Fujian.

Ancient sources: An inscription from 1150 is the earliest record of her cult, and later Chinese texts, such as the Account of the Blessings Revealed by the Princess of Heaven, preserve much of her legend.

Trivia

A name from silence. Moniang means "the silent girl", a name said to have been given because she did not cry at birth or during her first month of life.
The red dress as a beacon. She was said to stand on the shore in red garments to guide fishing boats home, even in harsh weather.
Titles avoided in emergencies. It was believed that if a formal title was used in an emergency, she would groom and dress herself before answering, so the name Mazu was preferred.
