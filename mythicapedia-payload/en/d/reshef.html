Canaanite god of plague and war

Resheph

Resheph is a god of plague, war and lightning from the Canaanite tradition, attested from the third millennium BCE in the city of Ebla. His cult reached Egypt in the New Kingdom, where he became a protective deity who was also invoked as a healer.

The myth

The surviving texts say more about his worship than about his stories. He appears in Ugaritic texts such as the Kirta epic and the text of the Mare and Horon, and in a Mesopotamian omen text he is described as doorkeeper of the sun goddess Shapash, possibly identified with the planet Mars.

His character combines two sides: he brings disease and the arrow, but he can also cure. In Egypt he was shown as a warrior armed with a shield, spear and axe, and he was linked with gazelles and horses. According to Egyptian tradition he was the husband of the goddess Qadesh and the father of Min, although other sources pair him with Itum.

Worship and religion

In Ebla he received offerings of weapons, especially daggers, axes and lances, and was venerated by both commoners and the royal family; foreign kings visiting the city also made sacrifices to him. One of the gates of Ebla bore his name. In Egypt his cult spread to all levels of society, and he was invoked alongside Astarte against stomach pain. On Cyprus he was identified with Apollo. His cult disappeared millennia ago.

Legacy

The Israeli city of Arsuf still keeps Resheph's name in its own, thousands of years after his worship ended. His name has been linked to Hebrew and Akkadian words meaning fire, fever or destruction, although the origin of the name is uncertain.

Ancient sources: Ugaritic texts such as the Kirta epic, third-millennium BCE tablets from Ebla, and the Hebrew Bible in Psalms (78:48), Job (5:7), Deuteronomy (32:24) and Habakkuk (3:5).

Trivia

God of the gazelle. In bilingual Phoenician-Hittite inscriptions he is called the deer god or gazelle god, an animal with which he was also depicted in Egypt.
Valley of Reshep. A region on the east bank of the Nile was known as the Valley of Reshep, and he was worshipped there, mainly at Deir el-Medina and Heliopolis.
