one of the three Fates of Greek mythology

Atropos

Atropos is, in Greek mythology, the third of the Moirai, the Fates who govern destiny. According to the myth, she decided how each mortal would die and cut the thread of their life, which makes her the Fate most closely linked with death.

The myth

According to the myth, Atropos worked alongside her sisters Clotho, who spun the thread of life, and Lachesis, who measured its length. When the time came, Atropos chose the manner of death and cut the thread with her shears. Her name, meaning something like 'without turn' or 'inflexible', suits that role well.

Ancient accounts disagree about her parentage. Hesiod's Theogony first makes her a daughter of Erebus and Night, and later a daughter of Zeus and Themis. Cicero calls the three Parcae and gives them Erebus and Night as parents, while Boccaccio, writing in the Renaissance, makes her the youngest. It also remains unclear whether Zeus stood above the Fates or was bound by them like any mortal.

She appears in stories such as those of Atalanta and Achilles. Over time the Moirai stopped being concerned only with death and came to decide what could happen to each individual. Some authors called her Aesa, and in Rome her equivalent was Morta, 'Death'.

Legacy

Boccaccio lists her name among those used for the personification of death, alongside Mors, Parca and Letum. In Norse-Germanic myth, Skuld, one of the Norns, plays a similar role. Seneca, in his letters to Lucilius, quotes Cleanthes' saying that the Fates lead the willing and drag the unwilling.

Ancient sources: Hesiod's Theogony, The Shield of Heracles, Cicero's De Natura Deorum, Seneca's Letters to Lucilius, and Aulus Gellius, who gives Morta as her Roman equivalent.

Trivia

A name that says it all. Atropos means something like 'without turn' or 'inexorable'. Servius distinguished her from Mors: Atropos would be natural death or the dissolution of things, while Mors was violent death.
The oldest and the smallest. In The Shield of Heracles she is described as both the oldest and the smallest of the three, an unusual description among ancient sources.
