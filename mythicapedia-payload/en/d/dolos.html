Greek personification of deceit

Dolus

Dolus is the divine personification of deceit in Greek and Roman mythology, paired with Truth and Falsehood. He is best known from a fable by Phaedrus, in which he is the apprentice of Prometheus.

The myth

According to tradition, Dolus was born of Aether and Earth, though other authors make him the son of Erebus and Night. Some accounts also place him at the episode of the Lemnian women, who killed their unfaithful husbands.

His best-known story comes from Phaedrus's fable Prometheus and Guile. Prometheus was modelling Truth in clay with great care so that justice could rule among men, but Jupiter summoned him away and left Dolus in charge of the workshop. Driven by rivalry, Dolus made a near-identical figure but ran out of clay before finishing its feet. When Prometheus returned, amused by the resemblance, he fired both figures in his kiln. Once brought to life, Truth walked gracefully while Dolus's figure, Falsehood, could barely stand. Hence the saying that a lie has no feet.

Legacy

Phaedrus's fable gave rise to the Spanish proverb «la mentira no tiene pies», often rendered in English as the idea that a lie has no legs, meaning falsehood eventually fails.

Ancient sources: He is mentioned by Hyginus and Cicero, and his main story survives in the fables of Phaedrus, the Roman poet of the Tiberian age.

Trivia

A proverb from a fable. The Spanish saying «la mentira no tiene pies» comes from Phaedrus's story of Dolus's figure without feet.
A pair of twins. In the fable, Truth and Falsehood are twin figures shaped from the same clay.
