mythical bird of Persian mythology

Simurgh

The simurgh is a benevolent mythical bird of Iranian mythology, acting as a mediator between sky and earth. The Sasanian kings adopted it as an emblem, and it appears in art and literature across the Iranian world.

The myth

Iranian legends describe the simurgh as so ancient that it had witnessed the destruction of the world three times. Its long life brought it knowledge of all ages, and it was said to live 1,700 years before burning in flames, much like the phoenix.

Zoroastrian sources place the simurgh in the tree of life, Hōm, which stands in the cosmic sea of Vourukasha. When the bird took flight, the tree's leaves shook and the seeds of every plant fell and drifted across the world on the winds of Vayu and the rains of Tishtrya, so that healing plants came into being.

In Ferdowsi's Shahnameh, the simurgh rescues the albino infant Zal, abandoned on Mount Alborz by his father, who thought the child was the spawn of devils. The bird raises him as her own. When Zal returns to the world of men, she gives him three feathers to burn if he ever needs her. Later, when his wife Rudaba faces a dangerous childbirth, Zal summons the simurgh, who teaches him how to perform a caesarean section. The child is Rostam, the greatest Persian hero.

Legacy

The bird's name has inspired wordplay. The poet Attar of Nishapur read it as "thirty birds" in The Conference of the Birds, where a gathering of birds searches for their king, the simurgh. Its image served as the emblem of the Sasanian kings and recurs in textiles and miniatures throughout Persian history.

Ancient sources: Ferdowsi's Shahnameh describes its relationship with Zal and Rostam, and the Avesta refers to the bird Saēna and the tree Hōm.

Trivia

Thirty birds. Attar of Nishapur read the name as "thirty birds" in The Conference of the Birds, although the real etymology is different.
Enemy of serpents. According to some descriptions, the simurgh was hostile to snakes and preferred places with plenty of water.
