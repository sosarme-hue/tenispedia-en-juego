great hall of the slain in Norse mythology

Valhalla

Valhalla (the hall of the slain) is the enormous hall ruled by Odin in Asgard, the home of the Norse gods. There the warriors chosen from those who fall in battle gather, preparing to fight alongside the god at Ragnarök, the final battle of the world.

The myth

According to Norse tradition, Valhalla stands in Asgard and is Odin's hall. The Poetic Edda describes it in detail: its roof is covered with gleaming golden shields, its rafters are made of spear-shafts, and coats of mail lie across its benches. A wolf is chained before the western door, and an eagle hovers above. The Grímnismál says the hall has five hundred and forty doors, and through each of them eight hundred einherjar march out when they go to fight the wolf Fenrir.

The dead who arrive there are warriors slain in battle. Each day Odin chooses some of the fallen, and the valkyries escort them to the hall, while the other half go to Freyja's Fólkvangr. The einherjar, who become Odin's adopted sons, fight and feast daily. The valkyries serve them mead, which the goat Heiðrún makes from the leaves of the tree Læraðr, while the stag Eikthyrnir drips liquid from its antlers into the spring Hvergelmir.

The Helgakviða Hundingsbana II shows another side of the hall. When the hero Helgi Hundingsbane dies, he goes to Valhalla, where he finds his former enemy Hunding doing household chores. Later Helgi returns to the world of the living with a host of men, and his valkyrie wife Sigrún welcomes him at his burial mound. According to the poem, the two were later reborn as Helgi Haddingjaskati and the valkyrie Kára.

In the Prose Edda, Snorri Sturluson recounts how King Gylfi travels to Asgard disguised as Gangleri and sees the great hall roofed with shields. Snorri also notes that the einherjar will stay with Odin until Ragnarök, when they will fight at his side.

Legacy

Valhalla has inspired countless works of art, publication titles and elements of popular culture. Its name has also become a byword for a hall of honour where great figures who have already died are commemorated.

Ancient sources: The Poetic Edda (Grímnismál, Helgakviða Hundingsbana II, Völuspá and Hyndluljóð), the Prose Edda and the Heimskringla by Snorri Sturluson, and the poem Eiríksmál, preserved in the Fagrskinna saga.

Trivia

Five hundred and forty doors. According to the Grímnismál, the hall has five hundred and forty doors. Through each one, eight hundred einherjar march out at once to fight.
A goat that makes mead. The goat Heiðrún feeds on the branches of the tree Læraðr and produces mead that never runs out.
