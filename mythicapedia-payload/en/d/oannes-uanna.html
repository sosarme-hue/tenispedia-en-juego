fish-man sage of ancient Mesopotamia

Oannes (Uanna)

Oannes, known in Sumerian as Uanna, is the first of the apkallu, fish-man sages of Mesopotamian tradition. According to Berossus, he came up from the Persian Gulf to teach humans the arts of civilization.

The myth

According to the stories, the apkallu were sent by the gods to pass knowledge on to humans. Uanna was the first of the seven, and his name appears on Sumerian tablets with the sense of « the one who finished the plans of heaven and earth ». Berossus, a Babylonian priest who wrote in Greek, describes him as an amphibious being who taught writing, mathematics, architecture and good manners, and who passed on to people the creation account known as the Enuma Elish.

Berossus says that under the reign of Dahös, around 2780 BC, other similar beings appeared: Eudocus, Eneugamus, Eneuboulus and Anemeutus. Later, according to Apollodorus, during the reign of En-men-dur-ana, the last one arrived, Anodaphus. For Berossus, these beings were called Musarus, a word meaning « repulsive creatures ». Eusebius of Caesarea preserved these accounts to show that the Babylonian gods were ancient humans who had been deified.

Some scholars identified Oannes with Ea or Enki, and others with Adapa, the wise son of Ea who is the hero of another myth. Today the name is generally seen as the Greek form of the Babylonian Uanna, one of the apkallu.

Ancient sources: Berossus, in his history of Babylonia preserved by Eusebius of Caesarea, and the Uruk List of Kings and Sages, dating to around 165 BC.

Trivia

A collective name. For Berossus, the amphibious beings who followed Oannes were called Musarus, meaning « repulsive creatures ».
A list found in a temple. The Uruk List of Kings and Sages, dating to around 165 BC, was discovered in 1959-1960 in the temple of Anu at Bit Res.
