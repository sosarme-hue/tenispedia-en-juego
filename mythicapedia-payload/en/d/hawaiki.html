mythical island of Polynesia, ancestral homeland of its peoples

Hawaiki

Hawaiki is, in Polynesian folklore, the original homeland from which the peoples who settled the Pacific islands came, and in many Māori stories it is also the underworld to which the spirits of the dead travel.

The myth

According to various oral traditions, the Polynesians set out from Hawaiki in open canoes, little different from the craft used across Polynesia, and spread over the Pacific. The Māori of New Zealand trace their ancestry to people who reportedly sailed from Hawaiki in about forty named waka, among them seven canoes that founded the seven original tribes.

The island's location varies from tradition to tradition, though it is usually placed to the west. In New Zealand, the spirits of the dead departed from Cape Reinga, at the far northwest of the North Island, heading back to Hawaiki. The name appears in many variants, such as Havaiki, Hawaiʻiki or ʻAvaiki, and it has been linked to real islands such as Raiatea, Fakarava and Savai'i.

Worship and religion

For Polynesians, Hawaiki was not only a place of origin: the belief that the spirits returned there made it a spiritual destination to which the dead went after death.

Legacy

Hawaiki's name has been compared with that of the Hawaiian Islands, which has sparked debate about a possible connection. In 1908, S. Percy Smith published Hawaiki, the original homeland of the Maori and suggested it might lie in Java. Today researchers do not take the legends literally: linguistics, genetics and archaeology point to Austronesian origins in East Asia, probably present-day Taiwan, from where peoples spread south and east. Modern navigators have built replicas of traditional canoes and sailed the presumed routes, showing that deliberate migration was feasible.

Trivia

A name that travels. In the Society Islands, Raiatea was once considered a sacred island known as Havai'i, and in the Tuamotu group the present-day Fakarava bore the name.
A geographic hypothesis. In 1908, S. Percy Smith proposed placing Hawaiki in Java, an idea that today is not taken literally.
