Hindu goddess of wine and intoxicating drinks

Varuni

Varuni is the divine personification of wine and intoxicating beverages in Hinduism, and also the name of a fermented palm drink. She is usually described as the daughter of Varuna, lord of the waters, although some texts make her his wife instead.

The myth

According to the Puranas, Varuni is the daughter of Varuna and Jyeshtha. She emerged during the Samudra Manthana, the churning of the ocean that gods and anti-gods undertook to win the elixir of immortality. Alongside her came an intoxicating drink, and the goddess had to choose between the two camps.

The tradition does not agree on her choice. In the Ramayana, the anti-gods reject her and the gods accept her, so those who welcome her become suras and those who refuse her become asuras. In the Bhagavata Purana, Sri chooses Vishnu, and the disheartened asuras take Varuni as a consolation. In the Mahabharata the daughter of Varuna is called Sura, and she is described as the sister of Bala and the granddaughter of the sage Shukra.

In the Vaishnava tradition she is shown offering wine to Balarama, and some sources link her to Ananta, the serpent king. The goddess Madira, a general word for liquor in Sanskrit, is also loosely associated with her.

Worship and religion

The goddess appears in Purana literature and in Vaishnava and Shakta traditions, where a Matrika linked to Varuna's energy is also worshipped. The Arthashastra lists Madira among the tutelary deities of fortified cities, which points to a ritual dimension to her cult. No clear evidence of a widespread modern cult is recorded.

Legacy

Varuni gives its name to a fermented palm drink and, in Ayurvedic medicine, appears in preparations using punarnavā. In language, varuni is a general term for strong liquor.

Ancient sources: She appears in the Ramayana, the Bhagavata Purana and the Mahabharata, where she is called Sura, as well as in the Arthashastra, the Matsya Purana and medical treatises such as the Aṣṭāṅga Hṛdaya.

Trivia

Names by role. When both father and daughter appear, the daughter is called Varuni and the wife Varunani; in some texts the roles are swapped.
