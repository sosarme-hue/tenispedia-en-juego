Electra

Electra is a princess of Mycenae, daughter of Agamemnon and Clytemnestra, and a central figure of Greek tragedy. Her story of revenge for her father's murder, carried out with her brother Orestes, is one of the most retold in Greek myth and has inspired plays, operas and films ever since.

The myth

According to tradition, Agamemnon sacrificed his daughter Iphigenia to Artemis to obtain favourable winds for Troy. While Electra was away from the kingdom, Agamemnon returned from the war bringing the Trojan princess Cassandra, his concubine, and both were killed on arrival. Some versions say Aegisthus, Clytemnestra's lover, did the killing; others say Clytemnestra did, or both. Some sources add that Cassandra's twin sons by Agamemnon were also slain.

Orestes, still a child, was saved by his nurse or by Electra and was raised by King Strophius. When he turned twenty, the Delphic oracle ordered him to avenge his father. In Aeschylus' version, the siblings met at their father's tomb and plotted together; Orestes, with Pylades' help, killed Aegisthus and then Clytemnestra. Afterwards the Erinyes pursued Orestes but not Electra. Athena presided over a trial in Athens, where her casting vote secured his acquittal.

In Euripides' Iphigenia in Tauris, Orestes, hounded by the Furies, reaches the Black Sea coast and meets Iphigenia, who had been rescued at the last moment. The three escape together, the Furies end their pursuit, and Electra marries Pylades. In the Iliad, Homer mentions a daughter of Agamemnon called Laodice, understood to be Electra.

Legacy

Sophocles and Euripides each wrote a tragedy titled Electra, and Aeschylus features her in The Libation Bearers. The Electra complex, proposed by Carl Gustav Jung, takes its name from her. Engels used the myth in The Origin of the Family to illustrate the shift from matriarchy to patriarchy. Massenet's Élégie draws on Electra's song from a play by Leconte de Lisle. Modern versions include works by Hofmannsthal, Eugene O'Neill, Sartre, and Richard Strauss's opera Elektra.

Ancient sources: Aeschylus, Sophocles and Euripides star her in their tragedies, and Homer mentions her as Laodice in the Iliad.

Trivia

A very sad song. Modern literary critics consider Electra's song in Euripides' play one of the saddest in ancient Greek tragedy.
A parody that saved Athens. According to Plutarch, after the Peloponnesian War, a Spartan soldier sang a parody of Euripides' Electra and the council was moved to spare Athens.
