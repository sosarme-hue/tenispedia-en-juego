Hindu goddess of smallpox

Śītalā

Śītalā is the Hindu goddess of smallpox, venerated mainly in North India. She is regarded as a form of Parvati and, in her fierce aspect, of Katyayani, and is invoked as a protector of children's health.

The myth

According to the tradition, when the asura Jvarasura spread fever among the children, the goddess Katyayani took the form of Śītalā to purify their blood and defeat the demon. Her iconography explains her power: grains fall from the basket on her head and turn into pustules, while her water pot and broom decide who survives. Those whose grains she had washed with water lived; those touched by her broom died.

The texts place her in Tantric and Puranic literature. Sixteenth-century copies of the Kāśī Khaṇḍa section of the Skanda Purana show her curing smallpox pustules. In the seventeenth century, the Bengali poem Shitala Mangal-Kāvya by Manikram Gangopadhyay helped spread her worship.

Worship and religion

She is worshipped throughout the year, but her main festival falls in the month of Chaitra, on Shitala Ashtami. Devotees, mostly women, offer flowers, food and leaves of the neem tree, which is closely associated with her. On that day many families do not light their stoves and eat cold food cooked the night before. Hindus who survived smallpox as children visit her temples, such as the one in Gurgaon, and the Jarag fair is held in Punjab. Her worship remains alive among Hindus, Buddhists and Adivasi communities.

Legacy

During the World Health Organization's smallpox campaign in the 1970s, some believers rejected vaccination, seeing the disease as a blessing from the goddess. The international programme even printed posters showing Śītalā holding a vaccination needle, presenting immunization as an expression of her power.

Ancient sources: The Skanda Purana (Kāśī Khaṇḍa section), Tantric and Puranic texts, and the Devi Mahatmyam, where the asura Jvarasura appears.

Trivia

Vaccination posters. The international smallpox programme produced posters showing Śītalā with a vaccination needle, so the campaign would be accepted by local people.
Cold food. On her festival, many families do not light their stoves and eat food cooked the day before, as a sign that spring gives way to summer.
