Odysseus's dog in the Odyssey

Argos

Argos is Odysseus's faithful hunting dog and one of the most memorable characters in Homer's Odyssey. His reunion with his master, who is disguised as a beggar, is one of the most emotional scenes in the poem.

The myth

In the Odyssey, Odysseus had raised Argos as a hunting dog, and the animal was known for its speed, strength and skill at tracking. When the hero left for the Trojan War, the dog was left in the care of others, who neglected him. During the twenty years of Odysseus's absence, Argos grew old and was left to lie in manure, covered in ticks.

On his return to Ithaca, Odysseus arrives disguised as a beggar, with the appearance Athena has given him, so that he can enter his palace unrecognised, where Penelope's suitors have taken over. Argos is the only one who recognises him at once: he drops his ears and wags his tail, but he has no strength to get up. Odysseus cannot greet him without revealing himself, so he walks on, weeping in silence, and the dog dies soon after.

Legacy

Jorge Luis Borges uses the name in his story The Immortal: the narrator calls the first man he meets in the city of the immortals Argos, who turns out to be Homer. The episode has drawn close attention from classical scholars, who read it as a symbol of loyalty and of the decline of Odysseus and his household. The name comes from the Greek adjective meaning shining white and, figuratively, swift, a sense that also appears in the name of the ship Argo, used by Jason.

Ancient sources: Homer's Odyssey, especially Book XVII, where he is reunited with Odysseus.

Trivia

A name meaning swiftness. The name comes from a Greek adjective meaning 'shining white' and, figuratively, 'quick' or 'agile'. In Homeric epic, this epithet was commonly applied to swift dogs.
Another famous Argos. Argos is also the name of the hundred-eyed watchman of other Greek myths, Argos Panoptes. One tradition holds that this figure was originally a watchdog.
