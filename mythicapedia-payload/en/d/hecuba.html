queen of Troy, wife of Priam, Greek mythology

Hecuba

Hecuba is the queen of Troy, wife of King Priam and mother of heroes such as Hector and Paris, as well as of the prophetess Cassandra. She matters because she embodies the suffering of the Trojan women after the fall of the city, a theme that inspired tragedies and paintings for centuries.

The myth

According to Homer's Iliad, Hecuba was the daughter of King Dymas of Phrygia, while Euripides and Virgil made her the daughter of Cisseus, king of Thrace. Pseudo-Apollodorus offers a third origin: the river god Sangarius and the nymph Metope. With Priam she had a large family, though the number varies by author, from fourteen children to fifty, with nineteen being the most widespread tradition. It is said that, while pregnant with Paris, she dreamed she would give birth to a burning torch that would set the city ablaze, and the seer Aesacus interpreted that the child would bring about Troy's ruin.

During the war, Hecuba appears as a figure of piety and comfort. In the Iliad she offers Hector the cup for Zeus, leads the Trojan women to Athena's temple to pray for protection, begs her son not to face Achilles and, at the end of the poem, mourns his death in a famous lament.

After the fall of Troy, Hecuba was given as a slave to Odysseus, according to one version. Earlier, she had sent her youngest son, Polydorus, to Thrace to keep him safe from the war; there he was cared for by King Polymestor, husband of her daughter Iliona. When Hecuba arrived in Thrace she discovered that the king had killed Polydorus to seize the gold the young man carried. She avenged her son by blinding Polymestor and killing his two sons.

The end of her story varies. According to one tradition, the gods turned her into a dog, and her grave on a promontory called Kynosema, or the «dog's grave», served as a landmark for sailors. According to Lycophron, the goddess Hecate took her in as one of her canine familiars.

Legacy

Euripides devoted two tragedies to her, Hecuba and The Trojan Women, in which she shows her grief after the war and her revenge. Dante recalls her in the Inferno (Canto XXX), where he describes her maddened, barking like a dog after finding the bodies of Polydorus and Polyxena. Painters such as Antonio Canova, Pierre Peyron and Michel Martin Drolling portrayed her in scenes from her story.

Ancient sources: Homer's Iliad, Euripides' tragedies (Hecuba and The Trojan Women), the works of Virgil and the Bibliotheca attributed to Pseudo-Apollodorus.

Trivia

A queen with a reputation for wisdom. The Roman emperor Tiberius, according to Suetonius, liked to pester scholars with obscure questions about mythology, and one of his favourites was who Hecuba's mother had been.
A dog's grave. One tradition says Hecuba threw herself into the sea and was transformed into a she-dog with fiery eyes, buried at a place called Kynosema, or «dog's grave».
