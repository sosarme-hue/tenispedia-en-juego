Greek god of beauty and desire

Adonis

Adonis was a youth of legendary beauty and the lover of Aphrodite. His cult came from the Near East and was absorbed into Greek religion, and his story of a god who dies and is mourned each summer makes him one of the most complex figures of classical antiquity.

The myth

Myth tells that Adonis was born out of incest. The sources disagree about his parents: Ovid makes him the son of Myrrha, who was cursed by Aphrodite with an impossible passion for her father, King Cinyras of Cyprus, and was turned into a myrrh tree before giving birth. Hesiod, who points to a Phoenician origin, names Phoenix and Alphesiboea as his parents, while other versions place his origins in Byblos or Cyprus.

Aphrodite raised him and entrusted him to Persephone in the underworld. As he grew up, both goddesses fell in love with him. Zeus settled the dispute by dividing his year into three parts: one third with Aphrodite, one with Persephone and one with whomever he chose. Adonis chose Aphrodite, though in other versions he was shared equally between them.

His end was tragic: while hunting, a wild boar fatally wounded him. According to Ovid, his blood mixed with Aphrodite's tears and the anemone sprang up. Other accounts blame Ares, jealous of the goddess, or Artemis, seeking revenge on Aphrodite.

Worship and religion

His cult was honoured at the Adonia, a midsummer festival in which women planted quick-growing "gardens of Adonis" of herbs and grains that sprouted and withered in the heat. They then mourned the god, beat their breasts and threw his effigy and the dead plants into the sea. Lucian of Samosata describes the yearly mourning at Byblos in De Dea Syria, where the river Adonis reddened with soil washed down by storms, and offers a natural explanation for it. This cult is no longer practised.

Legacy

The word "adonis" is used for a very handsome young man, often also with the sense of vanity. In modern times the myth has been read as a prime example of the dying-and-rising god, an archetype widely promoted by religious scholarship in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries.

Ancient sources: Sappho offers the earliest Greek reference to Adonis, in a fragment where a chorus of girls asks Aphrodite how to mourn him. Ovid retells his story in the Metamorphoses, and Hyginus and Lucian also record episodes of his myth.

Trivia

A river with his name. According to Lucian, the river Adonis, today the Nahr Ibrahim, turned red each year at Byblos. He also recorded a natural explanation based on red soil blown into the water by storms.
Short-lived gardens. The "gardens of Adonis" were small pots of fast-sprouting plants that withered in the summer sun, a symbol of a brief life.
