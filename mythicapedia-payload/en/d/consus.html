Roman god of silos and grain

Consus

Consus, also called Conso, was a very ancient god of Roman religion, linked to the protection of stored grain and to agricultural rites. His underground altar in the Circus Maximus makes him a chthonic deity, one tied to the earth.

The myth

Ancient texts give Consus few stories of his own, so much of what we know comes from his cult and from later interpretations. According to tradition, the abduction of the Sabine women took place during the first celebration of his festival in the time of Romulus, and some authors held that the god himself had advised that episode, linking his cult to the founding of Rome.

Late writers, struck by his resemblance to consilium (council), reinterpreted him as a god of councils and secret deliberations. Tertullian records an inscription on one of the turning posts of the Circus that called Consus powerful in counsel, Mars in war and the Lares in the home. Plutarch saw in his subterranean altar a symbol of the secret nature of counsel, and Augustine of Hippo still mentions him as the one who gave advice to children.

Livy and Dionysius of Halicarnassus sometimes identified him with Equestrian Neptune, god of horses, because of the races held at his festivals, although outside those festivals the two gods shared no functions.

Worship and religion

His main festivals were the Consualia, held on August 21 and December 15. During them, priests and the flamen Quirinalis offered sacrifices at his altar, which according to some sources was covered with earth that was swept away for the occasion. There were horse and mule races, with the animals garlanded with flowers and given a rest from work, along with rustic games such as running over greased ox hides.

Ancient sources: Tertullian, Servius, Plutarch, Livy, Dionysius of Halicarnassus and Augustine of Hippo mention him, and Varro links the Consualia to his name.

Trivia

An underground altar. According to some sources, his altar was buried or covered with earth, and that earth was swept away during his festivals, when the races were held in the Circus Maximus.
A name with a double reading. His name resembled the Latin word consilium, so late writers interpreted him as a god of counsel, although modern philologists doubt that the two words share the same root.
