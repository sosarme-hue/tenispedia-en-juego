second of the six Buddhist heavens, abode of Śakra

Trāyastriṃśa

Trāyastriṃśa is the second of the six heavens of the desire realm in Buddhist cosmology, located on the peak of Mount Sumeru and ruled by Śakra. Its name, meaning “thirty-three,” comes from Vedic mythology and refers to the pantheon of devas who dwell there.

The myth

According to Buddhist tradition, the heaven has its origin in a time before the historical Buddha. A village leader named Magha gathered thirty-three companions to do good deeds. When they died, he was reborn as Śakra, king of the devas, and his companions as the gods of this heaven.

The asuras, who then lived in the heaven, prepared a feast with sura, an intoxicating drink. Śakra warned his gods not to drink, but the asuras became drunk and were thrown down from Sumeru. They woke at the foot of the mountain, swore never to drink sura (hence their name, “not-sura”), and have since tried to win back their home by fighting the devas.

Because it is the only one of the six heavens physically connected to the world, its inhabitants cannot escape worldly affairs. They often quarrel with the asuras, though marriages between the two sides also occur, much as the Æsir and jötnar intermarry in Norse myth. Among its residents are Viśvakarman, the devas’ architect, and Mātali, the driver of Śakra’s chariot. The Buddha’s mother, Māyā, reborn in the Tuṣita heaven, came down to visit, and there her son taught her the abhidharma.

Ancient sources: Its name derives from the Vedas, which mention the thirty-three gods; Buddhist cosmology preserves it in works such as those of Vasubandhu.

Trivia

A celestial elephant. Airāvata, Śakra’s elephant mount, is among its residents. Pārileyyaka, an elephant that was reborn in the heaven after serving the Buddha, is also listed.
Not a headcount. The number thirty-three does not count the gods who live there, since there are far more. It is a general term inherited from Vedic mythology.
