Roman epithet of the goddess of childbirth

Lucina

Lucina was the epithet by which the Romans invoked the divine protector of childbirth. It was linked mainly to Juno and sometimes to Diana, and its Greek counterpart was Eileithyia.

The myth

Lucina has hardly any story of her own: as an epithet she was not a figure with an independent biography, but an aspect of Juno or Diana. According to Varro, she was one of the Sabine deities the Romans adopted. Ovid explains her name as 'she who brings children into the light', from lux, though it could also come from lucus, the sacred lotus grove on the Esquiline linked to the goddess.

She was shown as a matron holding a cup in her right hand and a spear in her left, or seated with a newborn in her lap and a flower in her right hand.

Worship and religion

Her ceremonies involved garlands and wreaths of flowers. Priests of Juno called her Juno Covella at the new moon, when the Moon's light was linked to female fertility and the length of pregnancy. Other birth deities worked alongside her, such as Vagitanus, who opened the newborn's mouth to cry, and Fabulinus, who enabled its first speech. The di nixi had an altar in the Campus Martius. The sources do not say whether this cult survives today.

Legacy

Her name lives on in the asteroid 146 Lucina. An extinct fossil ostracod, Luprisca incuba, was also named after this aspect of the goddess.

Ancient sources: Varro mentions her among the Sabine deities adopted by Rome, and Ovid explains the origin of her name.

Trivia

An asteroid named for her. Asteroid 146 Lucina, discovered in the 19th century, is named after the Roman goddess.
