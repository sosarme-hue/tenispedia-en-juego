castle and court of King Arthur

Camelot

Camelot is the legendary castle and court of King Arthur, the fantastic capital of his realm and a symbol of the whole Arthurian world. Its location is never precisely given, and most scholars regard it as an entirely fictional setting.

The myth

Camelot is absent from the earliest Arthurian material. It first appears in Chrétien de Troyes's Lancelot, the Knight of the Cart, from the 1170s, where it is mentioned only in passing and never described; there, Arthur's chief court is still Caerleon in Wales. Over time, in the 13th-century French prose romances, Camelot began to supersede Caerleon and inherited its grand architecture, its many churches and the courtesy of its inhabitants.

The Lancelot-Grail cycle places the city along a river, downstream from Astolat, surrounded by plains and forests. Its cathedral, St Stephen's, is the religious centre of the Knights of the Round Table, where Arthur and Guinevere marry and where many kings and knights are buried. In its castle stands the Round Table, made by Merlin for Uther Pendragon; there Galahad claims the Siege Perilous and the knights glimpse the Holy Grail. In some later works, King Mark of Cornwall razes Camelot to the ground after the Battle of Camlann, while in others it is simply abandoned and falls to ruin after Arthur's death.

The oldest Welsh tradition placed Arthur's hall at Celliwig, in Cornwall, and the Welsh Triads give him several courts, one in each region inhabited by the Celtic Britons. This shows how strongly Arthur was tied to many places and to none in particular.

Legacy

In the 15th century Thomas Malory fixed the image of Camelot best known today in Le Morte d'Arthur and identified it with Winchester in England; his editor William Caxton, however, preferred a Welsh location. Ever since, the search for the "real Camelot" has continued in popular works and for tourism purposes. Some scholars have proposed that its name comes from Camlann, the site of Arthur's last battle, or from Cavalon, a place name that Roger Sherman Loomis linked to Avalon. Arthurian scholar Norris J. Lacy summed up the problem in one line: "Camelot, located nowhere in particular, can be anywhere."

Ancient sources: Chrétien de Troyes, Lancelot, the Knight of the Cart (c. 1170s); Geoffrey of Monmouth, History of the Kings of Britain; Culhwch and Olwen, within the Mabinogion; the Welsh Triads.

Trivia

An uncertain name. Medieval French romances record many different spellings of the name, and its origin remains unclear.
Echoes of Camulodunum. John Morris suggested that the name may recall Camulodunum, the former capital of Roman Britain, in the memory of Romanized Britons.
An English exception. The poem Sir Gawain and the Green Knight places Arthur's court at Camelot, which was unusual for English literature of the period.
