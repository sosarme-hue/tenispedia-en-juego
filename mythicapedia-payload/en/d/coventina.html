Celtic goddess of wells and springs

Coventina

Coventina was a goddess of wells and springs, linked to abundance and fertility. Her cult, rooted in Celtic tradition, is documented mainly in Roman Britain, especially in the north, and is known chiefly through inscriptions and offerings.

The myth

Ancient texts say very little about Coventina. Almost everything we know comes from votive inscriptions and sculpted images, so her mythical figure is reconstructed largely from archaeology.

These images show her as a water nymph, reclining and partly clothed, often beside her spring. On some altars she appears in triple form or with two attendants, following the conventions of Roman nymph imagery used for divinities of springs.

Worship and religion

Her sanctuary stood beside a spring at Carrawburgh, near the Roman fort on Hadrian’s Wall. The water was enclosed in a walled area with a rectangular basin, and for generations offerings were left there: around 13,487 coins, altars dedicated to Coventina and Minerva, incense burners and reliefs of nymphs. Soldiers were among the worshippers, including the prefect of the First Cohort of Batavians, who left a dedication. The site is thought to have been abandoned around 388, perhaps because of the anti-pagan edicts of Theodosius I. No living cult is documented.

Legacy

Some scholars, including Roger Sherman Loomis, John Rhys and Caitlin Matthews, argue that the Lady of the Lake in Arthurian legend has roots in Coventina, citing parallels such as guarding waters, offerings of weapons and links with healing. In this view the name changed through medieval French manuscripts until it became Viviane. Jack Whyte presents her as the inspiration for the Lady of the Lake in his novel The Skystone, and Seamus Heaney wrote the poem “Grotus and Conventina” in his 1987 collection The Haw Lantern.

Ancient sources: Votive inscriptions on stone and pottery found at Carrawburgh; no ancient literary texts are known to mention her.

Trivia

Coins in the well. The well yielded around 13,487 coins, dating from Mark Antony to Gratian.
A Heaney poem. The Irish Nobel laureate Seamus Heaney dedicated the poem “Grotus and Conventina” to her in 1987.
