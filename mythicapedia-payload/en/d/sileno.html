aged satyr and tutor of Dionysus in Greek myth

Silenus

Silenus is an elderly satyr, wise and almost always drunk, who in Greek tradition taught and accompanied Dionysus. His figure, halfway between the divine and the grotesque, illustrates both hidden wisdom behind an unattractive appearance and the festive world of the wine god.

The myth

According to myth, Silenus was the teacher and faithful companion of Dionysus, described as the oldest, wisest and most drunken of his followers. When intoxicated he possessed special knowledge and prophetic powers, but he was vulnerable to mortals who captured him while he slept.

The Phrygian king Midas trapped him by mixing wine into a fountain he drank from, or, in another version, peasants found him and brought him to the king. Midas hosted him lavishly, and Dionysus, grateful, granted Midas the power to turn everything he touched into gold, a gift that soon became a curse. Silenus also voiced a pessimistic philosophy: it is best not to be born, and the next best thing is to die as soon as possible.

In Euripides' satyr play Cyclops, Silenus serves as a slave to Polyphemus and acts as go-between with Odysseus, lamenting his lost freedom and the festivities of Dionysus.

Worship and religion

Pausanias mentions a temple of Silenus in Elis, which held an image of Methe, Drunkenness, pouring wine for him.

Legacy

In Plato's Symposium, Alcibiades compares Socrates to the statues of Silenus that open to reveal gods inside. The word became a Latin term of abuse, and the figure lent its name to a dance and to decorative figures in Roman art.

Ancient sources: Euripides (Cyclops), Ovid (Metamorphoses), Plato (Symposium), Plautus (Rudens) and Pausanias.
