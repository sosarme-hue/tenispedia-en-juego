Norse god of war and justice

Tyr

Tyr is the god of war and law in Norse and Germanic mythology, best known for losing a hand to the wolf Fenrir. His name survives in weekday names and runes, suggesting that he once held a more central place among the Germanic gods.

The myth

According to the Poetic Edda, Tyr is the son of the giant Hymir and of a woman named Hrod, while Snorri Sturluson, in the Prose Edda, makes him a son of Odin and Frigg. The two genealogies do not agree, and scholars read the difference as a sign of separate traditions that later merged.

The best-known episode tells how the gods tried to bind the wolf Fenrir, who broke every chain they made. Dwarves then forged Gleipnir, a magical ribbon made of impossible things such as a woman's beard and the roots of a mountain. Fenrir suspected a trick and agreed to be tied only if one of the gods placed a hand in his mouth as a pledge. Tyr, renowned for his courage, offered his hand. When the wolf realised he had been deceived, he bit it off.

The tradition adds that Tyr was left-handed, which was then linked to bad luck. At Ragnarök he will kill and be killed by Garm, the hound guarding Helheim.

Worship and religion

According to a Roman writer, the Goths of the third century sacrificed captives taken in battle to Tyz, their war god, and hung the victims' arms in trees. This custom seems later to have been transferred to Odin in Scandinavia. Tyr is thought to have been the chief god among the eastern Germanic tribes until Odin overtook him in the north and west, perhaps helped by an old custom of shared rule between two figures.

Legacy

Tyr's name gives us Tuesday, 'day of Tīw', in English and other Germanic languages, while the Latin world dedicated the day to Mars. The rune ᛏ, called Tiwaz, bears his name. Traces of his name survive in Scandinavian place names such as the Tiveden forest and in plant names in Old Norse, such as týsfiola.

Ancient sources: The Poetic Edda and Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda recount his deeds, and the Gothic Tyz appears in a Vienna manuscript.

Trivia

A day of the week. English Tuesday comes from Tyr's name rather than directly from Mars, although the Latin day was dedicated to the Roman god.
A lost hand. The loss of his hand to Fenrir earned him the epithet of one-handed god in the Faroese rune poem.
