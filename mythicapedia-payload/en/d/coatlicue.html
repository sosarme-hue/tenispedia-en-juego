Aztec goddess of earth, fertility and mother of the gods

Coatlicue

Coatlicue is a goddess of Mexica mythology linked to the earth, fertility and the cycle of life and death. Her name means roughly 'she of the skirt of snakes', and she is the mother of Huitzilopochtli, the god of the sun and of war.

The myth

According to the Mexica story, Coatlicue lived on Coatepec hill, where she did penance and performed ritual duties such as sweeping the sanctuary. While sweeping, she picked up a heavenly feather and tucked it into her bosom; when she finished, the feather had vanished, and she was pregnant with Huitzilopochtli.

Her daughter Coyolxauhqui, leading her brothers, saw the pregnancy as a dishonor and plotted to kill her. As they climbed the hill, Huitzilopochtli was born fully armed, defeated his siblings, beheaded Coyolxauhqui and cast her body down the slope. The accounts differ: some make Coyolxauhqui an elder sister rather than a daughter, and in some versions she becomes the moon.

Another version says Coatlicue was one of the women who sacrificed themselves at Teotihuacan so that the present Sun could begin. Afterward, their skirts came back to life and were worshipped by the Aztecs.

Worship and religion

She was also called Tonantzin, 'our venerated mother', and Teteoh Innan, 'mother of the gods'. At the Templo Mayor in Tenochtitlan, the great pyramid crowned by the temple of Huitzilopochtli represented Coatepec hill, and the monolith of Coyolxauhqui, showing her dismembered body, lay at its base. Scholars have suggested that sacrifices on the summit recalled the goddess's fall down the slope.

Legacy

The famous Coatlicue statue, found on 13 August 1790 during leveling work in the main plaza of Mexico City, is now displayed in the National Museum of Anthropology. In 2018, researchers at UNAM named a freshwater fish species Vieja coatlicue after her. The band La Castañeda dedicated the song 'Tumba-matriz' to her, and the band Porter mentions her in 'Tzunami'.
