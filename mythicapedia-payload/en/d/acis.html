god of the Acis River in Greek and Roman mythology

Acis

Acis is the god of the river of the same name that rises near Mount Etna, in Sicily. His story, tied to his love for the Nereid Galatea and to the rivalry with the Cyclops Polyphemus, is one of the classical myths that has inspired the most poets, composers and painters over the centuries.

The myth

According to Ovid, in his Metamorphoses, Acis was the son of the god Faunus and the nymph Symaethis. He fell in love with the Nereid Galatea, and the two spent their days together by the sea. The jealous Cyclops Polyphemus tore an enormous boulder from the slope of Etna and hurled it at the young man, who was crushed to death.

The tradition holds that Galatea turned Acis's blood into a spring flowing from beneath the rock, the origin of the river that bears his name. In Ovid's version, the Nereid transformed her lover into a horned river god with a deep blue face. Other sources, however, say that Acis turned himself into a river to escape the rock. According to some later traditions, Galatea eventually yielded to Polyphemus, and their son Galates became the ancestor of the Gauls.

Legacy

The episode inspired Luis de Góngora's narrative poem Fábula de Polifemo y Galatea (1627) and several operas: Lully's Acis et Galatée (1686) and Handel's Acis and Galatea (1718), with a libretto by John Gay. Porpora and Haydn also set the story to music. In painting, Poussin, Claude Lorrain and Perrier depicted the lovers and the Cyclops.

Ancient sources: Ovid's Metamorphoses is the ancient source that recounts his love for Galatea and his transformation into a river.

Trivia

A Handel opera. Handel first set the story in Italy in 1708 and later reworked it in England as a pastoral with an English libretto.
A political satire. According to the scholar Athenaeus, the story was first devised by Philoxenus of Cythera as a satire against the Sicilian tyrant Dionysius I of Syracuse, whose favourite concubine was also named Galatea.
