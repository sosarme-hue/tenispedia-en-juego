Odysseus's cowherd in Greek mythology

Philoetius

Philoetius is Odysseus's chief cowherd in Homer's Odyssey, a figure from Greek mythology who stays loyal to his master throughout his long absence. He shows the loyalty of humble servants at a time when other slaves in the household turn against their lord.

The myth

According to Homer, Philoetius is Odysseus's principal cowherd. Throughout the twenty years his master is away from Ithaca he remains faithful to him, and when Odysseus returns he is one of the few slaves who has not betrayed him. His figure stands out among the household servants, many of whom side with the suitors.

Just before the climax, Odysseus orders him, together with Eumaeus, to lock the palace doors so that no suitor can escape, and to steal and hide their weapons so they cannot fight back. During the final battle, Philoetius joins the fight and kills the suitor Peisander. Afterwards, Odysseus promises freedom and power to Philoetius and Eumaeus for having remained loyal when the other slaves betrayed him.

Ancient sources: Homer's Odyssey, where he appears as Odysseus's loyal cowherd.
