legendary island in medieval Arabic geography

Wakwak (Waq Waq)

Wakwak, also spelled Waq Waq, is an island, or perhaps several, in medieval Arabic geographical and imaginative literature, usually placed far away in the seas of China. Its real location remains debated, with proposals ranging from Japan to Madagascar and Indonesia.

The myth

In Arabic versions, the island is ruled by a queen and inhabited only by women, an image that al-Qazwini's manuscripts show with the queen surrounded by her attendants. A distinctive motif is the Waq Waq tree: female figures grow from its branches, ripen like fruit and fall to the ground crying out what sounds like 'Waq Waq!'. An Andalusian version compares these women to beautiful fruit.

Geographical sources offer more down-to-earth features. Ibn Khordadbeh describes a land so rich in gold that its people make chains for dogs and collars for monkeys, and which exports ebony. Buzurg ibn Shahriyar, in the Ajayib al-Hind, reports that in 945 about a thousand ships attacked Qanbaloh on the African coast and failed to take it.

Legacy

Al-Qazwini's illustrated manuscripts are its best-known artistic presence, and its name has fuelled scholarly theories about maritime routes across the Indian Ocean.

Ancient sources: Ibn Khordadbeh (9th century), Buzurg ibn Shahriyar in the Ajayib al-Hind and al-Qazwini in his Wonders of Creation; the Chinese encyclopedist Du Huan, in the Tongdian, records an Arab account of a tree that grows children.

Trivia

A name possibly from Japan. Michael Jan de Goeje proposed that the name derives from a Cantonese form used for Japan. It is a hypothesis, not an established fact.
Dogs with gold collars. Tomé Pires, in his Suma Oriental, says that Java had many fine hounds with collars and rings of gold and silver, which matches Ibn Khordadbeh's description.
