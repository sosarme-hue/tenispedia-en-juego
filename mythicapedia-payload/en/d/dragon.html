Dragon

A serpentine creature found in the folklore of nearly every culture, often with features borrowed from other animals such as wings and claws. In the West it usually embodies evil and destruction, while in East Asia it is linked to rain, water and good fortune.

The myth

The word comes from the Greek drákōn, meaning 'serpent', a term that originally referred to any great snake rather than only to mythical beings. It is probably connected with dérkomai, 'to see', perhaps because of the piercing gaze attributed to serpents. The oldest accounts of dragon-like creatures come from Mesopotamia, and many mythologies share the theme of a storm god battling a giant serpent.

In the Babylonian epic Enuma Elish, written around 2000 BC, the goddess Tiamat, associated with the oceans, appears as a dragon who leads the forces of chaos. Her destruction makes possible the creation of an ordered cosmos. In Egypt, Apep is a giant serpent who lives in the Duat, the underworld, and tries to stop the sun god Ra on his nightly journey. According to the tradition, his roars caused thunderstorms and earthquakes, and his attacks brought about eclipses. A papyrus written around 310 BC preserves this ancient account, and in some versions the god Set defeats him.

In Greek myth, dracones were huge serpents with supernatural powers, such as the Lernaean Hydra or Ladon, which heroes and gods slew in their stories. In Norse-influenced Germanic lore, Níðhöggr dwells in the depths of the underworld, and in Persian myth Azi Dahaka is a wicked dragon. In East Asia, by contrast, Chinese, Japanese and Korean dragons are benevolent beings who govern rain, seas and rivers and bring good fortune to those who honour them.

Worship and religion

In China, dragons were associated with rain and water, and from the Han dynasty onwards they became emblems of imperial power. In Japan, for centuries, they were the official emblem of the imperial family. In several East Asian cultures they are still revered as representatives of the primal forces of nature and the universe. In ancient Greece, according to cult tradition, draconic serpents were gentle figures linked to healing and wealth, a striking contrast with their violent roles in myth. In medieval Christian Europe, the figure became an enemy that had to be destroyed.

Legacy

The dragon has inspired many works of modern fiction and film, notably by J. R. R. Tolkien, Ursula K. Le Guin and George R. R. Martin. The English word drake, another term for a mythological dragon, descends from the Old English draca. The flag of Dacia, in present-day Romania, features a dragon, and in medieval alchemy the ouroboros, the serpent eating its own tail, became a winged dragon with legs and a tail.

Ancient sources: The Babylonian Enuma Elish, the Bremner-Rhind papyrus and the Amduat from Egypt, the Rigveda with its serpent Vṛtra, the Hebrew Bible with Leviathan and the Book of Revelation, and Germanic poetry with Níðhöggr.

Trivia

Dragon bones. In China, fossils of large prehistoric animals were identified as dragon bones and are still used in traditional medicine.
Jade amulets. The earliest known dragons are jade amulets from the Hongshan culture in China, dated to between 6,700 and 4,900 years ago.
