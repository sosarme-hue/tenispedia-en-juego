mountain spirit woman in Japanese folklore

Yama-uba

Yama-uba, the «mountain nurse», is a spirit of Japanese folklore who dwells in deep forests and appears as an old woman who may be either a predator or a protector. She matters because she combines the dread of mountain paths with an ambiguous maternal image.

The myth

According to the tales, Yama-uba appears as a wizened crone with long white hair and a tattered kimono, and sometimes with a second mouth on the crown of her head, hidden by her hair. She lies in wait for travellers and merchants lost in the mountains. Sometimes she poses as a helpless old woman who offers shelter and then devours her guests; at other times she takes the form of a beautiful woman or of a loved one of the victim, and her hair can turn into serpents that lift the unfortunate traveller into the air. Skilled in witchcraft and poisons, she occasionally bargains with humans, trading for substitute prey.

Tradition also shows her in a kinder light. In one story she raised the orphan Kintarō, who later became the warrior Sakata no Kintoki. In another, a mother giving birth in a mountain hut discovers too late that her helper is a yamauba intent on eating her son. In tales such as Nukafuku Komefuku, she rewards the kind elder sister with wealth and punishes the rude younger one.

Worship and religion

The sources do not describe an organised cult, but in Kōchi some families venerated her as a protective household deity, since a yamauba who lived in a house was believed to bring wealth and prosperity.

Legacy

Her name gave rise to the Yamanba Gyaru fashion of the 1990s. She appears in the nō drama Yamauba, which portrays her as the personified mountain, and in writings by Lafcadio Hearn, who presented her to Western readers as a nurse who devours children. The writer Steve Berman included her in a modern short story.

Ancient sources: Noh dramas, including the one by Konparu Zenchiku, and folk tales recorded by folklorists.

Trivia

A fashion named after her. The Yamanba Gyaru fashion of the 1990s took its name from this legend, though it has no connection to the spirit beyond the name.
A fall into buckwheat. In one tale, a person chased by a yamauba climbs a chain that descends from the sky; when she follows by climbing too, she falls to her death in a field of buckwheat.
