Creusa

Creusa, also called Glauce, was the daughter of King Creon of Corinth and the wife of Jason, for whom he abandoned Medea. Her death, caused by a poisoned robe, is one of the best-known episodes in the Medea and Jason cycle of Greek myth.

The myth

According to the tradition, Jason married Creusa after abandoning Medea, who had been his ally and the mother of his children. Medea plotted revenge by pretending to make peace: she sent her own sons, Feres and Mermeros, to bring the princess a robe and a diadem soaked in poison as a gift.

As soon as Creusa put on the finery, her skin and hair began to burn. Her father, Creon, rushed to help her, but the poison reached him too, and he died beside her. Medea, overcome by madness, also killed her own children to wound Jason. The accounts differ in detail: Hyginus describes a golden crown, while Pseudo-Apollodorus speaks of a robe steeped in poison and recalls that Medea called on the gods by whom Jason had sworn.

Worship and religion

Near the agora of Corinth, on the road towards Sicyon, stood a temple and a bronze image of Apollo, and a little further on a fountain called the Fountain of Glauce. According to the story, Glauce threw herself into its waters, thinking they would be a remedy against Medea's poisons. Because her death had been violent and unjust, and she was said to be destroying the young children of the Corinthians, an oracle of the god ordained yearly sacrifices in her honour and the erection of a statue of Deima, 'Terror'. According to the source, this figure of a woman who inspires terror still existed in its author's time.

Ancient sources: Hyginus and Pseudo-Apollodorus tell her story, and the Greek tragedians included her in their accounts of Medea's revenge.

Trivia

A colour name. The name Glauce derives from the Greek glaukós, meaning something like 'bluish-grey'.
The Fountain of Glauce. Beside the road from Corinth to Sicyon stood a fountain named after the princess, into whose waters she is said to have thrown herself.
The statue of Terror. The statue of Deima, 'Terror', showed a woman who inspired fear and was erected following an oracle.
