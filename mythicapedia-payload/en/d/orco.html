Roman god of death and the underworld

Orcus

Orcus is the Roman god of death and the underworld, often confused with the realm of the dead itself. His name survives in the Italian word orco and the English word ogre, and in modern fantasy.

The myth

In Roman tradition, Orcus was a god of the underworld who gradually merged with Pluto and Dis Pater. Varro made him a brother of Jupiter and Neptune and credited him with the abduction of Proserpina. According to Hyginus, Saturn cast Orcus beneath Tartarus, just as he threw Neptune beneath the waves, fearing that his sons would take his kingdom.

His best-known role is that of the one who carries out death: Virgil calls him "pale Orcus", and Horace describes him reaping great and small alike. Ancient writers disagreed about his origin. Some saw him as a native Italic deity, while others linked him to Horkos, the Greek personification of oaths, or to Etruscan influence, which is poorly supported.

Worship and religion

Despite the name's popularity, there is scarcely any evidence of worship. Votive dedications are extremely rare, and the temple on the Palatine Hill appears only in the Historia Augusta and is considered apocryphal. One festival is known, the "wedding of Orcus", linked by Servius to Ceres and marked by a ban on wine, along with an epithet, Quietalis, "god of rest".

Legacy

The name left its mark on language: the Italian orco names the monster of fairy tales that eats human flesh, and it is linked to the English ogre. Ariosto turned him into a bestial Cyclops in Orlando Furioso. Tolkien drew at least in part on him for the orcs of The Lord of the Rings, and Dungeons & Dragons made him a demon prince of the undead. He also gave his name to the trans-Neptunian object (90482) Orcus.

Ancient sources: Virgil (Georgics), Plautus, Ennius, Lucretius, Varro, Hyginus, Horace and Tibullus mention him.

Trivia

A name with a Greek echo. Its resemblance to Horkos, the Greek god of oaths, led ancient writers to describe Orcus as a punisher of perjurers.
A doubtful temple. The supposed temple of Orcus on the Palatine appears only in the Historia Augusta, and many regard it as apocryphal.
