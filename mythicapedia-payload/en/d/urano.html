Uranus

Uranus is the personification of the sky and one of the primordial deities of Greek mythology. Son and husband of Gaia, the Earth, he fathered the first generation of Titans, from whom much of the Greek pantheon descends.

The myth

In Hesiod's Theogony, Gaia first bore Uranus, equal to herself, to cover her on every side and serve as a secure dwelling for the blessed gods. With Gaia, Uranus then produced the Titans, the Cyclopes and the Hundred-Handers, but he hated them and hid them inside the Earth.

Irritated, Gaia fashioned an adamantine sickle and urged her children to punish their father. Only Cronus, the youngest Titan, agreed. He ambushed Uranus as he lay with Gaia, castrated him with the sickle and threw the genitals into the sea. From the blood that fell upon the earth came the Giants, the Erinyes and the Meliae, and from the foam at sea Aphrodite was born. Uranus warned that the deed would be avenged in the future.

Apollodorus's Library gives a different version: Uranus casts his first children into Tartarus, and Gaia persuades the Titans to attack him. After Uranus is defeated, the Titans free their brothers. Hesiod and Apollodorus agree that Uranus and Gaia foretold that Cronus would be overthrown by a son, and that Zeus escaped that fate through a trick. A rationalizing account by Diodorus Siculus makes Uranus a mortal king of the Atlanteans, an astronomer and civilizer who was deified after death.

Other authors give him different parents: according to the lost Titanomachy he was the son of Aether; according to the Orphic texts, of Phanes and Nyx; and Hesiod presents him as born of Gaia alone. After the castration, Uranus recedes into the background of the myth.

Worship and religion

No cult addressed directly to Uranus survived into classical times. His presence in Greek religion is reduced to solemn formulas, such as the joint invocation of Earth, Sky and Styx in the Iliad, and the use of 'sky' as an alternative name for Olympus, the collective home of the gods.

Legacy

The name gave rise to the element uranium and to the planet Uranus, discovered in 1781 and named after him. His Roman counterpart is Caelus, the sky. Uranus also appears on the Gigantomachy frieze of the Pergamon Altar, fighting the Giants.

Ancient sources: Hesiod's Theogony, Homer's Iliad and Apollodorus's Library, along with the lost Titanomachy and Cicero's De natura deorum.

Trivia

A planet named after him. The planet Uranus was discovered in 1781 and named after the Greek god of the sky, following the tradition of naming planets after mythological deities.
A sickle with a disputed resting place. Several sites in Sicily and Achaea claimed to be where Cronus threw the sickle, and Drepanum, modern Trapani, takes its name from the Greek word for 'sickle'.
