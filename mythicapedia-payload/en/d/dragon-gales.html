red dragon, national symbol of Wales

Welsh Dragon

The Welsh Dragon, known in Welsh as Y Ddraig Goch ("the red dragon"), is the national emblem of Wales and appears on its flag. Its roots lie in the dragon images that Britons once associated with their war leaders, later shaped by medieval tradition into a struggle between a red dragon and a white one.

The myth

According to Welsh tradition, two dragons, one white and one red, lay beneath the land, and their fights were so destructive that their roar left anyone who heard it barren. King Lludd asked his wise brother Llefelys for advice, and Llefelys proposed digging a great pit, filling it with mead and luring the beasts to drink until they became drunk. The plan only partly worked: the dragons were trapped underground, but they remained alive.

Centuries later, King Gwrtheyrn tried to build a castle at Dinas Emrys, but its walls kept collapsing. A boy named Emrys, linked to Merlin, revealed that two dragons fought beneath the foundations. Once freed, the red dragon defeated the white one. Later, King Uther Pendragon took the red dragon as the emblem of his line and of Wales, and it passed on to his son Arthur.

Legacy

The red dragon appears on the flag of Wales, official since 1959. The Tudors used it in their heraldry after Bosworth, and Owain Glyndŵr raised a golden dragon standard against the English. Geoffrey of Monmouth linked it to Arthur, and the legend remains alive in popular culture, where the white dragon stands for the Saxons.

Ancient sources: Gildas, around 540, called the British chieftain Maelgwn Gwynedd the "insularis draco"; Nennius, in the Historia Brittonum, was the first to give the dragon the colour red; the story also appears in the Mabinogion, in the tale of Lludd and Llefelys.

Trivia

A colour for the first time. Nennius's Historia Brittonum is the first text that explicitly calls the dragon red, though Y Gododdin may contain an earlier hint.
A Roman standard. Romans carried the draco, a standard with a dragon head, and the draconarius was its bearer. This tradition may have influenced the Welsh symbol.
