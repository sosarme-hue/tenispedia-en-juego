Romano-British god of war and forests

Cocidius

Cocidius was a deity worshipped in northern Britain under Roman rule, especially along Hadrian's Wall. The Romans identified him with Mars and with Silvanus, and his name may be linked to the colour red.

The myth

Few stories about Cocidius survive, so his myth is almost unknown. He is known mainly from inscriptions and stone carvings.

He is thought to have been a protective spirit of a place, a genius loci, honoured by local Britons as a tribal god. Lower-ranked Roman soldiers also worshipped him.

Worship and religion

Altars were dedicated to him at forts along Hadrian's Wall and in Cumbria, such as Birdoswald and Bewcastle. An inscription at Ebchester calls him Cocidius Vernostonus, 'Cocidius of the alder tree'. At Yardhope, a relief figure brandishing spear and shield stands beside a small shrine.

Legacy

According to William A. Young, characters such as the 'Dark Man' of the Welsh romance Owain and the Brown Man of the Moor in John Leyden's poem may have their origins in Cocidius.

Ancient sources: Roman inscriptions from northern Britain and the Ravenna Cosmography, which records the place-name Fanocodi.

Trivia

The Red One. A figure found in the 1980s at the Otterburn Training Area is known as the Red One. Its name may refer to the possibility that statues of the god were painted red.
A 2000-year-old carving. In 2006 a carving of Cocidius nicknamed the little man was found near Chesters Fort, showing a figure with arms flung wide, holding a shield and sword.
