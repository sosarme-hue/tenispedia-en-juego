Oread of Greek mythology

Echo

Echo is an Oread, a mountain nymph linked to Mount Cithaeron in Greek mythology. Her story, especially her unrequited love for Narcissus, gave rise to the explanation of the echo and became a symbol of a voice that survives without a body.

The myth

In Ovid's Metamorphoses, Juno punished Echo for distracting her with long conversations while Jupiter pursued other nymphs. From then on, Echo could only repeat the last words she heard. When she saw Narcissus hunting in the fields, she fell in love with him and followed him in silence. When he asked whether anyone was there, she answered with his own words. As she emerged from the trees to embrace him, Narcissus rejected her harshly. Humiliated, she hid in woods and caves, and her body slowly wasted away until only her voice and her bones remained, which turned to stone. When Narcissus died beside the pool, Echo repeated his last farewell.

Longus, in Daphnis and Chloe, tells a very different story. Echo was said to be the daughter of a nymph and a mortal man, raised among the nymphs and taught music by the Muses, and she remained a virgin. Pan, envious of her musical talent and her chastity, drove the men of the countryside mad, and they tore her apart. Gaia hid the fragments, which still sing and imitate any sound of the earth.

Other texts add further details. According to the Suda, Echo bore Pan a daughter, Iynx. Nonnus, in the Dionysiaca, says that Pan chased her without ever winning her affection, and that during the Deluge Echo feared Poseidon. Pausanias rejects the romantic tale and records a local legend in which Narcissus gazed at his reflection to remember his dead twin sister.

Legacy

Calderón de la Barca wrote Eco y Narciso, and Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz wrote El Divino Narciso, during Spain's Golden Age. In the Renaissance, dialogue with an echo became a popular poetic device in pastoral drama, lyric poetry and opera. Victor Hugo used the nymph as an image of the poetic voice, and Christina Rosenvinge dedicated a song to the myth on her album La joven Dolores.

Ancient sources: Ovid's Metamorphoses, Longus' Daphnis and Chloe, Nonnus' Dionysiaca, the Suda, Photius' Bibliotheca, and the Homeric and Orphic Hymns to Pan.

Trivia

Colonnades that repeat the voice. Pausanias describes a colonnade at Olympia that repeats a human voice seven times or more, and another at Hermione that repeats it at least three times. Locals called both of them Echo Colonnades.
A mortal Echo. In Longus' version, Echo is not a nymph but a mortal, the daughter of a nymph and a human father, which changes her relationship with the gods entirely.
