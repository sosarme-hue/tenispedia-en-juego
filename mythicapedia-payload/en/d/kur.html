underworld of Mesopotamian mythology

Kur

Kur is the Sumerian name of the underworld, the realm of the dead in Mesopotamian cosmology. The name is usually translated as «ground» or «earth», though it can also mean «mountain», and the goddess Ereshkigal ruled there.

The myth

According to Sumerian tradition, Kur was a dark, dusty place lying deep beneath the earth, below the Abzu, the body of freshwater Mesopotamians imagined under the ground. It had seven gates, each secured with bolts and watched by the gatekeeper Neti. Its queen was the goddess Ereshkigal, who lived in the palace of Ganzir. In the older stories her husband was Gugalanna, while in later tales the god Nergal takes that role.

The dead were not judged or rewarded for their conduct in life. They appeared before Ereshkigal, who pronounced them dead, and the scribal goddess Geshtinanna recorded their names. Down there they had only dust to eat, so they depended on libations poured by their descendants from above. Those with no children suffered most, according to these texts.

In the hymn Inanna and Utu, the goddess Inanna asks her brother, the sun god Utu, to take her to Kur so she can taste the fruit of a tree that would reveal to her the secrets of sex. Utu complies, and she eats it. A different account presents Kur as a serpent-dragon who supposedly abducted Ereshkigal, but the detailed sources do not confirm this.

Worship and religion

The sources mainly describe funerary rituals. Relatives poured libations through a clay pipe down to the grave so the dead could drink, and having many descendants to continue this was considered essential. A proper burial was also believed to improve the dead person's treatment in the afterlife, while those left unburied were thought to cease to exist. This practice is no longer observed.

Ancient sources: The Sumerian myths «Inanna and Utu», «Nergal and Ereshkigal» and «In Those Distant Days», which describe the underworld, its inhabitants and its rules.

Trivia

River of the underworld. Some rivers far from Sumer were called the «river of the underworld». This suggests that one tradition placed the realm in a remote area, perhaps in the west.
Demons of the underworld. It was home to demons such as Lamashtu, a devourer of children, and Pazuzu, a wind demon who also served as a protector.
