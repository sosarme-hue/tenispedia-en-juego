Greek goddess of vegetation and queen of the underworld

Persephone

Persephone is the Greek goddess of vegetation and grain, daughter of Zeus and Demeter, who became queen of the underworld after marrying Hades. She matters for the myth of her abduction and return, which explains the cycle of the seasons, and for her central role in the Eleusinian Mysteries.

The myth

According to the most widespread version, Hades abducted Persephone and took her to the underworld as his wife, making her its queen. Her mother Demeter grieves over the loss of her daughter, and the myth goes on to tell of Persephone's return to the surface. Tradition also knows her as Kore, meaning 'maiden' or 'girl', and the Romans called her Proserpina.

In Homer's Odyssey, when Odysseus visits the underworld, he calls her the 'Queen of Iron' and finds her beside Hades, sharing control over the dead. The Orphic Hymns give her a different family, presenting Dionysus and Melinoe as children of Zeus and Persephone. Ancient sources disagree about her parents. Most name Zeus and Demeter, but Apollodorus records a version in which she is the daughter of Zeus and Styx, and the Orphics say she was born from a union of Zeus, in serpent form, with his own mother Rhea.

As for marriage, the most common tradition holds that Persephone and Hades had no children, since she was barren. The Orphics, however, speak of the Eumenides as their daughters.

Worship and religion

Persephone shared the heart of the Eleusinian Mysteries with her mother, initiatory rites that promised initiates a happy afterlife. In Athens, the mysteries celebrated in the month of Anthesterion were dedicated to her. In Epizephyrian Locris, in southern Italy, she was a goddess of marriage and childbirth. Her epithets include Brimo, 'the terrifying one', Chthonia, 'of the earth', and Nestis, a euphemistic title often rendered as 'the Fasting One'. Her true name was not to be spoken aloud, and she was simply called 'the Maiden'.

Legacy

Modern scholars have read her in many ways. Walter Burkert sees her and Demeter as a single figure in two aspects, the 'Two Goddesses'. Carl Kerényi links the Eleusinian mysteries to the idea that life is indestructible, while Jean-Pierre Vernant connects her with the Gorgon Medusa and with the boundary between light and shadow. The popular notion that she spends winter in the underworld owes much to Roman poets such as Ovid, whose literary reshaping of the myth became the familiar picture.

Ancient sources: Homer's Odyssey, the Orphic Hymns, Apollodorus's Library, Aeschylus, Plato's Cratylus and, among the Latin poets, Ovid.

Trivia

A forbidden name. Her name was considered unsafe to say aloud, so the Greeks referred to her indirectly, as 'the Maiden'.
A goddess of water. In a fragment ascribed to Empedocles, the name Nestis, meaning 'the Fasting One', stands for water and refers to Persephone.
Gateway to the afterlife. Lake Avernus, regarded as an entrance to the infernal regions, was sacred to her.
