Ceryneian Hind

The Ceryneian Hind is a marvellous creature from Greek mythology, with golden antlers and sacred to Artemis. Capturing her was the third of the twelve labours of Heracles.

The myth

According to tradition, the hind was one of a race of enormous deer, larger than a bull, with golden antlers, bronze hooves and a fiery breath. The accounts differ on her origin. In one version, Artemis killed four of the hinds, but the fifth fled across the river Celadon and, at Hera's design, became known as the Ceryneian hind. In another, Artemis yoked four of them to her golden chariot and, at Hera's suggestion, let the fifth escape to Mount Ceryneia.

Eurystheus ordered Heracles to bring her back alive to Mycenae. Because the hind was sacred to the goddess, Heracles would neither kill nor wound her, and he chased her for a whole year, from Oenoe to Mount Artemisium. When the exhausted animal crossed the river Ladon, Heracles shot her with an arrow, took her on his shoulders and carried her through Arcadia. Artemis, accompanied by Apollo, confronted him and reproached him for harming a creature sacred to her. Heracles argued that he was obeying Eurystheus, calmed the goddess, and brought the hind alive to Mycenae.

The stories disagree on how he caught her. Some speak of nets, others of a capture while she slept, and others of a chase that wore her out. In every case the hero succeeds through cunning rather than force.

Worship and religion

The hind was sacred to Artemis, and one version says she bore an inscription stating that Taygete, daughter of Atlas, had dedicated her to the goddess. The sources do not describe shrines or rites devoted specifically to the hind.

Legacy

The hind appears mainly in images of the labour of Heracles: on an Athenian plate of around 560 BC, on Roman bronzes, and on a 3rd-century Roman mosaic from Spain. In modern times, the scholar William Ridgeway proposed that the animal was really a reindeer, the only deer species in which females also grow antlers.

Ancient sources: Pindar mentions her in his odes, Euripides describes Heracles killing her and bringing her to Artemis, and Callimachus includes her in his Hymn to Artemis.

Trivia

Antlered females. In nature it is rare, but some female deer grow antlers when they experience unusually high levels of testosterone. In Greek literature such animals are usually linked to Artemis.
Four ways of catching her. The accounts give several versions of how Heracles caught her: with nets, while she slept, after an exhausting chase, or by shooting her with an arrow.
