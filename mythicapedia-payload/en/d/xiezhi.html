Chinese mythical justice-beast, known in Korea as the haetae

Xiezhi

The xiezhi is a legendary creature from Chinese mythology, a one-horned beast said to tell right from wrong. It became a symbol of justice and law, and its image spread to Japan and Korea, where it took on its own names.

The myth

According to Chinese legend, the minister Gao Yao, serving Emperor Shun, owned a mythical creature called zhi (廌), which he brought to criminal trials whenever he was unsure. The animal instinctively recognised the guilty party and punished them by butting them with its horn, so that the verdict emerged without the judge having to decide alone.

The xiezhi first appears in Chinese literature in the Han dynasty. The scholar Yang Fu described it as an oddly righteous beast: it condemns the wrong side when it sees a fight and bites the wrong side when it hears an argument. The dictionary Shuowen Jiezi calls it a strong, single-horned beast that, in antiquity, settled disputes by charging at the guilty party.

Legacy

The xiezhi was used as a symbol of justice and law. In the Ming and Qing dynasties, the Censorate, which oversaw officials, used it as an office badge. Military police of the Republic of China wear insignia bearing its image, and it is engraved on the gavels in courts of the People's Republic of China.

In Japan it is called kaichi or shin'yō, 'divine sheep', and is described as a lion with a horn on its head. In Korea it is called haetae, and its sculptures decorated Joseon-dynasty architecture, where it was believed to guard against fire. Seoul officially adopted the haechi, the Korean form of the name, as its symbol in 2009. In popular culture it appears in the Korean drama Haechi and in the character Yanfei from the video game Genshin Impact.

Ancient sources: The early Chinese texts that mention it are the Shuowen Jiezi and the writings of the Han-dynasty scholar Yang Fu.

Trivia

A fossil named after it. In 2022 a prehistoric giraffe-like artiodactyl from early Miocene China was named Discokeryx xiezhi, after a single bony plate on its skull that resembled the mythical creature's horn.
A unicorn-lion. In English, the haetae may be called 'the unicorn-lion'.
