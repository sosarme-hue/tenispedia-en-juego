Dragon God of the sea in Japanese mythology

Ryūjin

Ryūjin, whose name means "Dragon God", was the tutelary deity of the sea in Japanese mythology. He embodied the power of the ocean and was also linked to rain, thunder and the livelihood of coastal fishing communities.

The myth

In the myth, Ryūjin lived in Ryūgū-jō, an undersea palace built of red and white coral. From there he controlled the tides with two magical jewels, Kanju and Manju, which could calm or stir the sea. Turtles, fish and jellyfish served him, and he could take human form.

He was the father of the beautiful Otohime, who married Prince Hoori. Tradition holds that their descendant was Emperor Jimmu, Japan's first emperor, which made Ryūjin one of the ancestors of the imperial line.

Another tale explains how jellyfish lost their bones. Ryūjin wanted a monkey's liver and sent the jellyfish to fetch a monkey. The monkey tricked them by claiming it had left its liver in a jar in the forest. When they returned empty-handed, Ryūjin, enraged, beat the jellyfish until their bones were crushed.

In an alternative legend, Empress Jingū invaded Korea with the help of the jewels. She threw Kanju into the sea to lower the tide, stranding the Korean fleet, then threw Manju so the water rose and drowned the soldiers.

Worship and religion

Within Shinto, Ryūjin shinkō, or the "Faith in the Dragon God", venerates dragons as water kami. It is tied to agricultural rites, rain prayers and the success of fishermen. Ryūjin has shrines across Japan, especially in rural and coastal areas, and the cult remains alive today.

Legacy

Ryūjin appears often in Japanese art, such as medieval sculptures of the Dragon King. He is also the patron of several family lines and, in some versions, is credited with a hurricane that sank the Mongol fleet sent by Kublai Khan.

Trivia

The tide jewels. Kanju, the tide-lowering jewel, and Manju, the tide-raising jewel, are the objects with which Ryūjin controls the sea.
A festival with a legend. The Gion Matsuri, held at Yasaka shrine, commemorates the legend of Empress Jingū and the jewels.
