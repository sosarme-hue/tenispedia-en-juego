Roman goddess of equity

Aequitas

Aequitas was the Roman personification of equity, fairness and uprightness, and the root of the English word "equity". She appeared mainly in imperial propaganda and on coins, where she was shown with symbols of honest measure.

The myth

Unlike many Roman deities, Aequitas has no myth of her own preserved in the sources; she is known more as an abstract concept than as a character with a story. Her meaning is best understood through Cicero's reflection on justice. In his Topica, the orator described aequitas as having three parts: the first, pertaining to the gods above, was pietas, religious obligation; the second, concerning the spirits of the dead (the manes), was sanctitas, that which is sacred; and the third, concerning human beings, was iustitia, justice.

This tripartite division shows how the Romans linked justice to religious duty, to the dead and to relations between people. For Cicero, equity was not only a legal rule but also a way of treating each individual fairly.

Worship and religion

Under the Empire, the personification of aequitas became part of the emperor's religious propaganda under the name Aequitas Augusti, tying fairness to the figure of the ruler. Her image on coins made her an official emblem of imperial power. The sources give no details of temples or festivals devoted to her.

Legacy

The English word "equity" comes from the Latin aequitas, and the Spanish "equidad" shares the same root. Her balance scale, a symbol of honest measure for the Romans, appears alongside the cornucopia on her coins.

Ancient sources: Cicero's Topica (90) and Roman imperial coinage.

In art

Trivia

Measure more than justice. On coins, the balance scale was more often a symbol of honest measure to the Romans than of justice.
A virtue serving the emperor. The name Aequitas Augusti tied an abstract virtue to the emperor within imperial propaganda.
