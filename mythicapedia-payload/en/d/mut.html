mother goddess of ancient Egypt

Mut

Mut was a mother goddess of ancient Egypt whose name simply means "mother". As the wife of Amun in Thebes, she formed the Theban Triad with him and Khonsu, and her cult reached its height during the New Kingdom.

The myth

In Theban creation myths, Mut emerged from the primordial waters of Nu alongside her father, the dt-serpent, a form of Amun-Ra. She was depicted as a serpent who embodied the uraeus of the first god, her eyes lighting the world, and as the stem of the primordial lotus. Some texts call her the mother of all creation, and others describe her as both daughter and mother, the one who gave birth to her own father.

In later tradition she is the wife of Amun and the mother of Khonsu, the lunar god. Since she had no children of her own, according to the versions preserved in the reference texts she first adopted Montu and then Khonsu. Her role within the triad shifts from one account to another: she can be wife, mother or daughter, reflecting the idea of a creator god who begets himself.

In the myth of the return of the Eye of Ra, preserved on a Demotic papyrus in Leiden, a wandering goddess first named Tefnut leaves Egypt in the form of a wild cat. The god Thoth persuades her to return with a fable and by reminding her of her homeland and her twin brother Shu. On reaching Thebes she is attacked by followers of Apophis and flees with Thoth, hiding among the reeds. In the end she regains her divine form and is reconciled with her father.

Worship and religion

Her cult grew strong from the Eighteenth Dynasty onward, when she replaced the goddess Amunet as Amun's consort in Thebes. She had sanctuaries at Karnak, Tanis, Sais and the oases of Kharga and Dakhla, and in Nubia her main centre was the Temple of Mut at Gebel Barkal. Festivals such as the Opet Festival and the Beautiful Festival of the Valley included singing, dancing and music. Her temple had a "Hall of Drunkenness", linked to the myth of the destruction of mankind, where drinks were taken and sounds of the underworld were imitated. Private individuals also invoked her: a follower of Ramesses II donated all his property to her temple.

Legacy

Her name appears in well-known personal names, such as Meritenmut, "beloved of Mut", borne by Nefertari, wife of Ramesses II, and Senenmut, architect of Hatshepsut. Her iconography, with the double crown, the vulture and the papyrus sceptre, is still one of the most recognisable in the Egyptian repertoire.

Ancient sources: She is invoked in the Book of the Dead to prevent the deceased from decaying, and she features in the Demotic Papyrus of Leiden, which records the myth of the return of the Eye of Ra.

Trivia

The Hall of Drunkenness. Mut's temple had a hall with this name, confirmed by inscriptions from the time of Hatshepsut. There the goddess was linked to rituals of drinking and dancing.
A name in personal names. Her name appears in the names of members of the elite, such as Nefertari and the architect Senenmut, showing how popular she was.
