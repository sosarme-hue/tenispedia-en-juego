Phoenix

The phoenix is a legendary bird that dies and is born again from its own ashes. Originating in Greek mythology, it has parallels in Egypt and Persia, and its image of death and renewal remains one of the most recognisable symbols in Western culture.

The myth

According to the Greek accounts, the phoenix is a bird of very long life, which in most traditions lasts about five hundred years, and it is associated with the Sun. When its time to die comes, it builds a nest of branches and aromatic herbs. Some versions say it burns in a blaze of flames; others say it decomposes before being born again. From its ashes a new bird emerges, identical to its predecessor, and the cycle begins anew.

Ancient authors do not agree on the details. Hesiod mentions it in a fragment attributed to the Precepts of Chiron, where the bird is given a lifespan of roughly nine hundred seventy-two times that of a human. Herodotus, writing in the 5th century BC, describes it flying to Egypt, to Heliopolis, carrying the body of its father and placing it in the temple of the Sun. Pliny the Elder records several supposed appearances, including one under Emperor Claudius when a specimen allegedly captured in Egypt was brought to Rome, although Pliny himself admits that nobody believed it was genuine.

Modern scholars debate the origin of the myth. Many connect it with the Egyptian Bennu, a solar bird associated with Heliopolis and the flooding of the Nile, although the Egyptian sources are hard to interpret and some may have been shaped by Greek ideas. In Christian tradition the phoenix came to stand for resurrection. A medieval legend places it in the Garden of Eden, nesting in a rose bush, and says that a spark from the angel's sword that expelled Adam and Eve set its nest alight. Having been the only creature that refused the forbidden fruit, it was rewarded with immortality.

Worship and religion

Greek sources do not describe an organised cult of the phoenix. Its clearest ritual link is Egyptian: the Bennu was venerated at Heliopolis, the city of the Sun, and its figure was linked with the sun's daily rebirth. Today there is no living cult of the phoenix; it survives mainly as a symbol.

Legacy

The phoenix entered European heraldry and became one of the emblems of Queen Elizabeth I of England, who was associated with the idea of a figure beyond the distinctions of gender. Dante mentions it in Canto XXIV of the Inferno, and Shakespeare and John Fletcher cite it in their play Henry VIII. Thomas Carlyle used it in Sartor Resartus as a metaphor for the cycles of history. An Old English poem in the Exeter Book reads it as an allegory of Christ's resurrection. The constellation Phoenix has borne its name since the 17th century, and in English the word also describes an exceptional person.

Ancient sources: Hesiod (attributed, in the Precepts of Chiron), Herodotus, Ovid, Lucan, Pliny the Elder, Tacitus, Claudian, Lactantius and Isidore of Seville.

Trivia

A phoenix in Rome. Pliny says a specimen supposedly captured in Egypt was exhibited in the Comitium in Rome, but he himself considered it a fabrication.
A royal emblem. Queen Elizabeth I of England used the phoenix as one of her emblems, linked to her status as a sovereign who never married.
A Mycenaean word. The oldest Greek form appears on Mycenaean tablets as po-ni-ke, which probably meant griffin, though it may have meant palm tree.
