Japanese kami of poverty

Binbōgami

Binbōgami is a kami of Japanese folklore said to inhabit a person or their house and bring poverty and misery. It is one of several Japanese spirits of poverty, and its figure appears in classic tales, essays and rakugo performances.

The myth

According to tradition, the binbōgami usually appears as a skinny, dirty old man with a pale face and a sad expression, often holding a paper fan. Despite his looks, he is said to be fond of lazy people, and when he lives in a house he prefers to hide in the closets. Some accounts add that he wields a kendama or wears only one broken geta. As a kami he cannot be killed, though there are ways to drive him away.

The personification of poverty already appears in the thirteenth-century Shasekishū, where a monk named Enjōbō and his disciples chase Hinkyū-den, Lord Poverty, out of a house by striking him with peach branches while chanting incantations, and then shut the gate behind him. The term binbōgami itself is documented from at least the Muromachi period.

Worship and religion

In some regions, a fire was lit in the irori hearth on New Year's Eve to drive him away with its heat, since warmth was thought to attract the Fukugami, gods of good fortune. The Senba ritual in Osaka used the smell of baked miso to lure the binbōgami onto a folded miso plate, which was then thrown into a river. Today the Ōta Shrine in Bunkyō, Tokyo, venerates him as a kami able to turn poverty into fortune.

Legacy

The binbōgami appears in classic Japanese literature, rakugo and essays. In modern pop culture he is a character in Hudson Soft's Momotaro Dentetsu game series, and statues based on him stand at train stations in Kagawa, Nagasaki and Chiba. The proverb about a persimmon-wood fan attracting the binbōgami also remains part of everyday language.

Ancient sources: The thirteenth-century Shasekishū tells of the expulsion of Hinkyū-den, while Tsumura Soan's Tankai, Ihara Saikaku's Nippon Eidaigura and the Toen Shōsetsu record Edo-period versions.

Trivia

Statue with a monkey. A temple in Tokyo has a binbōgami statue with a monkey riding its head, a pun since saru means both to leave and monkey.
Fire and poverty. In Niigata it was believed that lighting a fire in the irori on New Year's Eve drove the binbōgami away with its heat.
