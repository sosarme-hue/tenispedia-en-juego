Mesopotamian god of evil

Hanbi

Hanbi (also Hanbu or Hanpa) is a god of evil from Sumerian, Akkadian and wider Mesopotamian tradition, lord of the malevolent spirits and father of Pazuzu. He matters chiefly because he may have shaped later images of evil in the ancient Near East and in the religions that followed.

The myth

Very little is recorded about Hanbi. In Mesopotamian tradition he ruled over all the evil forces, known as udug, and his son Pazuzu is the best-attested member of this demonic family.

Some scholars regard the god An (Anu) as his creator, although the surviving texts say little about this. His most distinctive trait is his origin: he is described as a shadow born in the underworld, unlike the Sumerian gods of the dead (dingir), which brings him closer to the Jewish shedim and se'iri or the Islamic shaitan.

Legacy

The Neo-Assyrian Pazuzu statuette in the Louvre, whose inscription calls the demon the son of Hanpa, is the main evidence for his name. He has been proposed as a possible inspiration for Apophis in Egyptian mythology, Angra Mainyu in Zoroastrianism and another form of Satan in the Abrahamic faiths. Popular Christian belief even makes him a brother of Archangel Michael, and some have linked him with Helel, the Hebrew name associated with Lucifer.

Ancient sources: Few literary texts name him; the main reference is a Neo-Assyrian inscription on the back of a Pazuzu statuette.

Trivia

An inscription on a statuette. His name appears on the back of a Neo-Assyrian statuette of Pazuzu, now in the Louvre, where the demon presents himself as the son of Hanpa.
An angelic brother. Popular Christian belief considers him a brother of Archangel Michael, a link that does not come from Mesopotamian texts.
