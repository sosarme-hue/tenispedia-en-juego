Sumerian creation myth of Enlil's hoe

Song of the Hoe

The Song of the Hoe is a Sumerian creation myth preserved on clay tablets from the late third millennium BCE, in which the god Enlil creates daylight, humankind and civilization with a golden hoe bladed in lapis lazuli. It matters because it shows how the Sumerians gave an agricultural tool a cosmic role in the origin of life and cities.

The myth

According to the story, the world begins when heaven and earth are separated at Duranki, the point of the world-axis. Enlil, god of the earth, creates daylight with his hoe, a tool of gold with a lapis lazuli blade fastened with cord, and then shapes human beings from brick moulds. The Anunnaki praise him, and other deities such as Nisaba, Ninmena and Enki organize work and make numerous shining hoes so that everyone can labour. The song then describes construction in several cities, including Enlil's Ekur and the Abzu at Eridu, and recalls that the hoe serves for building canals, farming and burying the dead.

Ancient sources: The Sumerian clay tablets of the late third millennium BCE, now scattered across several museums, are the main sources; the Lament for Ur also mentions the hoe as a tool of destruction.

Trivia

Play on sounds. The word al, meaning hoe, is repeated dozens of times in the poem, and many verb forms start with or contain that syllable.
Echo in Gilgamesh. The poem alludes to scenes from the Epic of Gilgamesh, such as Enkidu's ghost and Urshanabi's ferry over the Hubur.
