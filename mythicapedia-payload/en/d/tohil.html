Maya god of fire and patron of the K'iche'

Tohil

Tohil is a Maya deity of the Late Postclassic period, the god of fire and patron of the Kaweq lineage of the K'iche'. He matters because he links fire, war and human sacrifice in the religion of the Guatemalan highlands.

The myth

In the Popol Vuh, after the first people were created, they gathered at the mythical Tollan or Tula, the Place of the Seven Caves, to receive their language and gods. Tohil demanded blood sacrifice, so the K'iche' offered their own blood and that of captives taken in battle. The text compares his drinking of blood to a mother nursing her infant.

Tohil was linked to the feathered serpent of the Aztecs, Quetzalcoatl, and was compared to Qʼuqʼumatz, with whom he shared serpent traits before the two developed separate priesthoods. He was associated with thunder, lightning, rain, mountains and sunrise.

Worship and religion

The K'iche' built his main temple, a radial pyramid, at their capital Qʼumarkaj, and offered him sacrifices and quetzal feathers. His priests, the aj tohil, were chosen from the Kaweq lineage. Each year the K'iche' performed the Great Dance of Tohil before the maize harvest. Today deerskins, his symbol, are still venerated and used in dances in highland Maya communities.

Legacy

His name is associated with the meaning "tribute" or "payment", and in the colonial period his figure merged with Saint Martin of Tours in Santiago Atitlán and with Saint Paul in Rabinal.

Ancient sources: The Popol Vuh, the K'iche' chronicle, describes his arrival with the first peoples and his central role among the K'iche'.

Trivia

People of the fire thieves. The Kaqchikel refused the human sacrifices Tohil demanded in exchange for fire, so they stole it. Their name means "fire thieves".
Dance with deerskins. In Santiago Atitlán, deerskins linked to Tohil are blessed and worn in the Dance of Martin, held on November 11.
