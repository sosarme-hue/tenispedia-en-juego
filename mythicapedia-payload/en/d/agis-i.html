Agis I

Agis I was a legendary king of Sparta and the eponym of the Agiad dynasty. Tradition credits him with the conquest of Laconia and the origin of the helots, the servile class of classical Sparta, so his figure is more mythical than historical.

The myth

According to tradition, Agis was the son of Eurysthenes, the first king of the Agiad dynasty. Herodotus traced his line back to Heracles through Aristodemus, Aristomachus, Cleodaeus and Hyllus. Ancient authors credit him with conquering Laconia and subjecting its people. He is said to have taken Amyclae, south of Sparta, and to have driven out several non-Dorian groups. When the inhabitants of Helos resisted losing their privileges, he subjected them and reduced them to servitude. In this account, the helots, the famous serfs of classical Sparta, came into being.

The chronology is another matter. Eusebius of Caesarea, drawing on Diodorus, gives him a reign of just one year, but modern scholars propose correcting the figure to thirty-one years, given the many deeds attributed to him. Pausanias, by contrast, places the conquest of Laconia under the much later kings Archelaus and Charilaus. Herodotus also makes Agis the father of Lycurgus, the legendary lawgiver, which probably reflects an Agiad attempt to claim the fame of a lawgiver that other traditions linked to the rival dynasty.

Ancient sources: Herodotus, Pindar, Ephorus, Plato and Isocrates record the conquest of Laconia attributed to Agis, Pausanias offers a different version, and Eusebius of Caesarea preserves the king list with his brief reign.

Trivia

A very short reign. Eusebius of Caesarea assigns him a single year on the throne, a figure modern scholars consider hard to reconcile with the deeds attributed to him.
Origin of the helots. According to tradition, the people of Helos subjected by Agis gave rise to the class of helots.
Father of Lycurgus. Herodotus presents him as the father of the lawgiver Lycurgus, a genealogy that other sources do not share.
