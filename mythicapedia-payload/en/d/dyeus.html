Proto-Indo-European daylight-sky god

Dyēus

Dyēus is the reconstructed name of the daylight-sky god of the Proto-Indo-European peoples, a divinity conceived as the personification of the bright day. Although his existence is not directly attested, scholars regard him as the most securely reconstructed deity of that pantheon, since his heirs appear in Zeus, Jupiter, Tyr, Dievas and Diaus Pitar.

The myth

According to the reconstruction, Dyēus was the luminous sky and the dwelling of the gods, and from him descended the Divine Twins and the goddess of the Dawn. He was imagined united with the Earth Mother, Dʰéǵʰōm, in a relationship of union and contrast: the rain that falls from the sky makes the earth fertile. Rather than a supreme ruler, he seems to have been a prominent god whose wide vision made him a witness to oaths and treaties. Some propose a consort, Diwōnā, based on Dione and Indrānī, but the hypothesis is uncertain.

Over time, according to Mircea Eliade, new gods of thunder and storm took over the role of fertilizers, so Dyēus became a distant ancestor of figures such as Varuna or Thor and was pushed out of worship, becoming a deus ōtiōsus, an idle god.

Worship and religion

No temples or rituals specific to Dyēus are known. In later traditions his cult weakened, while his heirs, such as Zeus or Jupiter, had active worship in Greece and Rome. Today no living cult of this deity exists.

Legacy

His name survives in the root that gave Spanish dios and día, in Greek Zeus, Latin Jupiter, Germanic Tyr and Baltic Dievas. His epithet "Father Sky" was preserved in Vedic Dyáuṣ Pitṛ́ and Greek Zeus Patēr.

Ancient sources: The Rig-veda names Dyáuṣ Pitṛ́ as the father of the sun god, while Euripides' Medea and Beowulf describe sunlight in images that recall this god.

Trivia

God and day, relatives. The Spanish words dios and día both descend from the root that also gave Dyēus, linked to brightness and the sky.
The eye of God. Proto-Indo-Europeans saw the sun as the lamp or eye of Dyēus. In Romanian folklore, the sun is still called the "eye of God".
