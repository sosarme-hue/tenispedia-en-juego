gorgon of Greek mythology

Medusa

Medusa is one of the three Gorgons of Greek mythology, a female monster with snakes for hair whose gaze turned onlookers to stone. She matters because her severed head, taken by the hero Perseus, became a powerful protective emblem, the Gorgoneion, and one of the most recognisable images of antiquity. According to Hesiod and Apollodorus, she was a daughter of the sea god Phorcys and his sister Ceto, and sister of Euryale and Stheno. Unlike her sisters, who were immortal, Medusa was the only mortal of the three. Hesiod is the first to name her, in the Theogony, while Aeschylus places the Gorgons far away, on the dreadful plain of Cisthene. The tradition tells that King Polydectes of Seriphus wanted to marry Perseus's mother and sent the young man to bring back Medusa's head. Perseus received divine help: a polished shield from Athena, winged sandals from Hermes, a sword and Hades' helm of invisibility. Using the shield as a mirror, he avoided looking at her directly and beheaded her as she slept. From her neck sprang Pegasus, the winged horse, and Chrysaor, a giant with a golden sword, children of Poseidon, with whom Medusa had lain. Perseus gave the head to Athena, who set it on her aegis. Accounts differ. According to Ovid, Medusa was originally a beautiful maiden and priestess of Athena, violated by Poseidon in the goddess's temple, and her punishment was having her hair turned into snakes. Euripides makes her born from the Earth to aid the giants in their war against the gods, and Pindar already calls her "fair-cheeked Medusa". Pausanias and Tzetzes offer Libyan and Pisidian origins. Ovid also tells that Perseus turned the Titan Atlas to stone, and that the corals of the Red Sea and the vipers of the Sahara arose from drops of Medusa's blood.

The myth

Worship and religion

The Gorgoneion, the mask of Medusa's head, appears from archaic Greece onward on pottery, reliefs and temples as an amulet to ward off evil. Some scholars, such as Jane Ellen Harrison, see it as an ancient ritual object, a cult mask to which a body was later added. Robert Graves links the myth to the suppression of cults in which priestesses wore gorgon masks.

Legacy

Medusa has been a constant subject in art, from Greek vases to Roman mosaics and cameos. In literature, Ovid recast her as a beautiful victim, and Freud analysed her in his essay on Medusa's head. Twentieth-century feminists reinterpreted her, and the fashion house Versace used her head as its logo.

Ancient sources: Hesiod (Theogony), Aeschylus (Prometheus Bound), Pindar, Euripides (Ion) and Ovid (Metamorphoses).

Trivia

Red Sea corals. According to Ovid, the corals of the Red Sea formed from Medusa's blood that fell onto seaweed while Perseus rested her head on the shore.
Medusa and Atlas. Ovid tells that Perseus turned the Titan Atlas, who held up the sky, to stone when Atlas tried to attack him.
