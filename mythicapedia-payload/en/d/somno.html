Roman god of sleep

Somnus

Somnus is the personification of sleep in Roman mythology, the counterpart of the Greek Hypnos. He appears in poems by Virgil and Ovid, who place him in the underworld and surround him with the dream figures that visit mortals.

The myth

In Roman tradition, Somnus lived in the underworld. Virgil, following the Greek model, makes him the brother of Death (Mors) and places their dwellings near the entrance to Hell, among other figures of suffering such as old age, disease and hunger.

In the Aeneid, Somnus tricks Palinurus, the helmsman of Aeneas's ship, who refuses to hand over the steering. Taking the shape of the shipmate Phorbas, he sprinkles the helmsman with water from the Lethe, the river of forgetfulness, and pushes him overboard. In Ovid's Metamorphoses, Somnus lives in a doorless cave near the land of the Cimmerians, where the sun never enters and silence reigns. There he has countless sons, the Somnia, who take many forms: Morpheus imitates human beings, Icelos or Phobetor takes the shape of animals, and Phantasos imitates lifeless things.

In Book 11 of the Metamorphoses, Juno sends Iris to his cave so that he will send a dream to Alcyone in the form of her husband Ceyx, who has died in a storm. Somnus wakes Morpheus to carry out the task and then goes back to sleep.

Ancient sources: Virgil in the Aeneid and Ovid in the Metamorphoses, which build on the Greek figure of Hypnos from Hesiod and Homer.
