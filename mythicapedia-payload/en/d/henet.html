pelican goddess of ancient Egypt

Henet

Henet, sometimes spelled Henut, is an Egyptian goddess associated with the pelican, venerated since the Old Kingdom. She mattered as a protector, especially against snakes, and as a guide in funerary belief.

The myth

The Pyramid Texts call her “mother of the king,” a title scholars note was used exclusively for goddesses, and present her as a protective symbol against snakes. The pelican was said to catch fish, scooping dangerous elements out of the water, an image linked to the nets used in tombs to trap the dead's enemies.

In later funerary papyri, Henet speaks with the guardians of the Underworld and leads the deceased along heavenly paths. Her open beak stood for the dead person leaving the burial chamber toward the sun's rays, and she was linked with the goddess Nut and the sun's rising and setting. Some texts also say she could foretell a safe journey for those crossing the Underworld.

Worship and religion

Sources do not describe temples or festivals devoted to Henet. Her veneration is documented from the Old Kingdom, and she appears in tomb and temple scenes.

Ancient sources: The Pyramid Texts call Henet “mother of the king,” and later funerary papyri describe her guiding the dead through the Underworld.

Trivia

A title reserved for goddesses. In the Pyramid Texts, “mother of the king” was a title that, according to scholars, was used exclusively for goddesses.
A beak like a tomb shaft. The pelican's long, open beak has been compared to the shaft leading to the burial chamber, a possible analogy with the dead person's exit toward the light.
