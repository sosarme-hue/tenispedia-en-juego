sacred cobra of ancient Egypt, symbol of kingship

Uraeus

The uraeus is the upright cobra that adorned the brow of Egyptian pharaohs as an emblem of sovereignty and divine protection. It represented the goddess Wadjet, protector of Lower Egypt, and only the pharaoh was entitled to wear it as a sign of royal authority.

The myth

Wadjet was a powerful serpent from the Nile Delta who protected gods and pharaohs and embodied the solar goddesses. When Egypt was unified, her image was joined with that of Nekhbet, the vulture of Upper Egypt, and the two became known as the Nebty, the Two Ladies, joint protectors of the united land.

The uraeus could appear as a cobra rising above the brow or as a band encircling the head, and it was believed to spit fire at the pharaoh's enemies from its fiery eye. Since the king was regarded as a manifestation of the sun god Ra, the cobra defended him in battle and in the dangers of the afterlife. The serpent also symbolised rebirth and was linked to the sun's journey across the sky and through the Duat, the underworld.

Worship and religion

Her main cult centre was Per-Wadjet, which the Greeks called Buto. At death, serpents were placed in boxes of bronze or wood carved with snake reliefs, sometimes with human heads. The uraeus also crowned the upper parts of many temples.

Legacy

The uraeus remains present in Egyptology and in hieroglyphs, where the cobra functions as a sign. Sources also connect it with the seraphim of Hebrew, Christian and Islamic traditions, which are often associated with serpents.

Ancient sources: Horapollo mentions it in his treatise Hieroglyphica, where he interprets hieroglyphic writing.

In art

Trivia

Gold uraeus of Senusret II. In 1919 a solid gold uraeus of pharaoh Senusret II was found at Saqqara, with granite eyes and a lapis lazuli head. Before Tutankhamun's tomb was discovered, it was the only such ornament known from an entombed pharaoh.
