sacred bull of Heliopolis, embodiment of Ra

Mnevis

Mnevis is the Greek name of a sacred bull of ancient Egypt, regarded as the earthly embodiment of the sun god Ra and tied to the solar cult of Heliopolis. According to Plutarch, it ranked second in religious importance only to the Apis bull of Memphis.

The myth

According to Egyptian tradition, Mnevis was a manifestation of Ra, although it seems to have begun as a separate divinity that was gradually absorbed into the sun god. Its priests even claimed that it was the father of the more famous Apis, which shows how closely the two cults were linked.

Bulls were chosen under very strict criteria, and the ideal was an entirely black animal, like those of the Heliopolis region. If no such bull could be found, a completely white one was selected. Its movements were understood as an expression of divine will and served as an oracle. When it died, it was embalmed and buried with honours in a necropolis near the temple. A successor was then sought, and there was only ever one living Mnevis at a time.

Worship and religion

The bull lived in a special temple and had a sacred herd at its disposal. It was depicted as a black bull, with a solar disk and uraeus between its horns. The mothers of Mnevis, cows associated with the goddess Hesat, had a burial ground of their own. The cult was tolerated during Akhenaten's Amarna heresy, probably because of its solar attributes. It does not survive today.

Ancient sources: Plutarch places it second in importance after the Memphite Apis; Claudius Aelianus links it to the sun, and Akhenaten's stelae mention a tomb built for the bull.

Trivia

Two companions. The Mnevis bull was entitled to two cows as companions, representing Hathor and Iusaaset.
Only one at a time. According to the sources, there was never more than one living Mnevis bull at any given time, as each death led to a replacement.
