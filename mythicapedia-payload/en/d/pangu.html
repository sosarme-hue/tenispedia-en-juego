primordial giant and creator in Chinese myth

Pangu

Pangu is the primordial giant and creator figure of Chinese mythology, also revered in Taoism. According to the legend, he separated heaven and earth, and his body later became the features of the world.

The myth

In the beginning there was nothing but a formless, dark chaos. Over about 18,000 years this chaos coalesced into a cosmic egg, inside which the opposing principles of yin and yang came into balance, and from it Pangu emerged, a hairy giant with horns who is often shown holding an axe. With it he split the clear, which rose as the sky, from the murky, which sank as the earth. For another 18,000 years the sky rose and the earth fell each day, while Pangu grew at the same rate, until sky and earth stood at their extremes. In some versions four benevolent beasts assist him: the spirit tortoise, the qilin, the phoenix and the dragon Yinglong.

When Pangu died, his body became the world: his breath turned into wind, his voice into thunder, his left eye into the sun and his right into the moon, his body into mountains, his blood into rivers, his muscles into fertile land, his facial hair into stars and his bones into valuable minerals. His sweat fell as rain, and the parasites on his body, carried by the wind, became human beings. Other versions speak of offspring: in one, his union with the Jade Maiden produced the celestial and earthly rulers, while in another he had no children and his body transformed into all life.

Worship and religion

Pangu is revered in Taoism, and surviving depictions show him with symbolic animals such as the tortoise, the crane, the dragon and the phoenix, emblems of longevity, power and bliss.

Legacy

Scholars of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries compared him with the giant Ymir of Norse myth and with Tiamat of Babylon, and his fire-related counterpart Sui-Jen led some to compare him with Prometheus. These readings are modern interpretations rather than statements in the ancient texts.

Ancient sources: The earliest texts recording his creation are late: the Record of the Three Sovereigns and Five Emperors, attributed to Xu Zheng in the Three Kingdoms period.

Trivia

Roots in southern China. Derk Bodde linked the myth to the ancestral traditions of the Miao and Yao peoples of southern China. A flood story in which a couple survives inside a gourd appears among the Zhuang, whose names for the pair mean whetstone and gourd.
A hammer and chisel. The missionary James Legge described Taoist picture books in which Pangu appears as a shaggy, sturdy figure breaking the rocks of chaos with a huge hammer and chisel, a different image from the axe of other depictions.
