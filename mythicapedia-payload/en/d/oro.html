Polynesian god of war and the arts

Oro

Oro is the god of war on Tahiti and the Society Islands and, in peacetime, of the fine arts. His cult was one of the major religions of those islands in the 17th and 18th centuries.

The myth

According to tradition, Oro lived with his sisters Teouri and Oaaoa on Mount Pahia, on Bora Bora. After fruitless searches across the islands, he came down to earth on a rainbow, disguised as a warrior. His sisters found Vairaumati bathing in a pool, and Oro resolved to marry her; each morning he came down to visit her and each evening he returned to Pahia. They had a son destined to become a powerful chief, and Oro eventually made Vairaumati a goddess.

On Raiatea, by contrast, he is considered the son of Taaroa and Hina tu a uta.

Worship and religion

On Tahiti he was the god of war, and pigs as well as human victims were offered to him; the captain James Cook witnessed a human sacrifice in 1777. His effigy, a bundle wrapped in coconut fiber with red and yellow feathers, was kept on a ceremonial platform and re-clothed in tapa cloth during a ritual. The secret society of the Arioi was closely tied to his cult and claimed descent from him. The marae Taputapuatea on Raiatea was dedicated to him.

Trivia

Cook and a human sacrifice. In 1777 the captain James Cook witnessed a human sacrifice made to Oro. The victim, held on a platform, was struck on the skull with a sacred mace.
Mahui in the Marquesas. In the Marquesas Islands, Oro was known as Mahui, showing how the god took different names from island to island.
