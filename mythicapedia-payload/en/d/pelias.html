king of Iolcus, uncle of Jason and antagonist of the Argonauts

Pelias

Pelias was king of Iolcus in Greek mythology, son of Poseidon and Tyro. He is best remembered for sending Jason on the quest for the Golden Fleece, the story that gave rise to the tale of the Argonauts.

The myth

According to tradition, Tyro, wife of Cretheus, bore twin sons, Pelias and Neleus, to Poseidon, who had approached her disguised as the river god Enipeus. Tyro abandoned the boys at birth; Pelias supposedly got his name from the dark bruise left on him after a mare kicked him. As an adult, he killed Sidero, the stepmother who had mistreated Tyro, even at Hera's altar, which earned him the goddess's lasting enmity.

Accounts differ on the succession. In the oldest version, Zeus gave the twins separate kingdoms after they quarrelled, and Pelias stayed on to hold the sceptre of Iolcus. Others say he simply succeeded Cretheus. Hungry for power, Pelias exiled Neleus and imprisoned Aeson, the eldest son. Aeson sent his son Jason to be educated by the centaur Chiron. An oracle warned Pelias to beware the man who arrived wearing only one sandal.

Years later, Jason appeared in Iolcus with one sandal, having lost the other in a river, and Pelias, not recognising the danger, agreed to hand over the throne if he brought back the Golden Fleece guarded in Colchis. After the Argonauts' expedition, Pelias would not give up power. Medea persuaded his daughters, the Peliades, that he could be rejuvenated as an old ram had been; they cut him into pieces, but he did not return to youth and died. In another version, Acastus buried his father and drove Jason and Medea out.

Legacy

Pelias's name is linked to Mount Pelion in Thessaly, where Jason was raised by Chiron, and the term Peliades is used for his daughters. His story appears in the funeral games tradition, where Atalanta defeated Peleus in a wrestling match, a scene that became a popular subject in Greek art.

Ancient sources: Pindar, Apollodorus and Hyginus record versions of the story of Pelias and the Golden Fleece; Virgil's Aeneid includes the episode of the lost sandal.
