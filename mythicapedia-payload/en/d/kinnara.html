hybrid creature of Hindu and Buddhist mythology

Kinnara

Kinnaras are half-human, half-bird beings linked to music, song and love, said to dwell in the Himalayas and to watch over people in times of trouble. They appear in both Hindu and Buddhist traditions, and their image spread across South and Southeast Asia.

The myth

According to tradition, kinnaras are hybrid beings who live in the Himalayas, in the mythical Himavanta. Hindu texts picture them in different ways: some describe them as centaur-like, half man and half horse, while Buddhist understanding presents them as half human and half bird.

In the Jataka tales, kinnaras are gentle, harmless creatures who live in pairs bound by mutual love and devotion. In one story, a female kinnara's loyalty to her wounded husband brings Indra to heal him. They are described as music lovers: she beats a drum while he plays the lute. Yet they are also hunted, caged and presented to kings for entertainment. They feed on flower pollen and wear flowers, and one Jataka tells how they nursed a human baby whose parents had gone off into the forest.

The female kinnari is the form most often shown in the literature and art of Southeast Asia, where she has the head and torso of a woman with the wings, tail and feet of a swan.

Worship and religion

In early Indian Buddhist art, kinnaras appear frequently in reliefs at Sanchi, Barhut, Amaravati and Mathura, holding garlands or trays of flowers in homage to stupas. In Indonesia they guard the Kalpataru, the tree of life, at temples such as Borobudur and Prambanan. In Cambodia and Thailand they feature in classical dance and performance. No separate cult of the creature is described.

Legacy

The kinnari is a traditional symbol of feminine beauty, grace and artistic accomplishment in Southeast Asia. In Myanmar, a kinnari figure appears on the Myanmar Academy Awards statue, and the kinnara and kinnari couple is the symbol of the Karenni people. In Cambodia, the kenorei archetype appears in the Royal Ballet repertoire and the dance Robam Kenorei. In Thailand, the story of Manora, derived from Manohara, survives in the Pannas Jataka, and golden kinnaree figures adorn Wat Phra Kaew in Bangkok. The ancient Indian kinnari veena string instrument also carries its name.

Ancient sources: They appear in the Adi Parva of the Mahabharata and in the Buddhist Jataka tales, as well as in the Lotus Sutra.

Trivia

Confusion in art. According to Edward H. Schafer, in East Asian religious art the kinnara is often confused with the kalaviṅka, another half-human, half-bird hybrid, although the two are distinct and unrelated.
A symbol in Burma. In Burma, the kinnari appears on the statuette of the Myanmar Academy Awards, given to award winners.
