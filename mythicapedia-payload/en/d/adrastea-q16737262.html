Greek goddess of inescapable fate and divine retribution

Adrasteia

Adrasteia is a deity of Phrygian origin, linked to Cybele and later identified with Nemesis, the goddess of divine retribution. In Greek myth she also appears as a Cretan nymph who helped raise the infant Zeus, which connects an Anatolian mountain cult with the Cretan tradition of the god's birth.

The myth

According to tradition, Rhea entrusted the newborn Zeus to the Curetes and to the nymphs Adrasteia and Ida, daughters of Melisseus, so that they could raise him in secret and protect him from Cronus. The nymphs fed him on the milk of the goat Amalthea, and Adrasteia gave him a marvellous ball to play with; later Aphrodite used it to bribe her son Eros.

Ancient authors did not agree on her parentage. In Euripides' Rhesus she is called a daughter of Zeus. The earliest texts that mention her link her with Cybele, the mother goddess of Anatolia.

Worship and religion

Adrasteia was the goddess of inevitable fate and pressing necessity, embodying the impossibility of escaping punishment. She was worshipped at Cyzicus, where a temple stood nearby, and on Mount Ida in Phrygia. In Athens she received public worship from at least 429 BC, and her name appears in the accounts of the Treasurers of the Other Gods alongside the Thracian goddess Bendis. She was also worshipped together with Nemesis on Kos.

Legacy

Her name, which can be understood as 'the inescapable', gave rise to ancient etymological interpretations. As an epithet of Nemesis, her figure came to embody the idea of unavoidable punishment that Greek tradition associated with that goddess.

Ancient sources: Euripides mentions her in Rhesus, and Aeschylus places her cult in the land of the Berecyntians; she also appears in a fragment of the lost epic Phoronis.

Trivia

A divine ball. According to myth, Adrasteia gave the infant Zeus a marvellous ball to play with. Later, Aphrodite used it to bribe Eros.
A name with a meaning. Her name can be understood as 'the inescapable', and some ancient authors linked it to King Adrastus, who died in the war of the Seven against Thebes.
