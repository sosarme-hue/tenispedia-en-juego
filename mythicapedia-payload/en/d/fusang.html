mythical tree and eastern land in Chinese mythology

Fusang

Fusang is a fabled place in the east of Chinese mythology, linked to a sacred tree from which the sun rises each morning. Over time the name was also applied to real lands across the sea, most often Japan.

The myth

In Chinese tradition, Fusang held a divine tree, sometimes identified as a mulberry or a hibiscus, from which the sun rose. In the west stood the tree Ruomu, where the sun set. According to legend, ten birds, usually ravens, lived in the Fusang tree. Nine rested while the tenth carried the sun on its journey. One day all ten suns rose at once, and the archer Houyi saved the world by shooting down nine of them. Some scholars have linked these trees to the bronze trees found at the Sanxingdui site.

Fusang also appears in historical accounts. According to the Book of Liang, the Buddhist missionary Huishen described in 499 AD a land 20,000 li east of China, to which he said he had sailed. Another tradition tells that around 219 BC the emperor Shi Huang sent some 3,000 convicts to Fusang to make sacrifices to a volcano god who held the elixir of life.

Legacy

In Chinese poetry, the name Fusang came to designate Japan, and in Japanese it is pronounced Fusō, one of the ancient names of the country. The name was given to several warships of the Imperial Japanese Navy, such as the battleship Fusō, and to companies such as Mitsubishi Fuso Truck and Bus Corporation.

Ancient sources: The Shanhaijing (Classic of Mountains and Seas) mentions it as an eastern tree of life, and the Shiji of Sima Qian records the expeditions of Xu Fu sent under Shi Huang.

Trivia

A map with a name. A map attributed to Marco Polo's family mentions Fusang. Dated to the 15th or 16th century, it is at best a copy of a lost original.
The first Japan by that name. A Korean epitaph from 678 is, according to historians cited in the sources, the earliest text that calls Japan Fusang.
