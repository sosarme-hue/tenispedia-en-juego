Greek goddess of persuasion

Peitho

Peitho is the personification of persuasion, seduction and winning speech in Greek mythology. A constant companion of Aphrodite, she moved between being a goddess and an abstract power; the Romans knew her as Suadela.

The myth

Peitho appears mainly beside Aphrodite, whom she attended as a companion and, at times, as a herald. Some traditions make her a nurse of the young Erotes. Her parentage varies by author. Hesiod, in the Theogony, counts her among the Oceanids, daughters of Oceanus and Tethys. Sappho and Aeschylus make her a daughter of Aphrodite, while Aeschylus elsewhere calls her a child of Ate, Ruin. Alcman names her sister of Tyche and Eunomia, and Nonnus makes her a daughter of Dionysus and Aphrodite and one of the Graces.

In Works and Days, Hesiod shows her fastening golden necklaces on Pandora, the first woman. Other accounts make her the wife of Hermes, or of Phoroneus, the primordial king of Argos, according to commentary on Euripides. In Nonnus, she disguises herself as a slave and wraps Cadmus in mist to lead him to the palace of Electra.

A late source also credits her with a daughter, Iynx.

Worship and religion

Pausanias credits Theseus with founding a cult of Aphrodite Pandemos and Peitho in Athens after the city was unified, and he reports a sanctuary of Peitho at Sicyon. In Athens she was worshipped independently as the goddess of sexual and rhetorical persuasion, and a priestess of Peitho had a reserved seat in the Theatre of Dionysus. At Megara her statue stood beside that of Aphrodite Praxis. She was linked to marriage, since suitors had to win over the bride's family.

Legacy

Her name came to stand for persuasion in both artistic and rhetorical language, and Romans knew her as Suadela. She recurs in vase painting, where she appears at weddings and in the abduction of the Leucippidae.

Ancient sources: Hesiod (Theogony and Works and Days), Sappho, Aeschylus (Agamemnon and Suppliant Women), Alcman, Nonnus (Dionysiaca) and Pausanias.

Trivia

Necklaces for Pandora. According to Hesiod, Peitho and the Graces adorned Pandora with golden necklaces. Jewelry was viewed with suspicion in Greek literature, as a way for women to seduce.
A priestess in the theatre. In Athens, the Theatre of Dionysus had a seat reserved for the priestess of Peitho.
