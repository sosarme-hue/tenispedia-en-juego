Penelope

Penelope is the wife of Odysseus and queen of Ithaca in Greek mythology, daughter of the Spartan king Icarius and mother of Telemachus. Her story is told mainly in Homer's Odyssey, and her loyalty while she waited for her husband's return has made her the classic model of faithfulness in Western literature.

The myth

According to Homer's Odyssey, Penelope is the daughter of Icarius, a Laconian king, and the wife of Odysseus, king of Ithaca, with whom she had Telemachus. Before the Trojan War, her marriage came about through a bargain: Odysseus advised Tyndareus on how to avoid quarrels among the suitors of Helen, in return for the hand of his niece.

When Odysseus left for Troy, Penelope was left in charge of the household and the throne, besieged by many suitors who wanted to marry her and seize the estate. To gain time, she promised to choose a husband once she finished a burial shroud for Laertes, Odysseus's father. She wove by day and unpicked the work at night, until a maid betrayed her and she had to finish the cloth. She then set the contest of the bow: whoever could string Odysseus's bow and shoot an arrow through twelve axe heads in a row would win her hand. None managed it, and Odysseus, who had returned disguised as a beggar, passed the test and revealed himself.

The Odyssey ends with their recognition through the marriage bed, a secret only the two of them shared. Later traditions, preserved by Pseudo-Apollodorus, say that Odysseus set off again for Thesprotia, where he married the queen Callidice, while other versions speak of infidelity or of a lasting reunion. According to the myth, as a child Penelope was cast into the sea by her parents and rescued by birds called pēnelopes, from which her name derives. Later, when she was married, Nauplius threw her into the sea again to avenge his son Palamedes, and the same birds saved her once more.

Legacy

Her name was explained in folk etymology as the face of the weft, a nod to her habit of weaving and unweaving to deceive her suitors. Linnaeus took the term that the lexicographer Hesychius glossed as some kind of bird and gave the binomial Anas penelope to the Eurasian wigeon. She remains the paradigm of fidelity in Western culture.

Ancient sources: Homer's Odyssey is the main source; Pseudo-Apollodorus, Strabo and Pausanias preserve variants about her origin, her family and her later life.

Trivia

A bird's name. The lexicographer Hesychius of Alexandria glossed the word pēnelops as some kind of bird, and the heroine's name is traced to it.
A duck with her name. Centuries later, Linnaeus used that reference to give the Eurasian wigeon the scientific name Anas penelope.
