sorceress of Greek mythology, daughter of Aeëtes

Medea

Medea is a sorceress of Greek mythology, daughter of King Aeëtes of Colchis. Her story with Jason, the quest for the Golden Fleece and the tragedy that follows in Corinth make her the archetype of the witch and of the betrayed foreign woman.

The myth

According to the myth, Medea was the daughter of Aeëtes, king of Colchis, and a granddaughter of the sun god Helios. Hesiod mentions her as early as the Theogony, around 700 BC, but her story is best known from Apollonius of Rhodes's epic Argonautica and from Euripides's tragedy. Many versions present her as a priestess of Hecate and as a pupil of her aunt Circe in the arts of magic.

When Jason arrives in Colchis to claim the Golden Fleece, King Aeëtes sets him impossible tasks. Medea, who has fallen in love with him, gives him potions and advice: Jason yokes the fire-breathing oxen, sows the dragon's teeth that turn into warriors, and makes them fight each other with a stone. Then Medea lulls to sleep the serpent guarding the fleece with herbs. The killing of her brother Absyrtus is a key episode of the escape with Jason: some versions have her kill him, others have Jason do it.

In Corinth, Jason decides to marry the daughter of King Creon. In Euripides's tragedy, Medea takes revenge by sending the new bride a poisoned crown and robes, and then kills her own sons, so that Jason is left without heirs.

According to Herodotus, Medea ended up on the Iranian plateau among the Aryans, who later came to be called Medes. Other traditions speak of her son Medus, who gave his name to the region of Media.

Worship and religion

There is no evidence of a cult devoted to Medea herself. Her religious link is with Hecate, whose priestess she is imagined to be in many versions. In Corinth, the children she killed were buried in the sanctuary of Hera.

Legacy

Medea is the model of the witch and of the foreign, independent woman who defies the norms of her time. Her figure entered tragedy through Euripides and Seneca, whose plays kept her alive in Western literature. Her story also helped shape the image of the sorceress in later drama and narrative.

Ancient sources: Hesiod (Theogony), Euripides (Medea), Apollonius of Rhodes (Argonautica), Seneca (Medea), Herodotus (Histories) and Diodorus Siculus (Bibliotheca historica).

Trivia

The Medes. According to tradition, her son Medus gave his name to the region of Media. Herodotus also links Medea to the Aryans who came to be called Medes.
Burial in Corinth. The children Medea killed were buried by Jason in the sanctuary of Hera in Corinth.
An afterlife in the Elysian Fields. After her death, Medea dwelt in the Elysian Fields. Some sources say she could have married Achilles there.
