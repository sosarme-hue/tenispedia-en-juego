mother of Heracles in Greek mythology

Alcmene

Alcmene is, in Greek mythology, the wife of Amphitryon and the mother of Heracles, the most famous hero of the classical tradition, conceived with Zeus. Her story combines divine deception, a prodigious birth and an old age shaped by Eurystheus's persecution.

The myth

According to tradition, Alcmene was the daughter of Electryon, son of Perseus, and cousin of Amphitryon. Sources disagree about her mother: some name Anaxo, others Lysidice or Eurydice, and Pausanias records the poet Asius's version, which makes her the daughter of Amphiaraus and Eriphyle. Homer presents her as a woman of great beauty, and Hesiod praises her stature and wisdom.

Before the wedding, Amphitryon had to avenge her brothers, who had died in a war against the Teleboans. During that campaign, Zeus, desiring her, made one night last three and lay with Alcmene in the form of Amphitryon. When her husband returned, she told him they had already been together, and the seer Tiresias explained that it had been Zeus. From that union were born Heracles, the god's son, and Iphicles, Amphitryon's son.

Heracles's birth was marked by Hera's anger. Zeus had declared that the next descendant of Perseus would rule Mycenae, and Hera delayed Heracles's birth and hastened that of Eurystheus, the hero's cousin. In other versions, Heracles strangled the serpents Hera sent to his cradle, and Lucina, goddess of childbirth, was tricked by a servant of Alcmene, who deceived her into loosening her posture. Later, after Amphitryon's death, Alcmene married Rhadamanthys and lived with him in exile at Ocaleae, in Boeotia.

When Eurystheus pursued Heracles's family, he was captured and brought before Alcmene, who gouged out his eyes. About her end there are two accounts: according to the Megarians she died in Megara, while the Thebans say she was turned to stone.

Worship and religion

Alcmene was venerated in Thebes and Athens, where Pausanias mentions an altar to her at the Cynosarges alongside those of Heracles, Hebe and Iolaus. In Megara her tomb was shown near the Olympieum.

Ancient sources: Homer mentions her in the Iliad in connection with Heracles's birth, Hesiod describes her beauty, and Ovid narrates her labour in the Metamorphoses, where the maid Galanthis is turned into a weasel.
