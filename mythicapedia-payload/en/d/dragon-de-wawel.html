Wawel Dragon

The Wawel Dragon is a creature of Polish tradition tied to Wawel Hill and its castle in Kraków. One of the oldest legends in Poland, it explains the early history of the city and the role of the legendary king Krak, its founder.

The myth

According to the earliest account, recorded by the chronicler Wincenty Kadłubek in the 13th century, a dragon preyed on the people of King Krak's realm, and the king had to offer it cattle every week to keep it calm. In the end, two of the king's sons defeated it with a trick: they fed it a cattle skin stuffed with burning sulfur. But the younger brother murdered the elder to claim the victory, was banished, and Princess Wanda inherited the throne.

Later chronicles changed the details. Jan Długosz, in the 15th century, credits the king himself with the plan and makes the younger prince, Lech, the killer. The most popular version, recorded by Marcin Bielski in 1597, gives the credit to a cobbler named Skuba, who filled a lamb's skin with sulfur. The dragon, tormented by thirst after eating it, drank from the Vistula until it burst. The cobbler married Princess Wanda, and the monster's cave was placed beneath the castle. Historians have suggested readings such as an echo of Avar raids or an ancient Indo-European myth of a thunder god defeating a serpent.

Legacy

The cave, known as Smocza Jama, can still be visited beside the Vistula. In front of it stands a metal sculpture by Bronisław Chromy that breathes fire every five minutes, or whenever it receives a text message reading SMOK. A street in the city is called Ulica Smocza, and the Kraków Film Festival awards prizes named Wawel Dragons. The dragon also appears in Marvel's Nextwave comic series under the name The Beast of Kraków.

Ancient sources: The Polish Chronicle of Wincenty Kadłubek (13th century), the chronicle of Jan Długosz (15th century) and the Kronika Polska of Marcin Bielski (1597).

Trivia

A storybook name. In children's literature the cobbler appears as Dratewka, the hero of a play by Maria Kownacka published in 1935.
A biblical echo. A later chronicle compares the sons of Krak with Daniel, who killed the dragon of Babylon.
