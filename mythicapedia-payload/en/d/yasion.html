hero of Greek mythology, mythical founder of the Samothrace mysteries

Iasion

Iasion (also Iasíōn, Iasus or Eetion) is a figure of Greek mythology credited with founding the mystery rites on the island of Samothrace. The son of Zeus and the Pleiad Electra, he is best remembered for his bond with Demeter and for the punishment he suffered at the hands of the king of the gods.

The myth

According to tradition, Iasion and his brother Dardanus were sons of Zeus and Electra, although genealogies vary. Apollodorus, Hellanicus and Diodorus Siculus give this parentage, and Diodorus adds Harmonia as a sister. Hyginus makes him a son of Ilithyius, and an Italian version attributes him to Corythus. Depending on the source, he came from Italy, Arcadia or Crete, fleeing a flood to the island of Samothrace, where his father taught him the mysteries of Demeter. He was the first to initiate foreigners into them, among them the Theban Cadmus, who later married Harmonia on the island.

At the wedding of Cadmus and Harmonia, Demeter drew Iasion away from the other guests, and the two lay together in a freshly ploughed field. Their children were Plutus, god of wealth, and Philomelus, who were fated never to agree. When the couple returned to the feast, Zeus guessed what had happened from the mud on the goddess and struck Iasion down with a thunderbolt. Ovid gives a different ending: Demeter pleaded for his life, and Iasion lived to old age as her husband. Servius says his brother Dardanus killed him, while Hyginus attributes his death to horses.

Worship and religion

The Samothracian mysteries were linked to Demeter and were initiatory: those who received them took part in secret rites. Some traditions say that Iasion had a son, Corybas, with Cybele, and that the Corybantes, the goddess's priest-warriors, took their name from him. The sources say nothing about whether his own cult survived.

Legacy

According to some versions, after his death Iasion was placed among the stars alongside the hero Triptolemus, forming the constellation Gemini. The most common version, however, assigns that constellation to Castor and Pollux.

Ancient sources: Apollodorus, Hellanicus, Diodorus Siculus, Hyginus, Servius and Ovid.

Trivia

Many names. Según las fuentes, this figure appears under several names, such as Iasion, Iasus, Yaso or Eetion, reflecting the different traditions that recorded him.
Children doomed to disagree. Plutus and Philomelus, children of Iasion and Demeter, were condemned never to agree with one another.
