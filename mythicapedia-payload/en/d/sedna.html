Inuit goddess of the sea

Sedna

Sedna is an Inuit goddess of the sea, mistress of marine animals and ruler of Adlivun, the underworld where the dead are judged. Her story, which is also a creation myth, explains the Inuit bond with sea hunting.

The myth

According to the tradition, Sedna was a young woman who refused every suitor. One day a ship appeared on the horizon, and its captain, a handsome stranger, carried her away. Later she discovered he was really a shaman. When her father heard her cries from across the sea, he set out in his kayak to rescue her.

As she tried to escape, the shaman summoned a furious storm and ordered the sea to open. Terrified, the father threw his daughter overboard. She clung to the side of the boat, so he cut off her fingers with an axe. The fingers became small seals and fish, and her knuckles and hands became walruses, whales and other sea animals. Sedna sank to the bottom of the ocean, where she became a powerful spirit.

There are many versions. In some, her suitor is a dog or a bird, and in others her father drowns himself in grief.

Worship and religion

In her undersea house, Sedna holds back the animals when people break taboos, and hunters catch nothing. A angakkuq, or shaman, then travels beneath the sea to negotiate with her. Sometimes he brushes and braids her hair, something she cannot do without fingers. Traditional Inuit women wore tattoos on their fingers and knuckles that alluded to her story.

Legacy

Her image appears on a Greenlandic postage stamp from 1957 and on a statue in Nuuk, where she is shown with a shaman combing her hair. In 2024 Canada issued a two-dollar coin bearing her likeness, and Inuit art often portrays her with a woman's upper body and a marine mammal's tail. She also stars in the television programme Nuliajuk: Mother of the Sea Beasts.

In art

Trivia

Mermaid. In art she is usually shown with a woman's head and torso and a marine mammal's tail, similar to a mermaid, although the story itself does not mention this.
A father who drowns. In a version told by the Iglulik storyteller Oruto, the father, overcome with sorrow, drowns himself at the water's edge and also becomes a spirit.
