Burgundian warrior in Germanic and Norse legend

Hagen

Hagen is a warrior of the Burgundian kingdom at Worms, a central figure in the legends of the Nibelungs. He is the chief enemy of the hero Siegfried and, in the German tradition, the man who kills him, making him one of the darkest figures of medieval heroic literature.

The myth

In the Nibelungenlied, Hagen comes from Tronje and grew up at the court of the Huns, where his father served King Etzel. He later joined the service of King Gunther of Burgundy and became his finest knight, until the young Siegfried arrived. Envy of the hero drove him to seek his downfall. When Gunther married Brunhild and a quarrel broke out between the queens, Hagen saw his chance. Kriemhild, Siegfried's wife, asked him to watch over her husband's back, which was invulnerable except for one spot. During a hunt, as Siegfried drank from a stream, Hagen ran him through with a spear.

Hagen then seized the Nibelung treasure and threw it into the Rhine. Years later Kriemhild, now married to Etzel, invited the Burgundians to her kingdom to take revenge. There, after a quarrel, Hagen killed her son, and a battle followed in which only Hagen and Gunther survived, defeated by Dietrich of Bern. Hagen refused to reveal where the treasure lay even after his king was dead, and Kriemhild ended his life.

In Norse tradition, Hagen's counterpart Högni plays a smaller part. The killing of Sigurd falls to his brother Gutthorm, and it is Gunnar who keeps the secret while Högni lives. According to the Saga of Thidrek of Bern, Hagen was conceived by an elf and was not wholly human.

Legacy

Richard Wagner placed him in the opera Götterdämmerung, part of The Ring Cycle, where he is Gunther's half-brother, the illegitimate son of the dwarf Alberich, and a sinister schemer. According to the bass Kurt Moll, the role demands a shouting, piercing vocal style that only very powerful singers can sustain. Field Marshal Paul von Hindenburg referred to Siegfried's death at Hagen's hand in his 1920 memoir.

Ancient sources: The Nibelungenlied (around 1200), the Atlamál and Drápa Niflunga in the Norse tradition, and the Saga of Thidrek of Bern.

Trivia

One eye. In two of the German accounts Hagen has only one eye, a trait that reinforces his image as a grim and violent figure.
A disputed name. The epithet Tronje has been linked to Drongen in Ghent and to Xanten, but scholars consider these identifications highly speculative.
