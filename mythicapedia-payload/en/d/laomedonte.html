king of Troy, father of Priam

Laomedon

Laomedon is, in Greek mythology, one of the first kings of Troy and the father of Priam. He is remembered as a faithless ruler: according to myth, he refused to pay the gods who built the city walls and broke his word to Heracles, who ended his reign.

The myth

Laomedon was the son of King Ilus. Depending on the tradition, his mother was Eurydice, Leucippe or Batia, and his wives were Placia, Strymo, Zeuxippe or Leucippe. Among his many children were Tithonus, Lampus, Clytius, Hicetaon and Podarces, who would later be known as Priam.

According to Apollodorus, Laomedon commissioned Apollo and Poseidon to build the walls of Troy, a punishment imposed by Zeus after a conspiracy against him. In Homer and Diodorus Siculus, Poseidon built the wall alone while Apollo tended the royal herds on Mount Ida. When the work was finished, the king refused to pay the agreed wage of thirty Trojan drachmas. In revenge, Apollo sent a plague and Poseidon raised a sea monster that attacked the plain.

An oracle demanded the regular sacrifice of a young virgin of the royal family. When the turn fell to Hesione, the king's daughter, Heracles offered to save her in exchange for the divine mares that Zeus had given Tros as compensation for the abduction of Ganymede. Heracles killed the monster, but Laomedon gave him mortal horses instead of the promised ones. Diodorus adds that the king also imprisoned the heralds sent by Heracles, Iphicles and Telamon, whom Priam saved at the last moment.

Enraged, Heracles attacked Troy, killed Laomedon and nearly all his sons, sparing only Hesione and Podarces. Hesione was given to Telamon as a prize of war, but she was able to ransom her brother with her golden veil. Podarces thus became Priam, from the Greek priamai, meaning to buy, and inherited the throne of Troy.

Legacy

Laomedon hardly appears in art or language, but his story served as a classic example of perjury in Greek tradition. In the modern interpretation of Georges Dumezil, the legend displays three forms of hybris: economic disloyalty toward Apollo and Poseidon, betrayal of Heracles, and violation of the sacred status of heralds. For Dumezil, the tale reflects the Proto-Indo-European tripartite structure: the herder and craftsman, the warrior hero, and Zeus as protector of heralds.

Ancient sources: He is mentioned in Apollodorus's Library of Mythology, in Homer's Iliad (through his son Bucolion), in Pindar, Diodorus Siculus, Dictys Cretensis and Dares Phrygius.

Trivia

A stork. According to myth, his daughter Antigone boasted that her hair was more beautiful than Hera's, and the goddess punished her by turning her into a stork.
The name of Priam. Priam received his name after his sister Hesione ransomed him with her golden veil. His original name was Podarces.
