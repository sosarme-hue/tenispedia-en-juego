Greek goddess of wisdom and war

Athena

Athena is the Greek goddess of wisdom, strategic warfare and handicrafts, one of the twelve Olympian gods and the protector of Athens. Her association with civic order and justice made her one of the most influential figures in Greek art and thought.

The myth

According to the most widespread version, Athena is the daughter of Zeus and the Oceanid Metis, his first wife and counsellor. Hesiod tells that Zeus, warned that Metis's children could surpass him, swallowed the pregnant goddess. Athena was then born from her father's forehead, fully grown and fully armed, after Hephaestus, or in other accounts Prometheus, Hermes or Palaemon, split his head open with an axe. Homer makes no mention of her mother or of how she was born, while Pindar adds the war cry that shook the heavens.

Later traditions offer other origins. One makes her a daughter of Pallas, a winged giant she killed, whose skin she later wore as her aegis. Herodotus records a Libyan version in which she is the daughter of Poseidon and Lake Tritonis, and was adopted by Zeus. A fragment attributed to Sanchuniathon makes her a daughter of Cronus. Clement of Alexandria distinguishes up to five goddesses of that name. In the Athenian tradition, she competed with Poseidon for the patronage of the city: he offered a horse and she created the first olive tree, the gift the Athenians preferred. She also fought in the Gigantomachy and aided heroes such as Perseus, Heracles, Bellerophon and Jason. In the Iliad she supported the Achaeans, and in the Odyssey she watched over Odysseus.

The goddess was a virgin with no interest in love affairs, and her virginity was understood as independence and protection of the city. She acted with prudence rather than fury: she protected heroes but took no pleasure in bloodshed.

Worship and religion

Athena was the chief deity of Athens, where her temples stood atop the Acropolis. The Parthenon, dedicated to her, is the most famous monument on that hill. Her main festival, the Panathenaia, was celebrated in midsummer and was the most important event in the Athenian calendar. As a protector of justice, she was regarded as a guardian of law in the Areopagus.

Legacy

Since the Renaissance, Athena has become an international symbol of wisdom, the arts and classical learning, and Western artists have often used her as an emblem of freedom and democracy. Her owl, helmet and aegis have appeared on shields, emblems and coins. Her Roman counterpart, Minerva, has given her name to institutions and cultural works.

Ancient sources: Homer mentions her in the Iliad and the Odyssey, Hesiod in the Theogony, and Pindar describes her birth in his odes; she also appears in the Homeric Hymns.

Trivia

An olive tree against a horse. In the Athenian account, Athena and Poseidon competed for the city's patronage. The god offered a horse and the goddess an olive tree, and the Athenians preferred her gift.
Name and city. Ancient scholars debated whether the goddess took her name from the city or the city from her, and today it is generally agreed that she took it from Athens.
