Medicine Buddha of Mahayana Buddhism

Bhaisajyaguru

Bhaisajyaguru, known as the Medicine Buddha, is the buddha of healing in Mahayana Buddhism. He is revered for his power to cure suffering of body and mind, and for embodying the healing aspect of the historical Buddha Shakyamuni.

The myth

According to the sutra that bears his name, Bhaisajyaguru was a bodhisattva who, on reaching enlightenment, made twelve great vows to relieve the suffering of beings. His name is usually rendered as "Medicine Master and King of Lapis Lazuli Light". After attaining buddhahood he became the buddha of the eastern pure land of Vaiduryanirbhasa, "Pure Lapis Lazuli", where two bodhisattvas, Suryaprabha and Candraprabha, symbolizing the light of the sun and of the moon, attend him.

The tradition describes him as a physician who cures suffering through the medicine of his teachings. His vows promise light and good health, provision for the poor, the return of the misguided, relief for the hungry and the cold, and freedom for the oppressed. One vow stands out: to help women who wish to be reborn as men. Those who hear his name with faith or recite his sutra are said to receive protection and benefits for their health.

Worship and religion

His devotion was especially widespread in China, Korea, Japan and Southeast Asia, where monasteries gave rise to hospitals, leprosy centres and public dispensaries. In Chinese temples of the Pure Land school he appears on the main altar alongside Shakyamuni and Amitabha. Temples dedicated to him alone are rare, so devotees usually visit temples that house his image. Chinese tradition invokes him as the "Buddha who grants longevity and eases suffering". In Japan he is called Yakushi, and in Tibet, Sangye Menla.

Legacy

His image, usually blue and holding a bowl of medicine in his left hand, is among the most recognizable in East Asian Buddhist art. The lapis lazuli associated with him has been linked to healing since antiquity, and some scholars connect his colour with the lapis necklace of the Sumerian goddess Inanna.

Ancient sources: The Medicine Buddha Sutra, translated into Chinese during the Eastern Jin dynasty and later revised by Xuanzang, and Sanskrit manuscripts from Gandhara.
