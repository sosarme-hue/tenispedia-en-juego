Zen story of wordless transmission

Flower Sermon

The Flower Sermon is a Zen story about the origin of direct transmission of teaching: the Buddha holds up a flower without speaking, and only Mahākāśyapa answers with a smile. It is one of the founding tales of Chan and Zen Buddhism.

The myth

According to the tradition, the Buddha Shakyamuni was on Mount Grdhrakuta and, instead of speaking, held up a flower to his listeners. No one understood the gesture except Mahākāśyapa, who smiled. The Buddha thus confirmed that he was passing on his mind directly, without words, and Mahākāśyapa became the first patriarch of Zen.

The tale is known from Chinese Chan Buddhist sources, and the earliest documented version dates from 1036. Within Zen, the flower expresses the ineffable nature of things as they are (tathātā), and the smile stands for wisdom passed on without words.

Legacy

The story appears as Case 6 of The Gateless Barrier (Wúmenguān, in Japanese Mumonkan), a collection of Zen koans that remains one of the key references of this school.

Ancient sources: The earliest known version appeared in 1036, recorded by Chinese Chan Buddhists, and it is also included in Wumen Huikai's The Gateless Barrier.

Trivia

A smile with a name. The Chinese name Niān huā wéi xiào literally means 'picking up a flower and smiling'.
A classic koan. The story is part of The Gateless Barrier, a collection of Zen koans in which each case serves as a subject for meditation.
