Thracian horseman

The Thracian horseman is a recurring motif in reliefs from the Balkans, dating from roughly the 3rd century BC to the 3rd century AD, showing a hunter on horseback accompanied by a dog or a boar. He is a heroic divinity of Thracian tradition who later merged with other gods.

The myth

The ancient sources offer no single story; the figure is reconstructed mainly from reliefs and inscriptions. Inscriptions call him Heros, Heron, or simply lord (kyrios), and the word seems to be the term 'hero' used as a personal name. He is shown hunting on horseback, riding from left to right, with a dog or a boar beneath the horse's hooves; in some images the dog is replaced by a lion.

Scholars think he was a composite figure: a Thracian hero, perhaps inspired by Rhesus, the Thracian king mentioned in the Iliad, with Scythian and Hellenistic traits added. His image also borrows from Roman funerary reliefs of cavalrymen. In the Roman period he sometimes appears beside a tree entwined by a serpent, or approaching a goddess, motifs of Greco-Roman and possibly Scythian origin.

Worship and religion

His cult was especially important at Philippi, where he bore the epithets Auloneites, saviour (soter) and answerer of prayers (epekoos). Under Emperor Gordian III he appears on coins minted at Tlos in Lycia and at Istros in Lower Moesia. In Bulgaria he is associated with Asclepius and Hygieia.

Legacy

His motif survived in Christianised form in the equestrian imagery of Saint George and Saint Demetrius. The medieval Madara Rider, a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1979 in Bulgaria, incorporates elements of this ancient Thracian cult.

Ancient sources: Votive and funerary inscriptions from Romania and Bulgaria naming him Heros and Heron; the Iliad, which mentions Rhesus; and Festus, who records a Messapian horse sacrifice to Iuppiter Menzanas.

Trivia

A word for horse. The Palaeo-Balkan root of the name is reconstructed as Me(n)zana-, related to Albanian mëz ('foal') and Romanian mânz.
A medieval relief. The Madara Rider, carved on a plateau in northeastern Bulgaria, is dated to about the 7th or 8th century.
