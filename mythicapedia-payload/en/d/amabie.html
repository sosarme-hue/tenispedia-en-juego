prophetic mermaid-like creature of Japanese folklore

Amabie

The amabie is a prophetic creature of Japanese folklore, a mermaid-like being that, according to tradition, rises from the sea to foretell either a bountiful harvest or an epidemic. It belongs to the amabiko group, a family of yōkai with varied forms that share the gift of prophecy and the custom of displaying their image.

The myth

According to the account, in mid-May 1846 (around the middle of the fourth month of the third year of the Kōka era, in the Edo period), a glowing object was seen at sea almost every night off Higo Province, today Kumamoto Prefecture. A town official went to the shore and witnessed the appearance: the creature had long hair, a bird-like beak, three legs and scales covering its body from the neck down. It identified itself as an amabie, said it lived in the open sea, and prophesied six years of good harvests. If disease came, people should draw its likeness and show the picture to the sick, who would recover. Then it returned to the sea.

Later reports describe amabiko with ape-like, daruma-doll-like or torsoless forms, with three or four legs. One warns that 70 percent of Japan's population would die that year, a fate that could be averted by displaying its picture. An amabiko-no-mikoto, given a divine title, was supposedly seen in a Niigata rice paddy in 1875, and one scholar has compared its name to the Amatsukami, the heavenly deities of ancient Japan.

Worship and religion

No temples or festivals dedicated to it are documented. Its veneration consisted mainly of copying and displaying its image as protection against illness, and the story spread through woodblock bulletins (kawaraban) that printed its portrait. The sources do not show whether this practice survives today.

Ancient sources: The evidence consists of Edo-period woodblock bulletins and manuscripts (a kawaraban of 1846 and a leaflet of 1844), plus Meiji-era newspapers; no canonical ancient texts mention it.

Trivia

A town that never existed. Accounts of the ape-voiced amabiko place their appearance in Mana-kōri, a county of Higo that did not exist. Several locations in this group turn out to be fictitious place names.
A divine title. The amabiko-no-mikoto receives the honorific suffix -mikoto, reserved for deities. One researcher has linked it to the Amatsukami of ancient Japan.
