alleged Slavic agrarian god of the Polabians

Hennil

Hennil, also called Bendil, is an alleged agrarian god of the Polabian Slavs, mentioned only by Bishop Thietmar of Merseburg. His existence and nature are heavily debated, and some scholars believe he was more likely a ritual object or a misread name.

The myth

The only testimony is Thietmar's Chronicle, which records that in 1017, in his diocese, demons attacked a woman's house, and mentions a pagan rite in passing. According to him, Hennil was invoked with a greeting before a staff crowned by a hand holding a ring, a symbol of fertility.

Scholars have proposed many etymologies: Narbut linked the name to the Lithuanian Goniglis, Grimm to the Polish word hejnał, and Siebs to the Germanic Henno. Álvarez-Pedrosa Núñez, based on a second manuscript of the Chronicle, proposes the form Bendil, related to the root bʰendʰ- meaning "to bind".

Worship and religion

Thietmar describes the rite, but the sources offer no details about temples, festivals or priests, so little is known about how it was practised.

Ancient sources: The Chronicle of Bishop Thietmar of Merseburg, written in the early eleventh century, is the only source that mentions him.

Trivia

A staff as a god. Some historians believe Thietmar may have mistaken an ordinary ritual staff for a deity. This is one reason many doubt that Hennil was truly a god.
Two versions of the name. The name appears as Hennil in the best-known manuscript of the Chronicle and as Bendil in a second manuscript from the monastery of Corvey.
