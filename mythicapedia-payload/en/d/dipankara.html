Buddha of the past in Buddhism

Dipankara

Dipankara is one of the Buddhas of the past in Buddhist tradition, who attained enlightenment eons before Gautama, the historical Buddha. He matters because he illustrates the belief in a succession of Buddhas across vast spans of time.

The myth

According to tradition, Dipankara lived on Earth for a hundred thousand years, and attained enlightenment eons before Gautama. Texts speak of an enormous number of past Buddhas, often grouped together as the "Thousand Buddhas".

The best-known episode features Sumedha, a wealthy brahmin turned hermit, who kneels and lays his long black hair on the ground so that Dipankara can cross a puddle of mud without soiling his feet. Dipankara then tells him that in future ages he will become a Buddha called Shakyamuni, born among the Shakyas, son of King Suddhodana and Queen Maya, with Sariputta and Moggallana as his chief disciples. Sumedha replies that he aims to become a Buddha himself.

In the story, Gautama received this prediction after filling the ten paramitas, or "perfections". The encounter appears in the Mahayana Sangatha Sutra, among other prophecies of his future enlightenment.

Worship and religion

By the seventeenth century, Dipankara was a figure of veneration in Nepalese Buddhist communities, who regarded him as a protector of merchants and sailors. Statues were placed along the coast to guide and protect ships. According to the Buddhavamsa, he attained nirvana in Nandarama, where a stupa was built.

Legacy

His statues survive in the Longmen and Yungang Grottoes in China, where Chinese Buddhism honours him as one of the Buddhas of the past. One of the Bamiyan Buddhas, destroyed by the Taliban in 2001, was thought to depict him. In Yiguandao he forms, with Gautama and Maitreya, the "Buddhas of Three Times".

Ancient sources: The Buddhavamsa, a Pali chronicle of the Buddhas, and the Mahayana Sangatha Sutra.

Trivia

Sumedha's hair. In the story, the future Gautama offers his hair as a carpet so that Dipankara will not soil his feet. It is one of the most depicted episodes in Buddhist art.
Protector of sailors. In Nepal he was believed to protect ships, which is why his statues were raised along the coast.
