Yggdrasil

Yggdrasil is the great ash tree of Norse mythology, an immense tree that connects and holds up the Nine Worlds. For the Norse, the human world was just one branch of it, and its image remains one of the most recognizable in Germanic cosmology.

The myth

According to the Eddas, Yggdrasil is a vast, sacred ash whose branches stretch through the heavens, held up by three roots that reach very different places: one to the spring Hvergelmir, one to the well of Mímir, and one to the well Urðarbrunnr in the heavens. The Norns, maidens who dwell by the roots, water the tree so that it does not wither. The gods go to the tree every day to hold their assemblies, and Heimdall keeps watch beside it.

The tree is a scene of constant struggle. An eagle sits in its crown, and between its eyes sits a hawk named Veðrfölnir. The squirrel Ratatoskr runs up and down the trunk carrying messages between the eagle and the dragon Níðhöggr, who gnaws the roots from below along with a swarm of serpents such as Góinn and Móinn. Four stags, Dáinn, Dvalinn, Duneyrr and Duraþrór, feed on the highest branches, and according to the Grímnismál the tree suffers more than men can know.

Yggdrasil is also linked to Odin's sacrifice. According to the Hávamál, the god hung from a tree for nine days without food or drink, and when he fell he had won the runes, the knowledge he later gave to humanity. The poem never names the tree, but nearly all scholars identify it with Yggdrasil. In the Völuspá the tree appears again in the prophecy of Ragnarök, the end of the gods.

Worship and religion

The texts do not describe temples or rites dedicated to the tree, but they place it at the centre of religious life: in the Prose Edda it is the holiest place, where the gods hold their courts every day. It is therefore studied within the wider Germanic tradition of sacred trees and groves. No living cult exists today.

Ancient sources: The Völuspá, the Hávamál and the Grímnismál of the Poetic Edda, and Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda, which describe it in detail.

Trivia

A name with several readings. Yggdrasil is usually read as "Odin's horse", alluding to Odin riding between the worlds. Others translate it as "tree of terror" or "gallows of the hanged", and some scholars propose an even older origin.
A horse that is a gallows. In Norse poetry, "horse of the hanged" is a kenning for the gallows. This may explain how the name passed from Odin's gallows to the tree itself.
