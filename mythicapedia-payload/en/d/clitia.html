Clytie

Clytie is a water nymph of Greek mythology, daughter of the Titans Oceanus and Tethys, best known for her unrequited love for the sun-god Helios. Her story, told by Ovid, explains the origin of the heliotrope, the flower that turns to follow the sun.

The myth

According to the myth, Clytie loved the sun-god Helios in vain. He fell for the princess Leucothoe under the influence of Aphrodite and abandoned her. Hurt, Clytie revealed the affair to the girl's father, King Orchamus, who buried his daughter alive. Helios arrived too late to save her and turned her into a frankincense tree. Clytie, still unloved, sat naked on the rocks for nine days, staring at the sun, until the gods transformed her into the heliotrope, a flower that still turns toward the sun on its daily journey.

Ovid tells the fullest version in his Metamorphoses. Hesiod includes her among the Oceanids, and Lactantius Placidus attributes the love story to him, although some scholars doubt that attribution.

Legacy

The flower's name, heliotrope, means "sun-turning", and her story is often seen as the origin of the link between the plant and the sun. In modern tradition the sunflower has taken the place of the heliotrope, although it is a American plant unknown to ancient Greek and Roman authors. Edith Hamilton noted that Clytie's case is unique in Greek mythology, since here a maiden loves a god who does not love her back.

Ancient sources: Hesiod, who includes her among the three thousand Oceanids, Hyginus, who gives her the same parentage, and above all Ovid, in his Metamorphoses.

Trivia

A unique case. Edith Hamilton noted that in Greek mythology it is usually a god who loves an unwilling maiden, whereas here the roles are reversed.
A flower that closes at night. Pliny the Elder wrote that the heliotrope turns with the sun even on cloudy days and closes its blue flower at night, as if mourning.
