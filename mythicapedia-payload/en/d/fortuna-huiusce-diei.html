Roman epithet of the goddess Fortuna, the fortune of the day

Fortuna Huiusce Diei

Fortuna Huiusce Diei, "the fortune of this day", was an epithet of the goddess Fortuna in ancient Rome, best known for her temple in the Area Sacra of Largo Argentina. She embodies the Roman belief that each day of the year had a nature of its own, which priests had to understand.

The myth

This epithet has no myth of its own. What is known comes from her cult and from the founding of her temple, which followed a vow made in battle. Cicero counts her among the deities who should be honoured in his ideal state, because she empowers each day.

According to the historical tradition, Quintus Lutatius Catulus vowed the temple at the Battle of Vercellae in 101 BC, and it was dedicated on July 30, the anniversary of the battle. It is the only known temple to this goddess, and probably the first.

Worship and religion

Her festival was marked each year with a public sacrifice, showing that although the temple was privately vowed and sponsored, her cult belonged to the state religion. From 45 BC it coincided with the last day of the Ludi Victoriae Caesaris, games held in honour of Julius Caesar. The temple housed a colossal cult statue, an acrolith nearly 8 metres tall, probably standing and holding a cornucopia.

Legacy

The temple also served as an art museum, and Cicero recommended it to those too poor to own their own collections. Today the Area Sacra of Largo Argentina is an important archaeological site in Rome.

Ancient sources: Cicero mentions her in his writings on the ideal state, and Varro describes the temple building.
