Etruscan god of trade and messenger of the gods

Turms

Turms is the Etruscan counterpart of Hermes in Greek myth and of Mercury in Roman myth. A messenger of the gods and patron of trade, he also guided the souls of the dead into the afterlife, making him a key figure in Etruscan religion.

The myth

According to Etruscan tradition, Turms was the messenger of the gods, especially of Tinia, the Etruscan Jupiter. Sources also suggest he was thought to be at the service of other deities, an idea scholars connect with the epithet Camillus, meaning 'servant'.

One of his most striking roles was that of psychopomp, the guide of souls. He appears on Etruscan sarcophagi, once beside Charun and Cerberus. In another depiction, labelled 'Turms of Hades', he brings the shade of Tiresias to consult Odysseus in the underworld.

Turms also appears in scenes of the Judgement of Paris and alongside Hercle (Heracles) or Perseus. He is shown with Hermes' attributes: the caduceus, the petasos, often winged, and winged sandals.

Worship and religion

Turms is known more from everyday objects, such as decorated mirrors, than from cult images. One dedication is taken to suggest a temple to him at Cortona.

Legacy

The name Turms is distinctively Etruscan, unlike Hercle or Apulu, whose names were borrowed from Greek. His image survives mainly in the decoration of objects and sarcophagi.
