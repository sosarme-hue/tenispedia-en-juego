Roman goddess of the Earth

Tellus

Tellus, also called Terra or Tellus Mater, is the Roman goddess who personifies the Earth. She was linked to the fertility of the soil, the harvest and motherhood, and she ranked among the principal deities of Rome's ancient religion.

The myth

According to Hyginus, Tellus was born from Aether and Dies, together with Caelus, the sky, and the Sea. Augustine of Hippo, citing Virgil, presents her as the wife of Caelus. Her children include Saturn, Ops and Fama, and tradition also credits her with the Giants, born from her union with Tartarus.

In one of the few tales that are distinctly Roman, Tellus competed with Jupiter and Cura to create the first human being, and Saturn acted as judge. Tradition also tells that the goddess grew angry at the gods who refused to live on the earth, and she created the Titans, also called Giants, who tried to destroy the heavens by piling rocks on the mountains until Jupiter struck them down.

Varro ranked her above other goddesses such as Ops, Venus and Proserpina, and some philosophers identified Ops's three daughters with aspects of the Earth: Juno as the wife of the sky, Vesta as the common settlement and Ceres as nourishing soil. Ovid describes her as the place (locus) where growth happens, and Ceres as its cause.

Worship and religion

Romans called on her during earthquakes and in solemn oaths, when people turned their hands downward. Her temple stood in the Carinae district within the sacred boundary of the city, and it was raised after an earthquake struck during a battle. Together with Ceres she watched over the fertility of the fields. At the Fordicidia, on 15 April, pregnant cows were sacrificed under the direction of the Pontifex Maximus and the Vestal Virgins. The Sementivae in January honored both goddesses, and in private life offerings were made to her at sowing, at harvest and after the death of a family member.

Legacy

Terra gave its name to the planet Earth in the Romance languages. From Tellus come the adjective 'telluric' and the chemical element tellurium. Words such as 'terrigenous' and 'terrestrial' share her root. In Bavaria, the allegory Tellus Bavarica appears on coats of arms, paintings and statues.

Ancient sources: Hyginus, Varro, Ovid, Servius and Augustine of Hippo mention her, and her cult and festivals are described in texts from ancient Rome.

Trivia

From name to element. The chemical element tellurium takes its name from the Latin tellus, meaning Earth.
Two cows, two lives. According to tradition, Numa Pompilius was told in a dream to sacrifice two cows to Tellus. He read the message as a single pregnant heifer that yielded two lives, and so the Fordicidia was born.
