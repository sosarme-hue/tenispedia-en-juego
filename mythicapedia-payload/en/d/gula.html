Mesopotamian goddess of healing

Gula

Gula is the Mesopotamian goddess of healing, consort of the god Ninurta and one of the major deities of Babylonia. She matters because she combined the figure of the great physician with a cult that held a prominent place in several cities of the region.

The myth

According to Akkadian and Babylonian tradition, Gula, whose Sumerian name is usually understood as 'the great', was also known as 'the Great Mother' or 'Lady of Life'. The stories present her as a goddess who restored the breath of life, and after the Flood she was said to have helped give humanity a new 'breath of life'. Her usual companion was the sacred dog, which accompanied her as a messenger and as a symbol of healing.

Gula had seven children, among them Damu, god of healing and rebirth, and Ninazu, god of healing and of the underworld. Her functions overlapped with those of other goddesses such as Baba, Ninisina and Ninkarrak, with whom the texts equated her. Although they were originally independent beings, their qualities eventually became almost identical. Beyond her medical role, she was also invoked to curse those who trampled on the rights of rulers or who acted wickedly with poisons.

Worship and religion

Her main cult centre was Isin, where the sacred precinct of E-galmaj assigned her as consort to Pabilsag, the city's lord. Her worship was also prominent in Umma, Shirgulla and Nippur. In the Neo-Babylonian period, Nebuchadnezzar II mentions as many as three chapels within the E-zida complex at Borsippa, as well as a temple in her honour in Babylon. As protector of boundaries she appears on kudurru, inscribed boundary stones, represented by a statue, something that does not happen with other deities, who are often shown only through their shrines, sacred animals or weapons.

Ancient sources: The Gula Hymn of Bulluṭsa-rabi, which lists her identifications with other goddesses, was copied by Mesopotamian physicians during their formal training.

Trivia

A goddess with a statue. On kudurru stones, most deities appear only through symbols, while Gula is shown with a statue of her own.
A name meaning 'the great'. Her Sumerian name, gula, can be understood as 'the great', and in sources of the period it was also used as an epithet for other goddesses.
