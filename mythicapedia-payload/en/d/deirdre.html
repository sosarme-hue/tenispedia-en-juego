tragic heroine of Irish mythology

Deirdre

Deirdre, also called Deirdru, is the archetypal tragic heroine of Irish mythology, at the centre of the love story The Fate of the Sons of Usnech in the Ulster Cycle. According to American scholar James MacKillop, she is the best-known figure of Irish legend in modern times.

The myth

According to the tale, the druid Cathbad prophesied, at the birth of Deirdre, daughter of the bard Fedlimid mac Daill, that she would grow into a disturbingly beautiful woman whose beauty was cursed: kings and lords would go to war over her, and Ulster's three greatest warriors would be exiled because of her. King Conchobar, captivated, decided to raise her in seclusion under her nurse Leabharcham, intending to marry her himself.

One winter day Deirdre saw a raven feeding on blood spilled on the snow, and asked her nurse about a man with those colours. She learned of Naoise, son of Usnech, and the two fell in love. They fled to Scotland with his brothers Ardan and Ainle, where local kings tried to have them killed so they could claim her. Conchobar then offered a safe-conduct home, but it was a trap: on arrival at Emain Macha, Naoise and his brothers were slain and Conchobar took Deirdre.

After a year of forced marriage, Deirdre remained cold toward the king. When he asked whom she hated most besides himself, she named Éogan mac Durthacht, Naoise's killer, and Conchobar vowed to hand her over. On the journey he mocked her, and Deirdre threw herself from the chariot, dashing her head against a rock. Older and later versions differ on details, such as suicide versus death from grief.

Legacy

The story was hugely popular in Ireland and Scotland and survived in Scottish oral tradition. In the twentieth century, writers such as William Butler Yeats and John Millington Synge brought it to the stage, and Synge's play inspired many composers. Modern novels retell her story, and an Irish Naval Service ship was named after her.

Ancient sources: The oldest version survives in the Book of Leinster (c. 1160), within The Fate of the Sons of Usnech; it was later combined with other tales in The Three Sorrows of Storytelling.
