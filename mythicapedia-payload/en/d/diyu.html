realm of the dead in Chinese mythology

Diyu

Diyu, literally 'earth prison', is the Chinese underworld, where souls are judged and atone for their sins before being reborn. Its conception blends Buddhist ideas of hell, traditional Chinese beliefs about the afterlife and Taoist religion.

The myth

According to tradition, after death souls descend to a subterranean maze of chambers run by a celestial bureaucracy. At its head stands Yanluo Wang, the king of hell, a figure adapted from the Buddhist Yama, along with judges who assess each soul by its merits and faults in life.

Sources disagree on the number of levels. Some speak of three or four, others of ten courts, the most widely accepted figure, and some legends of eighteen hells. Each judge handles a different kind of offence, so murder and adultery, for example, fall under different courts. Punishments include being sawn in half, beheaded or crushed by boulders, and the stories present them as temporary, meant to purify.

Once the sentence is served and the soul has repented, it drinks Meng Po's Soup of Oblivion at the Bridge of Forgetfulness and is sent back to the world to be reborn.

Worship and religion

In Taoism, rituals such as the zhai (fasting) and the jiao (offerings) aim to shorten the suffering of souls. Fengdu, in Sichuan province, is regarded as the Taoist entrance to hell and is known as the City of the Dead.

Legacy

Fengdu and the temples on Mount Tai have been rebuilt as tourist attractions, with artistic depictions of hell and the afterlife. Taiwanese planchette novels such as Journeys to the Under-World have updated the punishments to include modern sins.
