humpbacked flute-playing fertility deity of the American Southwest

Kokopelli

Kokopelli is a fertility deity, also linked to music and communication, venerated by several Native American peoples of the Southwestern United States, especially the Hopi and Zuni. He is usually shown as a humpbacked flute player, and his figure appears carved and painted on rocks across the region.

The myth

In Hopi tradition, Kokopelli carries unborn children on his back and distributes them to women, which is why young girls sometimes fear him. He takes part in marriage rituals and is sometimes shown with a consort, Kokopelmimi. His flute announces the change from winter to spring, melts the snow and brings rain for the harvests. His hump is interpreted as a sack of seeds or of the songs he carries from village to village.

Other versions say his music made everyone dance and sing through the night, and that by morning the maidens were pregnant. Some local traditions present him as a goddess who met a traveller named Trully, meaning lone wolf, and had to leave; since then a wolf silhouette has been seen howling at the moon.

Worship and religion

Among the Hopi, Kokopelli appeared in ceremonies connected with maize and marriage rites, and a human dancer often embodied him during kachina ceremonies. Spanish missionaries persuaded Hopi craftsmen to frequently leave out the phallus in their images. Today he is still welcomed in many Native American homes.

Legacy

In modern versions, without the phallus, his image has become a symbol of the American Southwest and appears on t-shirts, caps, key-chains and patio decor. He is visible on the wall by the swimming pool at Walter White's home in several episodes of Breaking Bad, and a bicycle trail between Grand Junction and Moab bears his name.

Trivia

A visitor with news. In the Andes, the Ekeko figure played a similar role: he arrived with goods hanging from his body and announced a night of entertainment and trade.
Humpbacked water vessels. Between about 1200 and 1400, the Mississippian culture of the southeastern United States made water vessels shaped like a humpbacked woman, a similar figure.
