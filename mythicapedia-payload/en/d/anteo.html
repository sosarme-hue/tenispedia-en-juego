Antaeus

Antaeus is a giant from Greek mythology, son of Poseidon and Gaia, said to rule the desert interior of Libya. He is best known for his defeat at the hands of Heracles, which forms part of the hero's Labours.

The myth

According to the stories, Antaeus challenged every foreigner passing through his land to a wrestling match and killed them, since the aim of Greek wrestling was to throw the opponent to the ground. As long as he touched the earth, his mother Gaia renewed his strength, so he seemed unbeatable. He built a temple to his father Poseidon using the skulls of his victims.

Heracles reached Libya on his way to the garden of the Hesperides, which was his eleventh Labour. After knocking Antaeus down three times with no effect, he realised the giant regained strength from the ground. He lifted him clear of the earth and crushed him in a bear hug, cutting him off from his mother's breath.

Afterwards, Heracles is said to have joined Tinge (Tingis), Antaeus's widow, and their son Sophax gave his name to the city of Tingis, modern Tangier. Plutarch reports that the giant was buried in a mound there, and locals believed that digging into it would bring rain until the hole was filled. Other versions name a daughter, Iphinoe, who bore Heracles a son, Palaemon.

Worship and religion

The clearest religious link is the temple to Poseidon that Antaeus built, according to Pindar. His mound at Tingis was surrounded by beliefs about rain, but the sources describe no organised cult, and none is known to survive today.

Legacy

The wrestling contest between Heracles and Antaeus was a popular subject in ancient Greek art from the sixth century BC, and later in Renaissance sculpture. Francisco de Zurbarán painted it in 1634. The ancient city of Barca was also called Antapolis after the giant, and the Egyptian city of Tjebu was known in Greek as Antaeopolis.

Ancient sources: Pindar, Apollodorus (Bibliotheca), Philostratus and Plutarch mention Antaeus, and Pomponius Mela and Lucan refer to him in the Roman tradition.

Trivia

A city named after a widow. According to the myth, the city of Tingis, today Tangier, took its name from Tingis, Antaeus's widow, at the suggestion of her son Sophax.
Giant bones. According to Plutarch, the Roman general Sertorius had the tomb of Antaeus opened, found huge bones and ordered it covered with earth again.
