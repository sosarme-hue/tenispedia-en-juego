river of fire in the Greek underworld

Phlegethon

Phlegethon is one of the five rivers of Hades in Greek mythology, a river of fire tied to the world of the dead. Its burning image later passed into Western literature, especially Dante's Divine Comedy.

The myth

In Greek accounts, Phlegethon, also called Pyriphlegethon, flows through the infernal regions alongside Styx, Lethe, Cocytus and Acheron. According to Homer's Odyssey, it feeds into the Acheron, and it is considered less important than the Cocytus.

Ancient sources present it chiefly as a stream of fire. Plato describes it as a fiery current that coils round the earth and flows into the depths of Tartarus. In Orphic literature, which counts four rivers of the underworld, Phlegethon is linked with fire and with the east.

Dante turned it into a river of boiling blood in the Seventh Circle of Hell, where violence against one's fellow men is punished. Tyrants and murderers are submerged to a depth matching their cruelty, watched by centaurs who shoot arrows at anyone trying to rise above their level. There Dante and Virgil cross it with the help of the centaur Nessus.

Legacy

Phlegethon appears in Dante's Divine Comedy, where it becomes a river of boiling blood. In Seneca the Younger's tragedy Oedipus, a line of the chorus alludes to it to suggest that Death has already come to Thebes.

Ancient sources: Homer mentions it in the Odyssey, Plato in his dialogues, Virgil in the Aeneid and Seneca the Younger in his Oedipus, alongside Orphic literature.

Trivia

A river of blood. In the Divine Comedy Phlegethon is not made of fire but of boiling blood, in the Seventh Circle of Hell.
Four or five rivers. Orphic literature speaks of four rivers of the underworld, while other texts list five, with Phlegethon among them.
