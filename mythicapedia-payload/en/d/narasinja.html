man-lion avatar of Vishnu

Narasimha

Narasimha, the 'man-lion', is Vishnu's fourth avatar according to the Garuda Purana, though the Bhagavata Purana counts him as the fourteenth. A protective deity with a human body and a lion's head and claws, he is one of the most beloved gods in Hinduism.

The myth

According to myth, the demon Hiranyakashipu, elder brother of Hiranyaksha, whom Vishnu had killed in his boar avatar (Varaha), received a boon from Brahma that made him almost invulnerable: he could not be killed by man or beast, indoors or outdoors, by day or night, on earth or in the sky, or by any weapon. Emboldened, he persecuted Vishnu's devotees, including his own son Prahlada.

To get around the boon, Vishnu incarnated as Narasimha, neither man nor animal, and at twilight, on a palace threshold, placed the king on his lap and tore him apart with his claws. The story appears in seventeen Puranas with varying details. The Tamil poem Paripadal describes the god emerging from a broken pillar and wearing the king's entrails as a garland.

Scholars such as Deborah Soifer link this tale to a Vedic episode in which Indra kills the demon Namuci with a foam of water and a thunderbolt, under a pact forbidding death by 'neither palm nor fist', 'neither day nor night', 'neither dry nor wet'. The resemblance suggests continuity between the two stories, though this is a modern interpretation.

Worship and religion

Narasimha is one of the most popular deities in Hinduism and is worshipped by many Vaishnava groups, especially in southern India. He is revered as a protector who comes to the aid of devotees in times of need. Ahobilam in Andhra Pradesh is a major pilgrimage site where nine forms of the deity, the Nava Narasimha, are honoured. The festival Narasimha Jayanti, on the fourteenth day of the month of Vaishakha (April–May), commemorates his appearance. His worship remains alive today.

Legacy

His image persists in iconography, performance traditions and festivals across India, where he is honoured in temples and ceremonies.

Ancient sources: The Puranas, such as the Bhagavata Purana and the Vishnu Purana, the Narasimha Tapaniya Upanishad and the Tamil poem Paripadal.

Trivia

Three eyes. Some Puranas describe Narasimha with three eyes, like Shiva, and destroying with fire that comes from the third.
Nine forms. At Ahobilam the nine forms of the deity, the Nava Narasimha, are venerated.
