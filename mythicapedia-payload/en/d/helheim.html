realm of the dead in Norse mythology

Helheim

Helheim is the realm of the dead in Norse mythology, a cold and dark place where those who died of illness or old age were said to go. It is ruled by Hel, whose name it shares, and its exact place among the nine worlds of Yggdrasil varies from one source to another.

The myth

According to Norse sources, Helheim lay in the deepest and darkest part of Niflheim, one of the nine worlds supported by the world tree Yggdrasil. The sources do not fully agree. In the Prose Edda, Snorri Sturluson places Hel in Niflheim and says Odin made her ruler of the nine worlds, while other texts mention Niflhel as a neighbouring, icier place just outside Hel. Its entrance was watched by the dog Garmr, and an endless, nearly impassable river, the Gjöll, surrounded it, so that once someone had entered, not even the gods could easily leave.

The best-known stories concern the death of Baldr. In the Prose Edda, after Baldr dies, his brother Hermóðr rides for nine nights through valleys so dark that he sees nothing, until he reaches the Gjöll and its bridge, roofed with shining gold and guarded by Móðguð. Past the Gates of Hel he finds the hall and Baldr seated in a place of honour. Hel makes an offer, but it fails, and the gods later blame Loki for Baldr remaining in her realm. In the Poetic Edda, Odin rides to the edge of Hel to question a dead völva about Baldr's dreams.

Hel is not only a destination for gods. In the Poetic Edda, Brynhildr travels in a lavish cart along the road at the border of Hel after her death and meets a dead gýgr at a burial mound. Within Hel, the Nastrand, or 'corpse strand', is a hall for murderers, oath-breakers and notorious liars. Its walls are covered with serpents spitting venom that floods the hall, the sun never shines there, and its doors all face north.

Worship and religion

The surviving sources do not describe temples or festivals dedicated to Hel. Later Icelandic texts say that some of the dead were buried with items to help them on their journey to Hel, and the Poetic Edda uses the phrase 'arranging their journey to Hel' for funeral preparations. No organised cult of Hel is documented that has survived to the present day.

Legacy

The English word hell comes from the same Germanic root as Hel. Its cognates in other Germanic languages, and the related word hall, link it to Valhalla, the hall of the slain. In Christian belief, the Nastrand has been compared to hell.

Ancient sources: The Poetic Edda (Völuspá, Grímnismál, Baldrs draumar, Helreið Brynhildar and Guðrúnarkviða I) and Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda, especially Gylfaginning.

Trivia

From Hel to 'hell'. The English word hell shares a Germanic root with Hel. Its relative hall, meaning 'great room', is also related to Valhalla.
A bridge of gold. The bridge over the river Gjöll had a roof of shining gold. Its guardian, Móðguð, remarked that it echoed more loudly under Hermóðr than under the five who had just crossed it.
