eponymous hero of the Latins, in Greek and Roman myth

Latinus

Latinus is the eponymous hero of the Latins, a figure found in both Greek and Roman tradition. His genealogy changes from author to author, and he is best known as the king of the Aborigines of Latium in Virgil's Aeneid, where he is tied to the arrival of Aeneas in Italy.

The myth

In Hesiod's Theogony, Latinus was the son of Odysseus and Circe, and ruled the Tyrrhenians with his brothers Agrius and Telegonus on distant islands. Other authors make him a son of Odysseus and the nymph Calypso, or of Telemachus. One source credits him with giving his name to the Latin language. Lydus, for his part, presents him as the brother of Graecus, with a common origin in Zeus and Pandora.

In Roman tradition, Latinus, also called Lavinius, is king of the Aborigines, with his court at Laurentum, the city of laurels. One version makes him the son of the prophet Faunus and the nymph Marica; another makes him the son of Heracles and Palanto, a Hyperborean young woman whom the hero left in Faunus's care. His wife is Amata, and his daughter is Lavinia.

When Aeneas arrives with the exiled Trojans, the versions diverge. In one, Latinus welcomes them, grants them land and offers Lavinia in marriage; after Trojan raids he allies with Turnus, and both die in the final battle, after which Aeneas takes the throne and the two peoples adopt the name Latins. In another, Latinus fights Aeneas until the two agree an alliance in dreams, and Aeneas marries Lavinia. In the Aeneid, Latinus accepts the oracle that calls for his daughter to marry the foreigner, but Amata and Turnus push him toward war, and it is Latinus who seeks peace after Turnus dies.

A legend says Latinus vanished in a battle against Mezentius, king of Caere, and became the god Jupiter Latiaris.

Worship and religion

In historical times, the Latin League worshipped Jupiter Latiaris on Monte Cavo, overlooking Lake Nemi.

Legacy

The name of the Latins and their language is traced, in some versions, to Latinus as their eponymous ancestor.

Ancient sources: Hesiod mentions him in the Theogony, Virgil in the Aeneid, and Dionysius of Halicarnassus and Lydus record other versions.
