Roman god of fields and woods

Silvanus

Silvanus (from Latin silva, "wood") was a Roman tutelary deity of woods and uncultivated lands. He also watched over cultivated fields and their boundaries, and so stood for the bond between farmers, herders and the land they worked.

The myth

According to Roman tradition, Silvanus guarded the boundaries of fields. Higinio Gromatico, a Roman surveyor, claimed that he was the first to set up stones to mark them. Every estate was said to have three Silvani: a domesticus, an agrestis worshipped by shepherds, and an orientalis who presided over the point where the estate began. For this reason he was often called upon in the plural, as Silvani.

Virgil tells the myth of Cyparissus. Silvanus, or Apollo in other versions, loved the young man, who had a pet hind. Silvanus killed it by accident, Cyparissus died of grief and was turned into a cypress, and that is why Silvanus is shown carrying a cypress trunk.

He also protected herds from wolves and promoted their fertility. As a god of forests and pastures he loved music, and the syrinx was sacred to him. Poets pictured him as a cheerful, fertile old man who was in love with Pomona.

Worship and religion

Offerings to him included grapes, ears of grain, milk, meat, wine and pigs. In Cato's De Agricultura, Mars Silvanus receives an offering to safeguard the health of cattle. The same text says that his link with farming concerned only men's labour, and women were excluded from his worship, a point compared with the rites of Bona Dea. The sources say nothing about a surviving cult today.

Legacy

In Latin poetry and art he always appears as a cheerful old man in love with Pomona. Edmund Spenser included him in Canto VI of Book I of The Faerie Queene (1590-96), where his wild woodgods rescue Una from danger in the forest. Later scholars identified him with Pan, Faunus, Inuus and Aegipan, and in Gaul, Britain and Spain his name was merged with many local gods.

Ancient sources: Virgil, who links him with the myth of Cyparissus and with the Tyrrhenian Pelasgians, and Cato the Elder, who calls him Mars Silvanus in De Agricultura.

Trivia

Three Silvani per estate. According to Higinio Gromatico, each estate had three Silvani: one for the household, one for the shepherds and one for the point where the property began.
A god of many names. In the provinces, Silvanus was identified with local gods such as Sucellos in Gaul and Callirius in Britain, a link visible in many inscriptions.
