Greek heroine, mother of the Aloadae

Iphimedeia

Iphimedeia is a Thessalian princess of Greek mythology, mother of the giants known as the Aloadae and, in some accounts, of the young Pancratis. Her story, passed down through several ancient sources, places her among the most distinctive female figures of the world of the dead.

The myth

According to the tradition, Iphimedeia was the daughter of King Triopas of Thessaly and was married to her uncle Aloeus. Falling in love with Poseidon, she would walk to the seashore and pour the sea water into her lap until the god came to her. From this union came the twins Otus and Ephialtes, called the Aloadae after their stepfather Aloeus, who grew at an astonishing rate and reached gigantic size within a few years.

Another episode tells how Iphimedeia and her daughter Pancratis were taking part in a celebration of Dionysus near Mount Drius when they were seized by Thracian companions of King Butes and taken to Naxos. There both were given to men of the king's circle. On Aloeus's orders, the Aloadae defeated the Thracians, destroyed the city and rescued their mother and sister, although Pancratis died not long afterwards. In the Odyssey, Odysseus meets her spirit at the entrance to the Underworld, among other heroines.

Worship and religion

Iphimedeia was honoured as a heroine in Mylasa, in Caria, where she received her own cult. At Anthedon, in Boeotia, her tomb was shown alongside those of her sons. Her name also appears in Mycenaean tablets written in Linear B, which suggests a very early presence in the Greek tradition. No living cult is known.

Legacy

Pausanias mentions a painting of Iphimedeia by Polygnotus. In modern interpretations, the scholar Pietro Scarpi places her in the chthonic sphere as a double of Hecate.

Ancient sources: Homer's Odyssey, the Catalogue of Women, the Library of Apollodorus, Ovid, Hyginus and Pausanias all mention her, with versions that differ on the father of the twins and on the place of her encounter with Poseidon.

Trivia

A shade in the Underworld. Odysseus meets her among the shades of heroines when he descends to Hades, alongside other female figures of Homer's account.
Mycenaean writing. Her name appears on tablets from Pylos written in Linear B, one of the oldest pieces of evidence for the Greek tradition.
