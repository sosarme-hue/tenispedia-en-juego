Roman personification of destiny

Fatum

Fatum, known in Spanish as Hado, is the personification of destiny in Roman mythology, the idea that what has been written cannot be avoided. It shares ground with the Greek Ananke and Moira and with the Roman goddess Fortuna.

The myth

The texts that mention Fatum are few, and they do not always agree. In some sources it appears as an interpretatio graeca of the Greek god Moros (Μόρος), who was imagined as a son of Night (Nox) and Erebus (Erebus).

Cicero understood it differently. He linked it to the Greek himarmene and described it as the order of a chain of causes, in which each cause produces an effect that in turn determines what comes next. For him, the events of the past are those of the present and will be those of the future.

Legacy

The Latin word fatum gave rise to the Spanish word hado. It also named the fata Sibyllina, the Sibylline oracles, which were regarded as predictions of what had been written.

Ancient sources: Cicero analyses it as an unavoidable force and a divine personification, and some ancient texts identify it with the Greek god Moros.

Trivia

Sibylline oracles. The fata Sibyllina were the Sibylline oracles. In this way, the word fatum was associated with prophecy as well as with destiny.
Relatives in the language. Fas, the divine law, belongs to the same word family as fatum, and both terms relate to destiny.
