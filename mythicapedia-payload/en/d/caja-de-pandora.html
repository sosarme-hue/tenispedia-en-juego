mythical jar of Greek mythology

Pandora's box

Pandora's box is a mythical container from Greek mythology which, when opened, released all evils into the human world and left only hope inside. The story comes mainly from Hesiod and is one of the best-known images in Western culture.

The myth

According to Hesiod in Works and Days, Prometheus stole fire from the gods to give it to humans. Zeus, seeking revenge, ordered Hephaestus to shape Pandora, the first woman, and sent her to Epimetheus, Prometheus' brother, who married her.

Pandora was given a jar she was told never to open. Driven by curiosity, she lifted the lid, and harsh toil, grievous sicknesses and many other evils escaped into the world. When she managed to close it, only hope (elpis) remained at the bottom. Hesiod calls Pandora a «beautiful evil» (kalòn kakòn). Other ancient texts differ: the Iliad mentions two jars on Zeus's threshold, one of evils and one of blessings; Theognis of Megara describes a jar of blessings; and a fable by Babrius, number 312 in the Perry Index, blames a foolish man, with hope promising the return of the lost goods.

Scholars still debate whether hope is a blessing kept from humans or one more evil. The Greek word can mean expectation of good or expectation of evil. Nietzsche, in Human, All Too Human, argued that hope prolongs human suffering.

Legacy

The phrase «to open a Pandora's box» means to start something that causes many unforeseen problems, and its colloquial equivalent is «to open a can of worms». The story has inspired works such as John William Waterhouse's painting (1896) and James Gillray's political cartoon (1809). It appears in songs by Nach, El Cuarteto de Nos and Tierra Santa. The word «box» comes from Erasmus of Rotterdam, who in his Adagia (1508) replaced pithos with pyxis, meaning box.

Ancient sources: Hesiod, Works and Days (c. 700 BC); also Homer's Iliad and references by Theognis of Megara and Babrius.

Trivia

A jar, not a box. The Greek word pithos meant a large storage jar for wine, oil or grain. Erasmus of Rotterdam changed it to pyxis, meaning box, in his Latin version of the story.
A beautiful evil. Hesiod called Pandora kalòn kakòn, a beautiful evil, a phrase that shows how the poet saw the gift of the first woman.
