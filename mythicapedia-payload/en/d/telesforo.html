minor healing god of Greek religion

Telesphorus

Telesphorus was a minor god of healing, linked to children and to recovery from illness. According to tradition he was a son of Asclepius and often accompanied his sister Hygieia; his name, Telesphoros, means roughly "the accomplisher", and some scholars think he had Celtic origins. According to tradition, Telesphorus was the youngest son of Asclepius, the god of medicine, and he is often shown beside his sister Hygieia in statues and inscriptions. He was depicted as a dwarf whose head was always covered by a cowl or Phrygian cap. Ancient sources give him different names depending on the place: in Pergamon he was Telesphorus, in Epidaurus Acesis ("cure"), and in Titane Euamerion, as Pausanias records. The texts do not credit him with great deeds. His role was to protect children, and some ancient hymns thank him for guarding the birth of healthy offspring. Many scholars believe his protection extended even to death, as suggested by statuettes found in children's graves at Stobi.

The myth

Worship and religion

His cult grew at Pergamon, within the great sanctuary of Asclepius, where according to Aristides a temple was dedicated to him alongside Hygieia. From there it spread to Epidaurus and to regions such as Athens, Attica and Thessaly. No living cult survives today.

Legacy

He appears on bronze provincial coins struck under Caracalla in the early third century, sometimes alongside Asclepius and Salus, the Roman equivalent of Hygieia. His presence in the sources is modest, mainly limited to these coins and the ancient statuettes.

Ancient sources: Pausanias is the only known ancient mention of Telesphorus, placing him in the sanctuary of Asclepius at Titane; a second-century AD Attic inscription also names him as a son of Asclepius.

Trivia

A Phrygian cap. His head was always shown covered by a cowl or Phrygian cap, a feature that sets him apart in images.
Figurines in children's graves. Two children's graves at Stobi, dated to the second century BC, held statuettes of Telesphorus together with a child.
A coin with Caracalla. A coin struck in Lydia around AD 210 shows Caracalla consulting Asclepius, Telesphorus and Salus.
