Heracles

Heracles, known to the Romans as Hercules, is the most famous hero of Greek mythology. The son of Zeus and the mortal Alcmene, he was known for his superhuman strength, his twelve labours and his passage from hero to god, which made him the paradigm of the Greek hero.

The myth

According to the most widespread tradition, Zeus fathered Heracles with Alcmene, and the child was called Alcaeus or Alcides at birth. His foster father was Amphitryon. From infancy he suffered the enmity of Hera, the wife of Zeus. In one version, the goddess drove him mad, and in that frenzy he killed his wife Megara and their children. To atone, he served Eurystheus, king of Argos, who set him the famous twelve labours: killing the Nemean lion, slaying the Lernaean Hydra, stealing the golden apples of the Hesperides and capturing Cerberus.

The stories about Heracles do not form a neat chronological sequence. Beside the labours there are independent exploits, such as his voyage with the Argonauts, the sack of Troy in the generation before the Trojan War and the freeing of Prometheus, along with side adventures that occurred during his tasks. After a period of servitude to Omphale, queen of Lydia, he married several times and fathered many children, and his descendants were called the Heracleidae. Tradition also records several male lovers, including Iolaus and Hylas.

His death has its own version. His wife Deianira, deceived by the dying centaur Nessus, gave him a garment smeared with poisoned blood. Unable to remove it, the hero had a pyre built on Mount Oeta, where he immolated himself and left his mortal part behind. He ascended to Olympus as a god and married the goddess Hebe. The Odyssey offers a striking detail: in Hades, Odysseus meets a shade of Heracles, while his divine part dwells among the gods.

Worship and religion

In Greece Heracles received a double cult: as a hero, with libations, and as a god, with temples and sacrifices, sometimes within the same festival. Pindar called him hērōs theós, the divine hero. Remarkably, no tomb of his was ever identified. He was worshipped as a patron of young men, gymnasia and military training. The Heracleia, a festival commemorating his death, was held on the second day of Metageitnion. His cult spread largely through the influence of Sparta, whose kings claimed descent from him, and it absorbed local divine figures. By the 4th century BCE he was identified with the Phoenician god Melqart. The sources do not document a living cult today.

Legacy

In Rome he became Hercules, and emperors such as Commodus and Maximian identified themselves with him. His figure has inspired Western art for centuries, from the frieze of the temple of Apollo at Delphi to paintings in the Prado Museum, and he remains a frequent subject of popular culture. Several cities called Heraclea were named in his honour, and according to Greek legend he founded Herculaneum in Italy.

Ancient sources: Homer and Hesiod mention him in the earliest works, while Pindar, Herodotus, Apollodorus, Diodorus Siculus and Pausanias offer later testimony; of the epics by Peisinous, Pisander and Panyassis only fragments survive.

Trivia

An island named after a swim. A very small island near Lemnos was called Neai, from a Greek verb meaning to swim, because Heracles was said to have swum there.
A god of locusts. The people of Oeta worshipped him as Cornopion, because he helped them rid their land of locusts, which they called cornopes.
The nose-cutting statue. Pausanias reports a statue of Heracles at Thebes called Nose-docker, because the Thebans said he cut off the noses of heralds sent from Orchomenus to demand tribute.
