Egyptian symbol of the god Amun

Horns of Ammon

The Horns of Ammon are curling ram's horns that symbolised the Egyptian god Amun, and later came to depict kings and deities as well. Their shape gave the name to ammonites, extinct cephalopod fossils.

The myth

Amun was often shown with ram's horns, and as the god became a symbol of power, pharaohs and rulers of other peoples were also depicted with them. After Rome conquered Egypt, Jupiter was sometimes shown as "Jupiter Ammon", wearing the same horns.

Alexander the Great received the title "Son of Ammon" from the oracle at Siwa, and later tradition linked his horned portraits to the Quranic figure Dhu al-Qarnayn, "The Two-Horned One", an identification most scholars accept.

Legacy

Pliny the Elder was among the first to call spiral fossil shells "horns of Ammon". In the nineteenth century, the palaeontologist Karl Alfred von Zittel named the class Ammonoidea (1848), from which the term ammonite derives.

Ancient sources: Pliny the Elder, in his Natural History, calls them "horns of Ammon".

Trivia

A shell for a horn. Ammonite fossils are rare in Egypt, so the association may have begun with fossil snail shells found in Mokattam limestone near Cairo.
From fossil to name. Medieval writers such as Georgius Agricola and Conrad Gesner directly linked these horns to fossil cephalopod shells.
