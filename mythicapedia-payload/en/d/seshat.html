ancient Egyptian goddess of writing, time and surveying

Seshat

Seshat was the ancient Egyptian goddess of record-keeping, time, and surveying. She was regarded as the patron of scribes and libraries, and her role in laying out sacred buildings linked her to the founding of temples.

The myth

According to tradition, Seshat was the "Lady of Books" and the keeper of the pharaoh's life record. She was pictured seated at the foot of the cosmic tree, in the deepest part of the south of the sky, where the upper and lower heavens met. From there she wrote future events on the tree's leaves and archived past ones, and she was said to measure time and oversee the calendar and astronomy.

Her link to the pharaoh was central. She recorded his regnal years, his jubilees, and the names of his children on a palm rib or on the sacred ished tree, which at Heliopolis was identified with the persea. Some sources add that she could influence the length of the king's life and guaranteed his immortality. When a new pharaoh was crowned, she noted his royal title and his speech, sometimes with Thoth's help.

In the "stretching the cord" ritual she marked out the foundations of temples beside the king, aligning them with stars and geographic features. The ancient texts do not give her charge of the building work, which belonged to gods such as Ptah. Her relationship with Thoth varied by source: she is sometimes his consort, sometimes his daughter or feminine counterpart, and some texts identify her with Nephthys or Isis.

Worship and religion

Seshat's cult was small and faded over time. She had priests of her own, but they gradually disappeared, and the last pharaoh recorded as making offerings to her was Senusret I. She seems to have had no temple of her own and no popular following; her role was felt mainly in court rituals and in the world of scribes and Houses of Life.

Legacy

Seshat is still one of the most recognisable figures of Egyptian religion thanks to her iconography: a woman with a seven-pointed emblem above her head, a scribe's palette, a leopard skin, and a palm rib ending in a tadpole. Her name has been taken up in popular culture, in video games and in fiction about libraries and ancient knowledge.

Ancient sources: She appears in the Pyramid Texts, on the Palermo Stone, and in many temple and tomb inscriptions, such as those at Edfu.

Trivia

An enigmatic emblem. The emblem she wears on her head is described as a flower, a star, or a rosette beneath a crescent, and its exact meaning remains unclear.
More accountant than scribe. Although linked to writing, some scholars think she was more like an accountant than a scribe, since she was tied to notching and carving marks in stone and wood.
