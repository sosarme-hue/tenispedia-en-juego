Gaulish god of farming, forests and strong drink

Sucellus

Sucellus (also Sucellos, 'the good striker') was a god worshipped in Roman Gaul and neighbouring provinces, linked to farming, woodlands, fertility and alcoholic drinks. He matters because he is one of the few Gaulish deities known by name, although no written myths about him survive.

The myth

No classical text recounts his deeds: his name appears only in Latin inscriptions and in images. He is shown as a bearded, mature man in Gaulish dress, with a short belted tunic, cloak and hood, holding a long-handled hammer in one hand and a round-bellied pot in the other. Scholars suggest the hammer could bring frosts that ruined crops or reward the fields with good harvests and fair weather.

He often appears beside the goddess Nantosuelta, with whom he is paired on several monuments, including the altar found near Sarrebourg, where she holds a small house on a pole. He is sometimes accompanied by a dog, occasionally with three heads, or by a raven. His resemblance to the Roman god Silvanus led to his identification with that deity in some dedications. Some scholars link him to fertility and the grape harvest, others to the underworld, based on the three-headed dog and the hammer, which some compare to the Etruscan death demon Charun.

Worship and religion

About ten votive inscriptions are known, mostly from Gaul, with a few from the Rhineland, Britain and the Danube. His cult was concentrated in the valleys of the Rhône and Saône, and those who dedicated altars to him were generally ordinary people. He was also believed to protect springs in Burgundy. There is no evidence that his worship survived beyond the Roman period.

Ancient sources: He is absent from classical literature and is known from Latin inscriptions and numerous images from Gaul.

Trivia

The good striker. The name combines the Gaulish prefix su- ('good') with a root meaning 'to strike', which links it directly to his hammer.
A three-headed dog. On some monuments the dog accompanying the god has three heads, as at Ober-Seebach in Alsace.
