Roman goddess of the oven and of bakers

Fornax

In Roman religion, Fornax was the divine personification of the oven (fornax) and the patroness of bakers. People prayed to her so that the heat of the oven would not burn the grain or the bread.

The myth

Accounts of Fornax are scarce, and they survive mainly through the explanation of her festival. According to tradition, the goddess was conceived to account for the Fornacalia, held to toast the spelt (far) used to bake sacrificial cakes. Some accounts hold that the Romans first offered the produce of the land to Ceres, but ended up toasting the grain in the oven or the hearth.

The festival fell on 17 February, although other authors place it on a movable date in mid-February, set by the curio maximus. Each curia announced its day of sacrifice in the Forum, and for peasants who could not read it was their last chance to celebrate. Anyone who forgot the festival was called stulti, fools, and had to make an offering in the so-called Feast of Fools.

Worship and religion

Her worship was tied to the curiae, the most ancient divisions of the city, traditionally made by Romulus. The Fornacalia was the second of two festivals involving the curiae, the other being the Fordicidia on 19 April. Over time her role was merged with that of Vesta. The sources do not say that her cult survived beyond antiquity.

Ancient sources: Ovid, Fasti II; Festus, De significatu verborum, under Fornacalia; Plutarch, Roman Questions 89.

Trivia

Feast of Fools. Anyone who forgot which curia they belonged to and missed the Fornacalia was called a fool and had to make a special offering.
Fordicidia. The Fornacalia was the second festival involving the curiae; the other, the Fordicidia, was held on 19 April.
