Māori god of wind and storms

Tāwhirimātea

Tāwhirimātea is the Māori god of weather, controlling wind, thunder, lightning, clouds and storms. His story explains how the sky and earth were separated and why the sea, forests and food sources were divided among his brothers.

The myth

In Māori tradition, Tāwhirimātea is a son of Ranginui, the sky father, and Papatūānuku, the earth mother. He is the seventh of their seventy children, all of them sons, and he lives in the sky with his father and his brother, the star Rehua.

The parents lay in an eternal embrace, and their sons grew weary of the darkness between them. Tūmatauenga proposed killing their parents, but Tāne preferred to separate them, pushing them apart with his head on the earth and his feet on the sky. Tāwhirimātea was enraged, seeing this as a bid to seize his realm, so he persuaded his father to join a war against his siblings. He gathered an army of winds, clouds and rains and first attacked Tāne, destroying his forests. He then punished Tangaroa, the sea god, whose children fled to Tāne. He pursued Rongo and Haumia, who hid in their mother's bosom, and finally faced Tūmatauenga, who held firm by planting his feet in the earth. Neither could prevail, and Tāwhirimātea withdrew. As punishment for their cowardice, Tūmatauenga invented hunting, fishing, farming and other arts to subdue his brothers' domains as food for humans. According to the myth, the conflict continues, and the flooding of much of the land is attributed to Tāwhirimātea's rains.
