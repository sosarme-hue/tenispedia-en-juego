divine elephant, mount of Indra

Airavata

Airavata is the white divine elephant of Hindu mythology, mount of the god Indra and king of elephants. His story links the power of the sky with water and rain, and he appears in texts, temples and national symbols in Thailand and Laos.

The myth

According to the Ramayana, Airavata was the son of Iravati, while the Vishnu Purana says he emerged from the churning of the ocean of milk. The Matangalila tells that he was born when Brahma sang hymns over the two halves of the eggshell from which Garuda would hatch: Airavata came out of the right half, followed by seven more males, and eight females came from the left. These sixteen became ancestors of earthly and heavenly elephants and the diggajas who support the universe in the eight directions.

Prithu made him king of all elephants. His consort Abhramu, whose name means "the one who knits or binds the clouds", reflects the belief that elephants can produce rain clouds. Airavata is the chief of the guardian elephants of the cardinal points and stands at the entrance to Svarga, Indra's palace. His best-known episode is Indra's battle against Vritra: the elephant reaches with his trunk into the watery underworld, draws up water and sprays it into the clouds, so that Indra brings sweet rain.

In another story, the sage Durvasa gave a garland to Indra, who passed it to Airavata; the elephant threw it to the ground, and the sage cursed the gods to age and die. Airavata was destroyed by the curse, but later emerged from the ocean when it was churned again to recover lost power.

Worship and religion

At Darasuram near Tanjore, tradition holds that Airavata worshipped a lingam of Shiva; the lingam of the temple is named Airavateshwara after him. The temple was built by Rajaraja Chola II (1146-1173) and is today a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Legacy

The Thai name Erawan refers to a three-headed elephant often shown with Indra, which became a symbol of Bangkok. It appeared on the royal flag of Laos, in the Megami Tensei video game series and in the name of a Karnataka bus service.

Ancient sources: The Ramayana, the Mahabharata, the Bhagavad Gita, the Vishnu Purana and the Matangalila mention Airavata.

Trivia

Seven trunks. The sources describe Airavata with several trunks and tusks, though the number varies by text, from three heads to seven trunks.
Elephant of the clouds. One of his names, Abhra-matanga, means "elephant of the clouds", linked to the belief that elephants can produce rain.
