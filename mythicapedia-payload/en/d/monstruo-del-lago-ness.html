legendary creature of Scottish folklore

Loch Ness Monster

The Loch Ness Monster, affectionately known as Nessie, is a mythical creature said to inhabit Loch Ness in the Scottish Highlands. It is one of the most famous figures in cryptozoology and a modern staple of Scottish folklore.

The myth

Accounts usually describe Nessie as a large, long-necked creature with one or more humps rising from the water. Believers point to a 7th-century text, Adomnán's Life of St Columba, in which the Irish saint supposedly halted a "water beast" attacking a man in the River Ness. Many scholars doubt the link, since water-beast stories were common in medieval saints' lives, and they see no real connection to the modern monster. Some also mention kelpies, the water-horses of Scottish legend, though these lack the monster's typical features.

The modern myth took shape in 1933. That year, an Inverness Courier article described an enormous creature rolling in the loch, and the press soon adopted the name "Loch Ness monster". Shortly afterwards, George Spicer and his wife reported a long-necked creature crossing a road, which they compared to a dragon or a prehistoric animal. Researchers suggest his account may have been inspired by the film King Kong, then hugely popular in London. In 1934 the famous "surgeon's photograph" spread around the world, and it was exposed as a hoax in 1994.

Legacy

Nessie is one of the best-known figures of cryptozoology, alongside Bigfoot and the Yeti. The creature has drawn tourists to the loch and appears often in popular culture, from films and books to merchandise and the imagery linking Scotland with mystery.

Ancient sources: The oldest text linked to the loch is Adomnán's Life of St Columba, written in the 7th century.

Trivia

The surgeon's photograph. The 1934 photograph showing a long neck above the water was attributed to a surgeon, R. K. Wilson. In 1994 Marmaduke Wetherell's son-in-law claimed it was a fake.
A name from the press. The name "Loch Ness Monster" spread after the 1933 articles in the Inverness Courier, and the nickname Nessie has been used since the 1940s.
