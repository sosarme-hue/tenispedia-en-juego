minor Roman god of waters and the warm southeast wind

Volturnus

Volturnus is a minor god of Roman mythology, linked to waters and rivers and to the warm southeast wind. His archaic cult and his own flamen show that he had a place in the Roman religious calendar, and his name reflects how a single word could designate a river, a wind and a deity.

The myth

According to tradition, Volturnus was the god of the Tiber and perhaps of all rivers. Varro connected his name with the verb volvere (to turn), an idea that suggests a personification of the movement of waters or wind. Festus identifies him with the southeast wind that the Greeks called Euronotus, and Aulus Gellius with the river of the same name in Campania.

In later genealogies he appears as the father of the nymph Juturna, showing how ancient functional deities were folded into later mythological schemes. The sources offer no long narrative of his deeds; he is known mainly through the functions he fulfilled.

Worship and religion

His cult was marked by the Volturnalia, a festival held on 27 August and overseen by a minor priest of his own, the Flamen Volturnalis. According to some accounts he was originally Etruscan, and his worship spread to Rome, where it may have replaced or coincided with that of the Tiber. No living cult survives today.

Legacy

His legacy is mainly religious and linguistic. Kurt Latte suggested that his name might derive from the Etruscan personal name Velthurna. Horace and Columella recall the warm wind that could damage vines in Baetica if they were not protected. Today a lake in Antarctica bears his name.

Ancient sources: Varro, Festus, Aulus Gellius, Horace and Columella mention him in their works, and the Roman religious calendar records his festival.

Trivia

An Antarctic lake. A lake in Antarctica is named after this god, a modern reminder of a deity who has almost been forgotten.
The Greek wind. Festus linked Volturnus with the wind that the Greeks called Euronotus, that is, the southeast wind.
Protected vineyards. Columella describes how the Volturnus wind, during the Dog Days, could damage grapes in Baetica unless they were covered.
