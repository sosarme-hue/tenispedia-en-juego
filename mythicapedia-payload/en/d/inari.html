Japanese deity of rice, agriculture and prosperity

Inari

Inari is the Japanese kami of rice, agriculture, fertility and prosperity, and one of the principal deities of Shinto. Its best-known messengers are the white foxes, or kitsune, which are often mistaken for the deity itself.

The myth

According to tradition, Inari arrived in Japan at the time of its creation, during a devastating famine. The deity descended from heaven riding a white fox and carrying sheaves of grain. That grain was not rice but a cereal that grows in swamps, because in ancient times Japan was mostly water and marshland. Foxes roaming the paddies may have inspired the belief that they watched over the crops, and farmers left them red rice and fried tofu as a sign of thanks.

Inari's image varies. It appears as a young woman, as an old man carrying a load of rice, or as an androgynous bodhisattva. Some scholars identify Inari with Ukanomitama or Ōgetsu-hime, who appear in the Kojiki; others link it to Toyouke, or to the Buddhist Dakiniten, who rides a flying white fox. The number of kami also changed: Inari was worshipped as three deities (Inari sanza) and, from the Kamakura period, as five (Inari goza), with different names depending on the shrine.

Worship and religion

The main shrine, Fushimi Inari-taisha, sits on the slopes of Inari Mountain in Kyoto, and its official founding date is 711. It received imperial rank in the Heian period and became an imperial pilgrimage site in 1072. Destroyed by fire during the Ōnin War in 1468, it was rebuilt by around 1499. In the Edo period, worship spread across Japan, helped along by the daimyō. Inari became patron of blacksmiths, protector of warriors, fishermen and actors, and a deity of luck and prosperity. People prayed to it for health, money or a pregnancy. After the government ordered Buddhism and Shinto separated, many shrines changed, but devotion continues: in 1985, 32,000 Inari chapels were counted, more than a third of the Shinto chapels in Japan.

Legacy

Inari also left a mark on the world of business. In the Tokugawa period, when money replaced rice as the measure of wealth, its role extended to finance and commerce, and modern firms such as the cosmetics company Shiseido still keep Inari shrines atop their headquarters. The fox is one of the most recognizable images in Japanese folklore.

Ancient sources: The Kojiki mentions Ukanomitama and Ōgetsu-hime, figures some scholars link with Inari, and the Nijūni shaki lists three guardian kami associated with it.

Trivia

Coins with foxes. Early in the century, followers of Inari in Ginza minted coins as offerings. They bore images of two foxes and a jewel, symbols of long life and good luck.
A saying from Osaka. A popular saying from Osaka went: for illness, pray to Kōbō; for wishes, pray to Inari. Over time Inari was credited with curing coughs, toothaches and broken bones.
