völva of Norse mythology

Gróa

Gróa is a völva in Norse mythology, a practitioner of seiðr and the wife of Aurvandil. She matters mainly for two stories: Thor's battle with the jötunn Hrungnir and the funeral poem Grógaldr, where she appears as a woman who can still help from beyond the grave.

The myth

In the Prose Edda, after Thor defeated Hrungnir with the hammer Mjölnir, shards of the giant's whetstone remained lodged in the god's head. Gróa was summoned to remove them with a spell. While she worked, Thor, distracted, told her how he had helped her husband Aurvandil cross the river Élivágar and saved his life by snapping off a frost-bitten toe. The news startled her, the spell failed, and the fragments stayed in Thor's head forever.

In the Grógaldr, a poem from the Poetic Edda that may be a late seventeenth-century imitation, her son Svipdag wakes her at her burial mound by calling her name. Gróa, who has lost none of her powers, recites nine charms to protect him from the stepmother who sets him trials: they calm storms at sea, dull enemy weapons, and free him from fetters.

Ancient sources: The Prose Edda (Skáldskaparmál) and the Grógaldr in the Poetic Edda; Saxo's Gesta Danorum mentions a Gro who may be the same figure.

Trivia

A stone in the head. Shards of Hrungnir's whetstone stayed lodged permanently in Thor's head, according to the Prose Edda.
Nine charms. In the Grógaldr, Gróa recites nine charms for her son, including one that frees him from shackles if he breathes on the restraints.
