form of the Hindu god Shiva, destroyer of Tripura

Tripurantaka

Tripurantaka, "destroyer of Tripura", is a form of the Hindu god Shiva featured in the Puranas. His story, the destruction of three asura cities with a single arrow, is one of the best-known episodes of his figure in Hinduism.

The myth

According to the Padma Purana, the asura Taraka had three sons: Tarakaksha, Kamalaksha (also called Virayavana) and Vidyunmali. After their father was killed by Shiva's son Kartikeya, the asura princes performed penance to win the favor of Brahma, who granted them a boon. Since immortality was refused, they asked for three indestructible cities, which only Shiva could destroy when the Pushya Nakshatra was conjoined with the Moon and the cities were aligned in a straight line.

The architect Mayasura built the three cities: one of iron in hell, one of silver on Earth and one of gold in heaven. The asuras settled there, but soon they oppressed the good once more. Brahma directed the gods to Shiva, and in the war Nandi defeated Vidyunmali, though the fallen revived thanks to the pool of Tripura. When the time came, Shiva's chariot had the Earth as its body, the Sun and Moon as wheels and Brahma as charioteer; the bow was Mount Meru and the arrow was Vishnu. When he released it, the three cities burst into flames. Mayasura escaped because Nandi warned him in time. Afterwards, Shiva danced the tandava upon the ruins.

Worship and religion

Tripurantaka is venerated especially on Kartik Purnima, the full moon of the month of Kartika, and also during Maha Shivaratri. Devotees offer prasad and prayers, particularly on the day of the Rudra yajna. Images of him are enshrined at Tiruvatikai, near Chidambaram, and at Tiruvirkolam (Koovum), near Chennai, and he remains an object of devotion.

Legacy

His iconography, with Shiva as a four-armed archer, developed in Indian temple art from Pattadakal and Ellora to Hampi. Hindu art depicts him as a figure of light dispelling darkness.

Ancient sources: The Padma Purana recounts the legend, and the Yajurveda also mentions it.
