Egyptian sun god

Ra

Ra is the Egyptian god of the Sun and of the origin of life, most closely associated with the midday sun. His cult became one of the most important in ancient Egypt, and pharaohs were regarded as his descendants and embodiments.

The myth

According to Egyptian tradition, at first there was only darkness and the primordial waters of Nun. Ra, who often took the form of a bird, emerged from them and created the world by speaking the names of things: the sun, the wind of Shu, the moisture of Tefnut, the earth of Geb, the sky of Nut and the Nile of Hapi came into being, and finally humans.

Each day Ra crossed the sky in a solar barque: the Mandjet by day and the Mesektet by night, passing through the Duat, the underworld. There he had to defeat the serpent Apophis every night in order to be reborn at dawn. He was Khepri in the morning, Ra at noon and Atum in the evening.

In the myth of the Celestial Cow, Ra grew old while ruling as the first pharaoh, and people mocked and disobeyed him. He sent his eye in the form of the goddess Sekhmet to punish humanity, and she raged across the land. To stop her, Ra had beer dyed red with ochre-like ámbar; Sekhmet drank it believing it was blood, became drunk, and the slaughter ended. In another account, humankind was born from Ra's tears and sweat, which is why Egyptians called themselves the "cattle of Ra".

Worship and religion

Ra's chief cult centre was Iunu, which the Greeks called Heliopolis, near modern Cairo. The sacred Mnevis bull, an embodiment of the god, was worshipped there, and a formal cemetery for the sacrificed bulls lay north of the city. In the New Kingdom Ra was fused with Amun as Amun-Ra, the leading deity of the pantheon. Akhenaten suppressed his cult in favour of Aten, but it was restored after Akhenaten's death.

Legacy

His name survives in popular culture through the image of the falcon-headed god and the solar disc with a cobra. Modern writers link him with light and cosmic order, though his influence on contemporary language is slight.

Ancient sources: Egyptian religious texts such as the Book of the Dead describe his nightly journey through the Duat, and the myth of the Celestial Cow recounts the punishment sent to humanity.
