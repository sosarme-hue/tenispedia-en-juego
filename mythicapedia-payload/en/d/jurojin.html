Japanese god of longevity

Jurōjin

Jurōjin is one of the Seven Gods of Fortune in Japanese folklore, of Taoist origin, and the god of longevity. His figure comes from a Chinese old man linked to the South Pole, and he matters as the keeper of the lifespan of every living being.

The myth

According to Taoist tradition, Jurōjin descends from a Chinese old man of the South Pole, associated with the Southern Polar Star. In China he was first known as Shou Xing, one of the Fushoulu, originally identified with the star Canopus.

He is regarded as an immortal who may have lived in the Northern Song dynasty (960–1127), though that historical origin is not confirmed. He is a short old man, under three shaku, with a long white beard and a tall, bald head. He walks with a staff and a fan and carries a scroll on which the lifespan of every living being is written. A deer, a symbol of longevity, usually accompanies him as a messenger.

Worship and religion

Jurōjin never developed a following in Japan apart from the other six Gods of Fortune, and he is venerated mainly as part of that group. Paintings and statues of him are considered auspicious. He is often identified with Fukurokuju, and in some accounts the two share one body, which is why they are frequently confused.

Legacy

He is a popular subject of Japanese ink wash painting. Zen Buddhist painters introduced him into the art tradition, and depictions of him span from the Muromachi period (1337–1573) to the Edo period (1603–1868). Artists who painted him include Sesshū, Sesson Shukei, Kanō Tan'yū and Maruyama Ōkyo.

Trivia

The scroll of life. He carries a scroll on which the lifespan of all living things is written. The scroll is sometimes identified as a Buddhist sutra.
Two gods in one body. In some accounts, Jurōjin and Fukurokuju share the same body, which is why they are often confused.
A star in China. In China, Shou Xing, the forerunner of Jurōjin, was originally identified with the star Canopus.
