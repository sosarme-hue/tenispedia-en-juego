Gallo-Roman goddess of luck and blessings

Naria

Naria was a goddess of Gallo-Roman religion, venerated in what is now western Switzerland and attested in only two inscriptions. Her nature and responsibilities remain unclear.

The myth

No ancient texts preserve stories about Naria, so there is no myth of her own to tell. She is known almost only through votive dedications and a statuette. The name may come from the Gaulish nertos, strength, and the epithet Nousantia, which appears nowhere else, may combine nauson, ship, and anatia, soul; both explanations are hypotheses.

Worship and religion

She was honoured with votive offerings. A stone altar from Cressier records Titus Frontinius Hibernus fulfilling a vow to her, and at Muri a regional association dedicated a statuette to her, entrusted to the freedman Ferox as curator. The figure wears a long-sleeved dress and a diadem, in a style used in Italy for Fortuna, suggesting she was seen as a giver of good luck and blessings. There is no evidence that her cult lasted beyond the Roman period.

Ancient sources: Two Latin inscriptions: a votive altar from Cressier and the base of a statuette from the Muri group.

Trivia

Missing hands. The hands that once held her divine attributes on the Muri statuette are lost, so we do not know what the goddess carried.
A name with Gaulish roots. The name Naria is thought to derive from the Gaulish nertos, strength, linking the goddess to the language of the Gauls.
