Sumerian god of Umma, protector of farming and irrigation

Shara

Shara was a minor Mesopotamian god worshipped chiefly in the Sumerian city of Umma, where he watched over fields, livestock and irrigation canals. He could also be cast as a divine warrior and, in some traditions, was the son of Inanna.

The myth

According to tradition, Shara was a lesser god tied to a specific territory rather than to large narrative cycles. Some texts call him the "Prince of Heaven" and the "beloved son of Inanna", and make him a brother of Nidaba. In Umma his father was unknown, while in the story of Inanna's Descent to the Underworld he is among the servants who mourn the goddess during her temporary death. He also appears in the myth of Anzû, where he is one of three gods who refuse to fight the monster.

Worship and religion

His main temple was the Emaḫ, whose Sumerian name means "exalted house", and it included a ziggurat called Sigkuršaga. He also had shrines in Umma and nearby settlements, and his clergy included purification specialists, singers, flutists and snake charmers. His worship declined when Umma lost political influence, and the last clear reference to it dates from the Old Babylonian period.

Ancient sources: He appears in inscriptions of the kings of Umma and Lagash, in god lists from Shuruppak and Umma, and in Old Babylonian texts such as the myth of Anzû.

Trivia

A god who could be reinterpreted as female. In god lists from the first millennium BCE, Shara could be reinterpreted as a female deity.
A plow with a name. Documents from Umma mention a plow dedicated to Shara, possibly called "the plow named Marching with Shara".
