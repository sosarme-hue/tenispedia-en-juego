old demon of the Sanzu River in Japanese Buddhist mythology

Keneō

Keneō is an old demon of the Buddhist underworld who sits at the edge of the Sanzu River, where the souls of the dead must cross. His name means "the old man who hangs clothes", and together with the old woman Datsue-ba he carries out punishments according to each soul's sins.

The myth

In Japanese Buddhist tradition, when an adult dies, the soul arrives at the Sanzu River and must cross it. Datsue-ba forces the soul to undress, and Keneō hangs the clothes on a branch by the bank. The branch bends according to the weight of the garments: the worst sinners carry more water, since they had to swim through the deepest part, so the branch bows lower.

The punishments depend on the offence. For thieves, Datsue-ba breaks their fingers and, together with Keneō, ties the sinner's head to their feet. If a soul arrives without clothes, Keneō flays its skin and hangs it from the tree instead. The pair also walk the riverbank to torment the souls of children too young to cross the river.

Worship and religion

In Japan, coins of six mon were placed with the body as payment for entering the underworld.

Trivia

Clothes and weight. The weight of the clothes hung on the branch shows the gravity of the sins, since the worst sinners carry more water when crossing the river.
Children on the bank. Keneō and Datsue-ba also torment on the riverbank the souls of children who are too young to cross the river.
