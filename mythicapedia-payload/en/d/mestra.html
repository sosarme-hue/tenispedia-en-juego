daughter of Erysichthon, heroine of Greek myth

Mestra

Mestra is a figure of Greek myth, the daughter of Erysichthon of Thessaly, known for her ability to change shape and for her relationship with Poseidon. Her story, shaped by her father's insatiable hunger, appears in the Catalogue of Women attributed to Hesiod and above all in Ovid's Metamorphoses.

The myth

According to the myth, Erysichthon cut down a sacred grove of Demeter to build a banqueting hall. The goddess punished him with an insatiable hunger that eventually ruined him. When he had no possessions left, he sold his daughter Mestra into slavery. She asked Poseidon for help, since he had been her lover, and the god granted her the power to change shape. She escaped her master by turning into a fisherman, and when he searched for her, she cleverly denied having seen any woman on the shore.

Erysichthon discovered his daughter's gift and sold her again and again. Each time Mestra escaped by transforming into a heifer, a deer or a bird and returned home, where her father used the money to buy food. In the end, the hunger consumed him until he devoured himself.

In the Catalogue of Women, attributed to Hesiod, the story changes: Erysichthon married her off to suitors in exchange for bride prices. Sisyphus wanted to match her with his son Glaucus, but Mestra fled from his house, and Poseidon carried her off to the island of Cos. There she bore a son, Eurypylus, who became king of the island. Ovid, however, says that Mestra ended up married to Autolycus, grandfather of Odysseus, a version found only in his work.

Ancient sources: She appears in the Catalogue of Women attributed to Hesiod and in Ovid's Metamorphoses; Antoninus Liberalis calls her Hypermestra.

Trivia

Another name. Antoninus Liberalis calls her Hypermestra and her father Aethon, a name tied to the hunger that consumed him.
A king of Cos. Her son by Poseidon, Eurypylus, ruled Cos, and his descendants Phidippus and Antiphus led the island's forces in the Trojan War.
