Mesopotamian sun god and god of justice

Shamash (Utu)

Shamash, known as Utu in Sumerian, was the sun god of ancient Mesopotamia and one of its principal deities, responsible for justice. He was especially venerated in Sippar and Larsa, and he was also associated with the protection of travellers.

The myth

According to tradition, the god saw everything that happened in the world each day, which is why he was entrusted with justice and the protection of travellers. As a divine judge he could be linked to the underworld, and he also acted as a god of divination, often alongside the weather god Adad.

The sources do not agree on his parentage. Akkadian and Babylonian texts made him a son of Anu or, on other occasions, of Enlil. From the Sumerian Renaissance onwards he was regarded as a son of Nanna and Ningal, and brother of Inanna and Iskur. His wife was Aya (Sherida), the dawn goddess, and texts describe their daily reunions on a mountain where the sun was believed to set.

No myths focus on him, yet he often appears as an ally of other figures. In narratives about Dumuzi's death, he helped protect him when the galla demons tried to drag him to the underworld. In several versions of the Epic of Gilgamesh he helps the hero defeat the monster Humbaba, and in the myth of Inanna and An he assists his sister in acquiring the temple Eanna.

Each day he crossed the sky from east to west in a solar chariot pulled by four animals whose Sumerian names refer to the light of heaven. At night he returned through a region of the sky above the underworld, though this idea developed later.

Worship and religion

His main sanctuary was at Sippar, called E-babbar, and in the Sumerian period there was a temple of the same name at Larsa, dedicated to him and to his wife Aya. He was shown as a solar disc with eight points or as a male figure with flames rising from his shoulders, and later the scales became his symbol of justice. He was associated with the number 20. According to the record, Hammurabi built a great wall at Sippar in 1767 BC dedicated to him. He was probably also invoked outdoors, as a celestial body.

Legacy

His name gave rise to Semitic words for the sun: šamšu in Akkadian, šams in Arabic and šemeš in Hebrew. The name also survives in Mandaean cosmology, where the sun is called Shamish, derived from Akkadian. In Amorite, his form Samsu appears in personal names such as Samsu-iluna, meaning Samsu is our god.

Ancient sources: The Epic of Gilgamesh, in both its earlier versions and the Standard Babylonian version, and the myth of Inanna and An mention him as an ally of heroes and of his sister, while Sumerian and Akkadian god lists place him among the principal deities.

Trivia

A borrowed name. The name Utu was used to write the names of many foreign solar deities logographically. The link with the Hurrian sun god Shimige is especially well attested.
The number 20. One spelling of his name used the numeral 20, which was associated with him. That figure served as a sign representing him in texts.
