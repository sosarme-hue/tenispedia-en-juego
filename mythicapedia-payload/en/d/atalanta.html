huntress heroine of Greek mythology

Atalanta

Atalanta is a heroine of Greek mythology, a swift huntress and companion of Artemis, best known for her part in the Calydonian boar hunt and for the footrace that decided her marriage. Her story reflects the tension between the wild life she loved and the social expectations placed on women.

The myth

According to the tradition, her father, who wanted a son, abandoned her as a baby on Mount Parthenion. A she-bear that had just lost its cubs nursed her, until hunters found her and raised her themselves. She grew into a virgin huntress who admired Artemis, and her speed was such that no one could catch her when she chose to run.

The sources differ on her origin. Hesiod makes her the daughter of Schoeneus, linking her to Boeotia, while Arcadian traditions name Iasus and Clymene as her parents. As an adult, she fought the centaurs Hylaeus and Rhoecus, who tried to abduct her, and killed them with her arrows.

She took part in the Calydonian boar hunt, sent by Artemis as punishment. Her arrows were the first to wound the beast, and Meleager gave her the hide, but his uncles took it away and Meleager killed them. She also defeated Peleus in a wrestling match at the funeral games of Pelias. Her place among the Argonauts is disputed: some authors count her as the only woman on the voyage, while in Apollonius of Rhodes Jason stops her from sailing for fear she would cause strife.

To avoid marriage, she set a footrace: whoever beat her would wed her, and whoever lost would die. Many suitors perished until Hippomenes, or Melanion in other versions, won with Aphrodite's help, carrying three golden apples. Each time she drew near, he dropped an apple and Atalanta stopped to pick it up. Later, the couple made love in a sacred sanctuary, and the gods turned them into lions, though the sources do not agree on who carried out the transformation.

Legacy

Her story inspired Algernon Swinburne's play Atalanta in Calydon and many paintings of the boar hunt. The Italian football club Atalanta, from Bergamo, took its name from the heroine, and its crest shows her face. The European Union's anti-piracy mission off Somalia is named after her. She appears in Emily Hauser's novel For the Winner (2017), Jennifer Saint's Atalanta (2023) and the light novel Fate/Apocrypha. The Olympic javelin thrower Fatima Whitbread said the myth of Atalanta inspired her to take up track and field. In Madrid, two lions on the Cibeles fountain on the Paseo del Prado represent the transformed couple.

Ancient sources: Hesiod in the Catalogue of Women, Callimachus, Euripides, Apollonius of Rhodes in the Argonautica, Pseudo-Apollodorus, Diodorus Siculus and Ovid in the Metamorphoses.

Trivia

A spring at Cyphanta. According to one tradition, a spring rose at Cyphanta where Atalanta struck a rock with her javelin when she was thirsty.
Lions in Madrid. The two lions into which the couple were transformed appear in the Cibeles fountain in Madrid.
