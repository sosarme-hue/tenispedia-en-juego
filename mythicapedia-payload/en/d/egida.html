shield or breastplate of Zeus and Athena in Greek mythology

Aegis

The aegis is the prodigious shield or breastplate of Zeus and Athena in Greek mythology, often decorated with the head of Medusa. It matters because its name came to mean protection under the care of a powerful figure.

The myth

According to Homer, in the Iliad the aegis is the shield of Zeus, crafted by Hephaestus, fringed with tassels and bearing the head of Medusa at its centre. When the god shook it, Mount Ida was wrapped in clouds, thunder rolled and men were struck down with fear. Zeus sometimes lent it to Athena, and in the poem Apollo also carries it to drive the Achaeans back to their ships.

The name has several explanations. It may derive from a verb meaning 'to rush violently', linked to the storm, or from aix, goat, which would make it a goatskin. Later accounts favour the second idea: Zeus supposedly used as a shield the skin of the goat Amalthea, who had suckled him on Crete, when he went to fight the Giants. Another tradition says that Athena slew a fire-breathing monster, similar to the Chimera, and wore its skin as a cuirass. Some also claim it was the skin of the giant Pallas.

Over time the aegis came to be a shield at times and a cuirass at others. In art it appears as an animal skin draped over Athena's shoulders, bordered with serpents, or as a cuirass with the head of Medusa at its centre.

Legacy

The aegis symbolised the invulnerability granted by the gods, and Roman emperors were often shown wearing a small shield bearing Medusa's head on the chest. It appears on statues of emperors, heroes and warriors, and on coins, cameos and vases. In a first-century BC fresco from Pompeii depicting Alexander the Great, his armour shows a female face resembling a Gorgon. In modern English, 'under the aegis of' someone means under their protection, a sense the word still keeps.

Ancient sources: Homer's Iliad, which describes it as the shield of Zeus and Athena; Euripides' Ion, which identifies it with the Gorgon's skin; and Virgil, who places it in Hephaestus's forge.

Trivia

A play on words. The name of the storm may have been linked to the Greek word for goat, αἴξ, through sound similarity, which could have given rise to the idea of a goatskin.
An Egyptian aegis. In Egyptian mythology, the goddess Bastet is sometimes shown with an aegis resembling a collar decorated with a lioness head.
Herodotus and the Libyan women. Herodotus believed the aegis originated with the women of Libya, who dressed in a similar way, though with leather thongs instead of serpents.
