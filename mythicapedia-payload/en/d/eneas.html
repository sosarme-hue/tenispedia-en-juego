Trojan hero, mythical ancestor of the Romans

Aeneas

Aeneas is a Trojan hero of Greco-Roman mythology, son of the prince Anchises and the goddess Aphrodite. After the fall of Troy, tradition takes him to Latium, where he becomes the mythical ancestor of the Roman people.

The myth

According to Hesiod's Theogony and the Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite, the goddess lay with Anchises, a Dardanian prince herding his cattle on Mount Ida. Disguised as a Phrygian princess, she seduced him, then revealed her identity and promised him protection on condition that he keep silent. Anchises boasted of the encounter, and Zeus struck him with a thunderbolt that left him lame. Aeneas was born on Ida, raised by nymphs until he was five, and then returned to his father. Another tradition says that the centaur Chiron taught him the art of hunting.

In Homer's Iliad he is a brave warrior, the most valiant Trojan after Hector. The gods save him several times: Aphrodite shields him from Diomedes, though she is wounded herself; Apollo wraps him in a cloud and carries him to Pergamos; Poseidon rescues him from Achilles. Two lost poems of the Trojan Cycle told different endings: in the Little Iliad he becomes part of Neoptolemus' spoils and regains his freedom, while in the Iliupersis he escapes the burning city.

In the Latin tradition, Aphrodite (Venus) orders him to flee Troy. He leaves with his father Anchises, his son Ascanius (or Iulus) and several companions, carrying the Lares and Penates. His wife Creusa is lost during the escape. Anchises dies in Sicily. In Carthage, Queen Dido falls in love with him; when Jupiter orders him to leave, she takes her own life. In the underworld, her ghost refuses to forgive him. Palinurus falls into the sea near Lucania.

Aeneas reaches the coast of Latium. King Latinus has a daughter, Lavinia, and oracles foretell that she will marry a foreigner. Turnus, her cousin and suitor, declares war with Etruscan allies, while Aeneas allies himself with King Evander. When Turnus dies, Aeneas marries Lavinia and they have a son, Silvius. According to the myth, his descendants lead to Romulus and Remus, the founders of Rome.

Worship and religion

In later versions, Aeneas died in his first battle, was buried on the banks of the river Numicus and received worship under the name Jupiter Indiges. The sources do not describe specific temples or festivals dedicated to him.

Legacy

Virgil made Aeneas the protagonist of the Aeneid, where he is called pater and pius, epithets that set him apart from the cunning of Odysseus. William Shakespeare included him among the characters of Troilus and Cressida. Snorri Sturluson identifies him with the Norse god Víðarr.

Ancient sources: Homer's Iliad and Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite, Hesiod's Theogony, the lost Little Iliad and Iliupersis of the Trojan Cycle, and Virgil's Aeneid; later Trojan narratives attributed to Dares Phrygius and Dictys of Crete also tell his story.

Trivia

Name with a folk etymology. The Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite links his name to an adjective meaning 'terrible', for the 'terrible grief' that his mortality causes the goddess.
Twenty-eight enemies. The Roman mythographer Hyginus credits Aeneas with killing 28 enemies during the Trojan War.
A red-haired hero. Dares Phrygius describes him as red-haired, well proportioned, eloquent and cheerful-eyed.
