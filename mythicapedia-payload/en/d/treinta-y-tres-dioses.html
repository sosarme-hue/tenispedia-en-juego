Hindu pantheon of thirty-three deities

Thirty-three gods

The Thirty-three gods, or Trayastrimshaddevas, are a Hindu pantheon rooted in the Vedas, grouping Adityas, Rudras and Vasus, with lists that vary by source. They matter because they show how Indian tradition organised its deities into numbered sets.

The myth

The Sanskrit name Traias trimsha means "the thirty-three". The Samhitas, the oldest layer of the Vedas, count thirty-three devas spread across three worlds, eleven in each. Later Brahmana and Puranic texts group them as twelve Adityas, eleven Rudras and eight Vasus, and sometimes add the two Ashvins, twin solar deities.

The names shift from one source to another. The Adityas include Indra, Mitra, Varuna, Aryaman or Vishnu; the Vasus stand for elements such as earth, fire, wind or the moon; and the Rudras include abstractions such as bliss, knowledge or speech. Indra, also called Shakra, comes first in the list, and Prajapati, lord of creatures, appears as a creator deity.

Ancient sources: The Rig Veda does not mention the number, which appears in the Atharva Veda. Buddhist texts after the 3rd century BCE use the same name for the realm of the devas.

Trivia

A number that changes. The lists of deities vary between manuscripts and regions of South Asia, especially regarding the Ashvins and the personified devas.
From the Vedas to Buddhism. The same Sanskrit name appears in Buddhist sources after the 3rd century BCE as a name for the realm of the devas.
