Heimdallr's horn in Norse mythology

Gjallarhorn

The Gjallarhorn is the horn of Heimdallr, the guardian of Asgard, whose blast will signal the start of Ragnarök, the end of the world. It is one of the best-known objects in Norse myth because of its role in the final battle of the gods.

The myth

In Norse mythology, the Gjallarhorn belongs to Heimdallr, the guardian of Asgard. Its sound will be heard in every world and will mark the beginning of Ragnarök, the great final battle.

In the Poetic Edda, the horn appears only once, in the Völuspá, in the context of the end of the world. In the Prose Edda, Snorri Sturluson mentions it three times in Gylfaginning. In the first passage, the enthroned figure Hár, or "High", explains that Heimdallr drinks from Mímir's well, which lies beside one of the roots of Yggdrasil, and gains wisdom from it. In the second, its blast is said to be heard in all worlds. In the third, when Ragnarök comes, Heimdallr will blow hard to wake the gods, who will gather at the assembly.

Legacy

The name has entered popular culture. It is the title of a Finnish folk metal band and, in the anime Mobile Suit Gundam: Iron-Blooded Orphans, it names the enemy army that Tekkadan faces throughout the series. It also appears in the video game Destiny, as the name of an exotic rocket launcher, and in God of War: Ragnarök, where Kratos uses it at the start of Ragnarök.

Ancient sources: The Völuspá in the Poetic Edda and Gylfaginning in Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda.

Trivia

A horn for drinking. According to Snorri, Heimdallr uses the Gjallarhorn to drink from Mímir's well, which makes him wise. The horn thus serves both as a signalling instrument and as a drinking vessel.
A horned figure in stone. A figure with a large horn at its lips and a sword at its hip appears on a stone cross from the Isle of Man, and another on the Gosforth Cross in Cumbria. Some scholars believe they depict Heimdallr with the Gjallarhorn.
The Golden Horns of Gallehus. The Golden Horns of Gallehus, from Denmark and dating to the 5th century, are seen by some specialists as examples of sacred Germanic horns, an earthly version of Heimdallr's horn.
