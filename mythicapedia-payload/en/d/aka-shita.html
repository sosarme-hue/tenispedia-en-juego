Japanese yōkai of the red tongue

Aka-shita

Aka-shita is a Japanese yōkai drawn by Toriyama Sekien in his Gazu Hyakki Yagyō: a clawed beast with a hairy face and an enormous tongue sticking out of an open mouth, hidden behind black clouds. Its name links it to Onmyōdō calendar lore and to the older figure of Akaguchi, which makes it a useful case for understanding how yōkai were reshaped during the Edo period.

The myth

Sekien's picture carries no explanatory note, so it is unclear whether the yōkai came from popular tradition or from the artist's imagination. Some scholars connect the name with shakuzetsujin, a red-tongued god who guards the western gate of Jupiter, or with shakuzetsunichi, an unlucky day in the Onmyōdō calendar. Katsumi Tada reads aka as filth and shita as tongue, so the image would warn that whoever keeps their mouth open will never find good fortune.

In Tōhoku folklore, an Akaguchi settled a dispute over irrigation water in Tsugaru: it punished those who stole water from the floodgates by swallowing them with its giant red tongue. This story appears in twentieth-century books rather than in ancient sources, and was probably attached to the yōkai later.

Legacy

Its image spread through twentieth-century illustrated yōkai guides such as Arifumi Satō's Yōkai Daizukan, which describes it as a being that sticks its tongue out of a red evening sky to kidnap people. That version does not come from Sekien or older literature, but from a modern reinterpretation.

Ancient sources: It appears in Toriyama Sekien's Gazu Hyakki Yagyō and in illustrated scrolls such as the Bakemono no e and the Hyakkai Zukan (Sawaki Suushi, 1737), where it is known as Akaguchi.

Trivia

A warning in the tongue. Katsumi Tada suggests the name hides a saying: the tongue is the gate to misfortune.
Just a cloud. In many depictions only a black cloud appears, and the floodgate in Sekien's picture is absent from later Akaguchi images.
