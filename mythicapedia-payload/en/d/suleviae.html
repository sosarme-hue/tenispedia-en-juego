Celtic goddesses of household and personal protection

Suleviae

The Suleviae were a group of Celtic goddesses worshipped in Gaul, the German provinces, Britain and Rome, regarded as mother goddesses and protectors of individuals and households. Their name, nearly always in the plural, is usually read as 'those who guide well'.

The myth

Ancient authors never mention them, so almost nothing is told about them as stories. They are known only from around forty votive inscriptions and from some sculptures that sometimes accompany them.

They are generally grouped with the Celtic mother goddesses, the Matres, and often appear as Matres Suleviae or Suleviae Iunones, equivalents of the Roman guardian spirits. A dedication from Ladenburg shows them as a group of sisters. An inscription from Colchester links them with Camulodunum.

Worship and religion

Most dedications were made by private individuals, including soldiers and veterans. They are especially numerous at Rome, which scholars explain by the presence of Celtic and Germanic soldiers in the capital in the 2nd and 3rd centuries AD. At Bath and Cirencester they were the local name of the mother goddesses, and the same sculptor, Sulinus, dedicated works to them in both towns. At Colchester they are linked with an apsidal shrine. Their cult did not survive.

Ancient sources: They are known only from Latin votive inscriptions; no ancient author mentions them.

Trivia

A recurring sculptor. The same sculptor, Sulinus son of Brucetus, dedicated works to the Suleviae both at Bath and at Cirencester.
An inscription at Colchester. An inscription from Colchester (Camulodunum) names them Matribus Sulevis, the Suleviae Mothers.
