Shinto kami, lord of the Central Land

Ōkuninushi

Ōkuninushi is a kami of Japanese Shinto, associated with the building of the nation, the land, agriculture, business and medicine. He is closely tied to Izumo Province and to the myths recorded in the Kojiki and the Nihon Shoki.

The myth

According to the myth, Ōkuninushi, first known as Ōnamuji, was one of eighty brothers who travelled from Izumo to court the princess of Inaba. On the way they met a flayed hare that had tricked some wani into forming a bridge across the sea. Ōnamuji advised it to bathe in fresh water and roll in cattail pollen, and the hare predicted he would win the princess.

His jealous brothers killed him twice, first by luring him into catching a red-hot boulder and then by trapping him in a split tree trunk. His mother asked Kamimusubi for help, and two clam goddesses restored his life. In the underworld he fell in love with Suseribime, Susanoo's daughter, and with her help he passed her father's trials before fleeing with her.

He later ruled Izumo and the Central Land until he handed the land to Takemikazuchi and withdrew into the world of shadows. The Kojiki makes him a sixth-generation descendant of Susanoo through Ame no Fuyukinu, while the Nihon Shoki makes him Susanoo's son.

Worship and religion

He is worshipped at many shrines, above all the Izumo-taisha, one of Japan's oldest and most important. The sectarian group Izumo-taishakyo regards him as its central deity, and his worship continues within Shinto today.

Legacy

From the medieval period he was identified with the popular deity Daikokuten, which is why he is also known as Daikoku-sama, a figure very common in popular imagery.

Ancient sources: The Kojiki and the Nihon Shoki record his myths, and the Izumo fudoki tells of Ōanamochi in Izumo Province.

Trivia

The White Hare of Inaba. In the Kojiki, a flayed hare receives help from Ōnamuji and predicts his marriage to the princess. It is the character's best-known episode.
One Hundred Eighty-One Children. According to a variant in the Nihon Shoki, Ōkuninushi had 181 children.
