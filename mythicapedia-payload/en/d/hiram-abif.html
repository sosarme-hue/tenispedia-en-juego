master builder of Solomon's Temple in Masonic legend

Hiram Abiff

Hiram Abiff is a craftsman who, according to the Bible, worked on Solomon's Temple, and whom Freemasonry turned into the central figure of a moral allegory about loyalty and the certainty of death. The third degree of the Masonic ritual retells his story to every candidate.

The myth

In the Masonic story, Hiram is the chief architect of the temple Solomon builds in Jerusalem. As the work nears completion, three fellow craftsmen ambush him as he leaves the building and demand the secrets of a master mason. Hiram refuses, and each of them strikes him with a mason's tool: the first two wound him, and the third kills him.

The murderers hide the body under rubble and, at night, bury it outside the city with a sprig of acacia as a marker. When Solomon notices his absence, he sends men to search for him. The sprig is discovered by chance, the body receives a proper burial, and the culprits are brought to justice. Because the master's secret word is now lost, the king adopts a substitute. In the continental tradition the secrets are not lost but buried beneath the temple.

Legacy

He is the central figure of the third degree in Anglo-American Freemasonry, which has used his story in lodges since the early 1720s. The French writer Gérard de Nerval included a version in his Voyage en Orient (1851), woven into the tale of Solomon and the Queen of Sheba. His origins remain a subject of study and debate among historians of Freemasonry.

Ancient sources: The Hebrew Bible (1 Kings 7 and 2 Chronicles 2), which mentions a Tyrian craftsman sent to Solomon, and Josephus in his Antiquities of the Jews, who describes him as an artificer from Tyre.

Trivia

The widow's son. The Bible describes a Hiram whose mother was a widow from the tribe of Naphtali. For this reason Freemasons often call him 'the widow's son'.
Adoniram. In some versions of the Continental Masonic tradition the character is named Adoniram.
