the morning star in Roman mythology

Lucifer

Lucifer, meaning «light-bringer», was the Roman name for the morning star, that is, the planet Venus when it shines before dawn. Roman poets often pictured him as a male figure carrying a torch, and his image later passed into Christian tradition, where he became an epithet for the Devil.

The myth

According to Roman tradition, Venus appeared to ancient observers as a light that did not stay fixed in one spot, unlike the stars, and stories tried to explain this wandering in different ways. Lucifer was personified as a man bearing a torch, and poets presented him as the herald of dawn. The Greeks called the planet Phosphoros or Eosphoros, while its evening form was Hesperus.

He was said to be the son of Aurora and Cephalus, and father of Ceyx. Ovid, in his Metamorphoses, treats the morning and evening stars as one and makes him the father of Ceyx and Daedalion, while the grammarian Servius links him to the Hesperides. These genealogies differ between authors, so there is no single family tree.

In the classical Roman period, Lucifer was not usually regarded as a god and had few myths of his own. He mostly appeared as a poetic personification of the planet, which was linked to several deities. Cicero used the star as an argument: if the Moon was a goddess, so were Lucifer and the other wandering stars.

The roots of the story reach deep into the Near East. The Sumerian goddess Inanna, associated with Venus, stars in myths whose rhythm seems to follow the planet's cycle, and in Canaan the morning star was personified as the god Attar, who tried to take Baal's throne and ended up descending to the underworld. An echo of this fall from heaven reached the Book of Isaiah, where the king of Babylon is called Helel, son of the morning. The Vulgate translated that title as Lucifer, and from there the name came to designate the fallen angel and the Devil in Christian tradition.

Legacy

The word lucifer long served as the name of the morning star, and Old English called the planet morgensteorra. Some Satanist groups have venerated him as a bringer of freedom and enlightenment, while anti-Masonic literature has accused Freemasons of worshipping him, a charge with no historical basis. In Christian literature he appears in Dante's Divine Comedy, Milton's Paradise Lost and Vondel's Lucifer.

Ancient sources: Hyginus, Ovid in his Metamorphoses, Cicero, and Catullus, who calls him Noctifer as the evening star.
