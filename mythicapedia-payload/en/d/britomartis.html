Cretan goddess of mountains and hunting

Britomartis

Britomartis is a goddess of mountains, nets and hunting, worshipped mainly on Crete. Over time she was identified with Artemis, and her figure shows how older island deities blended with later Greek religion.

The myth

According to the myths, Britomartis was a daughter of Zeus and Carme and a companion of Artemis, with whom she roamed the mountains hunting. She vowed to remain a virgin and to avoid men. Minos pursued her for nine months through mountains and woods until, cornered, she leapt into the sea from a cliff. She landed in the nets of some fishermen, who rescued her, and this gave her the name Dictynna, Lady of the Nets, as well as the name of Mount Dikte.

Another version, preserved by Antoninus Liberalis, has her reach Aegina by boat after escaping Minos. A fisherman named Andromedes tried to assault her, but she jumped into the woods and vanished. For this she was called Aphaea, the invisible one.

Worship and religion

Her main temple stood at Cydonia, and on Crete she was honoured as Dictynna with sacrifices and temples. On Aegina she had a sanctuary linked to that of Athena, where a statue stood, and she received sacrifices as a goddess. In the second century CE, Pausanias noted that both Cretans and Aeginetans honoured her.

Legacy

In the sixteenth century Edmund Spenser created Britomart in The Faerie Queene, a female knight of chastity who embodies English virtue. Her name inspired many Britomart figures in British art and literature.

Ancient sources: Callimachus mentions her in his hymn to Artemis, Diodorus Siculus and Antoninus Liberalis recount her story, and Pausanias describes her cult.

Trivia

A name from nets. Diodorus Siculus explains that Dictynna comes from the Greek word diktya, meaning nets, because she was said to have invented hunting nets.
Zeus's mountain. Mount Dikte was, according to tradition, Zeus's birthplace, and the goddess Dictynna took her name from it.
