one of the Hundred-Handers of Greek mythology

Cottus

Cottus is one of the Hundred-Handers, giants of Greek mythology with a hundred arms and fifty heads, sons of Uranus and Gaia. He matters because he was the only one of his brothers to answer Zeus when Zeus freed them.

The myth

According to the myth, Uranus and Gaia produced the Hundred-Handers: Cottus, Briareus and Gyges, beings with a hundred arms and fifty heads each. After Zeus released them, Cottus was the only one of the three to answer him and promise his help. Like his brothers, after the Titanomachy Zeus allowed him to live in a palace above the roots of the river Oceanus.

Unlike Briareus, Cottus has almost no story of his own and usually appears alongside his brothers. An euhemerist account cited by Palaephatus, however, presents him as a mortal man rather than a monstrous giant. In that version, Cottus and Briareus lived in a city called Hecatonchiria, which gave them their name, and they helped the Olympians drive the Titans out of Olympia.

Trivia

Euhemerist version. Palaephatus records a version in which Cottus and Briareus are mortal men who lived in the city of Hecatonchiria, from which they would have taken their name.
The Cotians. According to Graves, Cottus gave his name to the Cotians, who worshipped the orgiastic Cotytto and spread her cult from Thrace across northwestern Europe.
