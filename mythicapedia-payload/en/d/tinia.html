Etruscan sky god

Tinia

Tinia, also known as Tin, was the highest sky god in Etruscan religion, equated with Jupiter in Rome and Zeus in Greece. His name appears in inscriptions and on the famous Piacenza Liver, making him one of the best-attested figures in the Etruscan pantheon.

The myth

According to Etruscan tradition, Tinia was the husband of Uni and the father of Hercle. He belonged to a powerful divine triad with Menrva and Uni, and he was associated with thunderbolts, spears and sceptres.

The accounts present him as master of thunder. The Etruscans distinguished eleven kinds of thunderbolt, and Tinia wielded three of them. He was shown at times bearded and seated, and at other times youthful, standing and beardless. His name, tin, also meant day, so he seemed to govern the passing of time.

Tinia also guarded boundaries. His name appears as guarantor on three identical boundary stones found in Tunisia, placed there by Etruscan colonists.

Worship and religion

Tinia is the deity named most often on the Piacenza Liver, a bronze model used in liver divination, where he appears five times, always first or ranked above other gods. His possible epithets, such as Tin Cilens and Tin Θuf, still lack an agreed translation because the Etruscan language is poorly understood.

Legacy

His figure survives mainly through his identification with Jupiter, who inherited much of his role as celestial and thunder god.

Ancient sources: He appears in Etruscan inscriptions, such as the Oltos painted kylix of around 500 BC and the Chimera of Arezzo, and on the Piacenza Liver.

Trivia
