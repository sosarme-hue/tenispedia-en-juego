Roman goddess of fruit and fruit trees

Pomona

Pomona was the Roman goddess of fruit, fruit trees, orchards and well-kept gardens. Her name comes from the Latin pomum, 'fruit', and she embodied the Roman ideal of abundance linked to cultivation.

The myth

According to Ovid, Pomona was a hamadryad, a tree nymph, skilled in the study of trees and orchards. Many woodland gods pursued her, the satyrs among them, but she showed no interest in any man and preferred a tended garden far from wild nature. Other traditions name Silvanus or Picus among her suitors.

Vertumnus, a god of the seasons and of fruit trees, fell in love with her and courted her for a long time. Pomona refused even to receive him, so he disguised himself as an old woman, praised her fruit and embraced her. Then he showed her an elm entwined with a vine and argued the case for love, telling her the story of Anaxarete, a woman who scorned her suitor and was punished. Fascinated, Pomona saw his true face, radiant with youth and health, and accepted him. The British historian Herbert Jennings Rose considered this episode a pure invention by Ovid or by some later writer.

Worship and religion

Her cult was entrusted to a minor flamen, the flamen pomonalis, the lowest in rank among the priesthoods. She had a sacred grove, the Pomonal, beside the Via Ostiensis, not far from Ostia, Rome's ancient port. She had no festival of her own, though the poet Ausonius placed the month of September under her protection, since fruit ripens then. Some sources link her to a festival shared with Vertumnus on 13 August.

Legacy

Pomona returned to view in the Renaissance, and between the sixteenth and eighteenth centuries painters often depicted the scene of Vertumnus disguised as an old woman, among them Rubens and Goltzius. Auguste Rodin carved her in marble, and a bronze statue crowns the Pulitzer Fountain in New York. Robert Cambert's opera Pomone premiered in 1671, and Constant Lambert wrote a ballet called Pomona in 1928. Her name also lives on in the city of Pomona, California, in Pomona College and in the asteroid 32 Pomona.

Ancient sources: Ovid recounts her story in his Metamorphoses, and the poet Ausonius, writing in late antiquity, links her with the month of September.

Trivia

An asteroid named for her. 32 Pomona is a main-belt asteroid discovered in 1854.
A Californian college. The city of Pomona, California, takes its name from the goddess, and Pomona College kept the name when it moved to Claremont.
