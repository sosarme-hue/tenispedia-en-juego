monstrous lion of Greek mythology

Nemean lion

The Nemean lion was an invulnerable beast of Greek mythology that terrorized the region of Nemea. Its killing by Heracles was the first of his twelve labours, and its pelt became one of the hero's most recognizable symbols.

The myth

According to Hesiod, in the Theogony, the lion was the offspring of Orthus and of a mother he calls only "she", an ambiguous pronoun that scholars have linked to the Chimera, Echidna or perhaps Ceto. Other authors give different parentage: Apollodorus makes it a son of Typhon, while Hyginus and Aelian tell how it was raised by Selene, the moon goddess, who threw it down to earth at Hera's request. Photius makes it a brother of Ladon, the dragon guarding the apples of the Hesperides. In these accounts the lion was invulnerable and terrorized the land of Nemea.

King Eurystheus of Tiryns set Heracles his first task: to bring back the lion's skin. On the way to Nemea, the hero lodged with Molorchos, a poor farmer who was about to sacrifice an animal. Heracles asked him to wait thirty days: if he returned alive, the offering would go to Zeus the Saviour, and if he died, it would be dedicated to him as a hero. Finding that his arrows could not wound the beast, Heracles chased it with his club into a cave with two openings. He blocked one, entered by the other, stunned the lion and strangled it with his bare hands. Unable to cut the pelt with his knife, the tradition says Athena suggested he use one of the lion's own claws.

Heracles returned carrying the carcass on his shoulders on the thirtieth day. Eurystheus, terrified, forbade him from entering the city again and ordered him to display his trophies outside its gates.

Legacy

Zeus placed the lion in the sky as the constellation Leo, one of the most visible according to Eratosthenes. The Nemean Games were founded to honour this feat, and their victors crowned themselves with wild celery, as Heracles did after defeating the beast. The lion's pelt became Heracles' defining attribute in Greek and Roman art, and the Spanish painter Francisco de Zurbarán devoted one of his ten canvases on the labours of Hercules to the lion's death, now in the Prado Museum.

Ancient sources: Hesiod mentions it in the Theogony; Apollodorus in his Bibliotheca narrates the full labour; Eratosthenes and Aelian discuss its placement among the stars.

Trivia

Claws like knives. According to tradition, the lion's claws cut through anything a mortal sword could, which allowed Heracles to skin it.
A cave with two mouths. Heracles blocked one of the cave's entrances so the beast could not escape while he pursued it through the other.
