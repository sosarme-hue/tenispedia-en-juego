Aztec god of obsidian, frost and justice

Itztlacoliuhqui

Itztlacoliuhqui is, in Mexica mythology, the god of obsidian, frost and cold, and also the embodiment of justice and punishment. His figure combines the harshness of winter with the idea that no one escapes retribution.

The myth

According to Mexica tradition, this god began as Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli, lord of dawn and the planet Venus. Tonatiuh, the sun, demanded obedience and sacrifice from the other gods before he would move. Enraged by his arrogance, Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli shot an arrow that missed the sun. Tonatiuh's return shot pierced his head, and the dawn lord was transformed into Itztlacoliuhqui, lord of obsidian and cold.

Accounts differ on details. Some say his head is made of obsidian with an arrow lodged in it; others say the blindfold covers a wound on his brow. His name is often translated as "curved obsidian blade", though J. Richard Andrews proposes "everything has become bent by coldness".

Worship and religion

His role in worship is tied to punishment. According to the sources, drunkards were beaten to death with a club and adulterers stoned, instruments that appear in his iconography. He is also associated with pulque, a sacred drink of the indigenous peoples of Mexico.

Ancient sources: Bernardino de Sahagún describes him in his General History of the Things of New Spain, also known as the Florentine Codex, where he links him to Cintéotl.

Trivia

A dawn god. Before becoming Itztlacoliuhqui, the god was Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli, linked to the planet Venus as the morning star.
A broom in hand. His iconography shows a straw broom, symbolizing his role in clearing the way for new life to emerge.
