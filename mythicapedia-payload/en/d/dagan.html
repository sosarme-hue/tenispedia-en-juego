Syrian god of prosperity and father of the gods

Dagan

Dagan, called Dagon in the Hebrew Bible, was a god worshipped across ancient Syria and the middle Euphrates region. He was regarded as the father of the gods and a source of general prosperity. Although his name is linked to the word for grain, the sources present him less as an agricultural deity than as a guarantor of prosperity and royal legitimacy.

The myth

Surviving texts tell few stories about Dagan. No known text explains his parentage or creation. His wife was Shalash, and his children included Hadad, the counterpart of Ugaritic Baal, and possibly Hebat. He is portrayed as a father of the gods and a creator, a role similar to that of Enlil in Mesopotamia.

An Ugaritic myth tells how Baal alone refused to bow to the sea god Yam and defeated him with two magic maces made by the craftsman god Kosar wa Hasis. Dagan's temple stood on the acropolis of Ugarit, beside Baal's, and Baal was considered Dagan's son.

In the Bible, Dagon is the chief god of the Philistines. After capturing the Ark of the Covenant, they placed it in his temple (1 Samuel 5), though there is no evidence outside the biblical text for this Philistine cult. In Mesopotamia, one document records that King Sargon prostrated himself before Dagan at Tuttul, a gesture that legitimised his rule. Centuries later, Assurnasirpal II of Assyria called himself beloved of Anu and Dagan.

Worship and religion

His main centres were Tuttul, where his clergy had a role in traditional governance, and Terqa near Mari, home to his temple E-kisiga, meaning the house of the silent place. His worship also spread to Ebla, Emar, Aleppo and Ugarit. At Ugarit, two inscribed stelae dedicated to him have been found, along with a temple featuring a paved courtyard, a square hall and fifteen stone basins for ritual washing. He was invoked to ensure abundant harvests and to witness oaths. His cult disappeared with the ancient religions; in Carthage he was identified with Baal Hammon.

Legacy

The extrasolar object Fomalhaut b is named after him. The historian Sanchuniathon, preserved through Philo of Byblos, called him Zeus Arotrios, meaning the ploughman, because he had discovered grain and the plough. In the Middle Ages, the link between his name and the Hebrew word for fish led to his being read as a water god with fish-like features, an interpretation now considered mistaken.

Ancient sources: He appears in cuneiform tablets from Ebla, Mari and Emar, in texts from Ugarit, in the biblical account of 1 Samuel 5, and in the work of Sanchuniathon transmitted by Philo of Byblos.

Trivia

A planet named after him. An extrasolar object, Fomalhaut b, is named in his honour.
A fish god by mistake. The link between his name and the Hebrew word for fish produced the idea of a sea god with a fish body, which specialists consider a misreading.
