Apollo's prophetess at Cumae

Cumaean Sibyl

The Cumaean Sibyl was the priestess who presided over the oracle of Apollo at Cumae, a Greek colony near Naples. For the Romans she was the most famous of the sibyls, largely because of her role in Virgil's Aeneid and the prophetic books she was said to have sold to a Roman king.

The myth

Ancient writers give the Sibyl differing origins. One tradition makes her a native of Erythrae, in Ionia, daughter of Theodorus and a nymph, born in a cave on Mount Corycus, with the gift of prophecy delivered in verse. Her name also varies: Herophile in Pausanias and Lactantius, Deiphobe, daughter of Glaucus, in the Aeneid, and also Amaltheia, Demophile or Taraxandra.

According to the myth, Apollo offered to grant her a wish in exchange for her virginity. She took a handful of sand and asked to live as many years as grains she held, but forgot to ask for eternal youth. Over the centuries she withered until only her voice remained, kept in a jar hung in the temple. Ovid says she lived about a thousand years; other accounts speak of nine human lifetimes.

In the Aeneid, Virgil makes her the guide who leads Aeneas through the underworld, from the lake Avernus, so he can visit his father Anchises. In another story she offered nine prophetic books to Tarquin the Proud, burned three when he refused her price, and in the end sold him the last three at the original figure.

Worship and religion

The oracle at Cumae was a sanctuary of Apollo, with a temple on the acropolis, arranged in a manner said to resemble Delphi. The Sibylline Books were kept in the Temple of Jupiter on the Capitoline Hill and consulted only in emergencies. After a fire in 83 BC a new collection was assembled, and it was destroyed in 405 on the orders of Stilicho.

Legacy

The Sibyl appears in Michelangelo's Sistine Chapel ceiling and in Raphael's frescoes at Santa Maria della Pace. In literature, T. S. Eliot took the epigraph of The Waste Land from Petronius's Satyricon, and Sylvia Plath's The Bell Jar has been linked to the jar in which the Sibyl lived.

Ancient sources: Virgil in the Aeneid and the Eclogues, Ovid in the Metamorphoses (Book XIV) and Petronius in the Satyricon.

Trivia

A voice in a jar. According to Ovid, by the end of her life the Sibyl had shrunk so much that only her voice remained, kept in a jar.
Books burned for a price. The Sibyl burned three of the nine books to raise the price, an episode recorded as part of early Roman tradition.
