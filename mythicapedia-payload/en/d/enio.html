Greek goddess of war and the destruction of cities

Enyo

Enyo is a Greek war goddess who regularly accompanies Ares and is best known for her role in the destruction of cities. Closely linked to Eris, she helps explain how the Greeks imagined the terror and tumult of battle.

The myth

The name Enyo, according to ancient writers, is often explained as meaning 'horror' or 'burning', and Homer knows her only through the epithet 'sacker of cities'. Tradition shows her marching with Ares, delighting in the din of combat, sometimes shown splattered with the blood of the fallen and carrying weapons such as swords, spears and helmets.

Homer places her in the Trojan War, while Latin poets have her take part in the war of the Seven against Thebes and in Dionysus's campaign against the Indians. In the battle of Zeus against Typhon, Enyo took neither side: she kept the balance between them, while Eris escorted Typhon and Nike led Zeus.

Her parentage varies from source to source. Hesiod and other early writers barely connect her to any family. Some late poets call her a sister of War, Polemos, and in some versions she is the mother of Enyalius, although in Homer Enyalius is an epithet of Ares rather than a separate god. A scholiast citing Alcman makes Enyo and Ares his parents. Cornutus records an ancient debate over whether she was Ares' mother, daughter or nurse, and suggests her name comes from 'breathing courage' into fighters or, euphemistically, from her gentle nature. In Hesiodic texts she also appears as one of the Graeae, while Tzetzes considers her merely an epithet of Hera.

Worship and religion

At Thebes and Orchomenos a festival called Homoloia was held in honour of Zeus, Demeter, Athena and Enyo. Zeus was said to have received the surname Homoloios from Homoloïs, a priestess of Enyo. In Athens, a statue of Enyo by the sons of Praxiteles stood in the temple of Ares.

Legacy

In Rome her counterpart was Bellona, goddess of war. Some scholars also identify her with the Anatolian goddess Ma. Her name may appear on the frieze of the Gigantomachy altar, though the reading is uncertain.

Ancient sources: Homer mentions her in the Iliad, while Hesiod and later Greek and Latin poets include her in their accounts of war.

Trivia

One of the Graeae. In Hesiodic texts, Enyo is also the name of one of the three Graeae, grey-haired sisters who shared a single eye and a single tooth.
Neutral in the divine battle. According to one poetic tradition, Enyo took no side in the struggle between Zeus and Typhon, instead keeping the two sides in balance.
