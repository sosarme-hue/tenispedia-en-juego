legendary episode in the founding of Rome

Rape of the Sabine women

The rape of the Sabine women is an episode from Rome's founding tradition in which Romulus's followers abduct women from the neighbouring Sabine people so that they can start families. It is one of the most frequently depicted stories of Roman mythology in painting and sculpture.

The myth

According to the tradition recorded by Livy, after Rome's founding its population consisted mainly of men from Latin and other Italic peoples, along with bandits. As the city grew, Romulus worried that a lack of women would doom it, so the Romans sought wives from neighbouring peoples, but the Sabines refused.

The Romans then staged games in honour of Neptune Equester and attracted Sabines and people from nearby towns. During the spectacle, Romulus gave a signal and the Romans seized the women, around thirty according to Livy, and fought the men. Later the Sabines attacked Rome and reached the Capitoline Hill thanks to the treachery of Tarpeia, whom they killed by crushing her with their shields. When the two armies were about to clash, the Sabine women stepped between them so that fathers, brothers, husbands and sons would stop killing one another, and peace was made. Titus Tatius ruled jointly with Romulus until his death.

Legacy

The subject has been frequent in painting and sculpture, especially since the Renaissance, offering the chance to depict an intense battle with semi-nude figures. The scholar Georges Dumézil read it as a founding myth illustrating the integration of the three social functions into a harmonious society.

Ancient sources: Livy tells the story in his history of Rome, while Dionysius of Halicarnassus and Cicero mention it, and Ovid treats it with a focus on desire.

In art

Trivia

Coins of the Social War. Coins depicting the event were minted in 89 BC, during the Social War between Rome and its Italian allies, suggesting the story regained relevance in that context.
