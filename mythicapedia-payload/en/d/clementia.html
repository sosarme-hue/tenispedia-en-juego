Roman goddess of forgiveness and mercy

Clementia

Clementia was the Roman goddess of clemency, forgiveness and mercy. Her cult became tied to Julius Caesar, who made this virtue a hallmark of his political image after the civil war.

The myth

Clementia has no myth of her own; she was an abstraction of a virtue. After the civil war of 49 BC, Caesar became famous for pardoning his rivals, as at Corfinium, and this conduct gave rise to the idea of the clementia Caesaris, a way of winning support.

In 44 BC the Roman Senate dedicated a temple to the Divus Iulius and to the cult of the divina clementia Caesaris, possibly at Caesar's own instigation. In the temple, Caesar and the goddess were shown clasping hands.

Worship and religion

Little is known of her cult. She seems to have been venerated alongside Caesar and the Roman state, and was seen as a desirable trait in a leader. She was opposed to savagery and bloodshed. She was the Roman counterpart of Eleos, the Greek goddess of mercy, who had a shrine in Athens. She is traditionally shown holding an olive branch and a scepter, and leaning on a column.

Legacy

Under Augustus, clementia was one of the four Stoic virtues of the clipeus virtutis, alongside virtue, justice and piety. Cicero praised it in speeches such as the Pro Marcello and the Pro Rege Deiotaro.

Ancient sources: Cicero, in the Pro Marcello, in a letter to Atticus and in the Pro Rege Deiotaro, discusses Caesar's clemency.
