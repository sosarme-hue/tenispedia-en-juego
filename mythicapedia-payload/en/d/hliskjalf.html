Odin's high seat in Norse mythology

Hliðskjálf

Hliðskjálf is Odin's high throne in Norse mythology, from which the god can see and hear across the nine worlds. Its name suggests a seat with a wide view, and it is one of the best-known mythical places in the Eddic poems.

The myth

According to Norse tradition, Hliðskjálf is Odin's elevated seat, from which the god looks out over the nine worlds. In the Poetic Edda, in Grímnismál, Odin and Frigg are sitting there when they see their foster sons Agnarr and Geirröðr: one lives in a cave with a giantess, and the other is a king. Frigg accuses Geirröðr of being stingy and inhospitable to guests. After wagering over whether this is true, Odin sets out to visit the king and settle the matter.

In Skírnismál, Freyr sneaks into Hliðskjálf to look toward Jötunheimr and sees the beautiful giantess Gerðr, with whom he falls instantly in love. In the Prose Edda, Snorri Sturluson mentions the seat several times in Gylfaginning: once as a dwelling, and elsewhere as the throne itself, set in Valaskjálf, Odin's hall, roofed with pure silver. Snorri also says Odin used the throne to find Loki after he fled the killing of Baldr.

Legacy

The name appears in music too: the Burzum album is titled Hliðskjálf. Scholar Rudolf Simek suggests that the throne may have served as a model for the raised platforms called seiðhjallr, linked to the seiðr magic attributed to Odin.

Ancient sources: Poetic Edda (Grímnismál and Skírnismál) and Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda (Gylfaginning).

Trivia

A name that describes its function. Hliðskjálf is usually translated as high seat with an expansive view, a name that sums up its role as a divine lookout.
A possible model for seiðr magic. Although not explicit in any surviving source, some researchers link the throne to the raised seiðhjallr platforms, where seiðr was practiced.
