Taoist deity of Chinese mythology, vanquisher of ghosts

Zhong Kui

Zhong Kui is a Taoist deity in Chinese mythology, traditionally regarded as a vanquisher of ghosts and evil beings. His fierce image later spread to Korea, Japan and Vietnam, and he remains a protective spirit in popular belief.

The myth

According to folklore, Zhong Kui travelled with Du Ping, a friend from his hometown, to sit the imperial examinations in Chang'an. He earned the highest honours, but Emperor Gaozu of Tang withdrew his title of Zhuangyuan because of his disfigured appearance. Furious, he killed himself by repeatedly striking the palace gates, and Du Ping buried him.

At his posthumous trial, the judge of the underworld saw his talent but condemned him to Youdu because of his bitterness. Yama then made him king of the ghosts, charged with hunting and disciplining all spirits. Tradition says he returned to his hometown on New Year's Eve and, to repay Du Ping's kindness, gave him his younger sister in marriage.

The best-known version dates from the Tang era: the ailing Emperor Xuanzong dreamed of two ghosts. One stole a purse from Yang Guifei and the emperor's flute; the other, wearing an official's hat, tore out the thief's eye, ate it, and introduced himself as Zhong Kui. On waking, the emperor recovered and commissioned the painter Wu Daozi to depict the protector.

Worship and religion

During the Tang dynasty it was customary for the emperor to give ministers portraits of Zhong Kui with the New Year almanac, to ward off evil. His exorcistic ritual dance survives across China, especially in Shaanxi. He is venerated at temples such as the Zhong Kui Temple in Guanqiao, Hunan, and at the Shōki shrine in Kyoto.

Legacy

His paintings hung in homes to drive away ghosts, and he remains a popular subject in New Year pictures. The idiom Da Gui Jie Zhong Kui ('use Zhong Kui's name to crush the ghost') describes completing a task in the name of someone more powerful. Some credit Mao Zedong with the first use of the phrase.

Ancient sources: Shen Kuo's Supplementary Notes to Dream Pool Essays (Northern Song) and the Ming anthology Tianzhongji, which gives the story its canonical form.

Trivia

Five bats. Zhong Kui often appears with five bats, which represent the five blessings of fortune.
The sister's wedding. The scene of Zhong Kui marrying off his sister plays on two near-homophones: 'marrying off a younger sister' and 'sending an evil spirit away'.
