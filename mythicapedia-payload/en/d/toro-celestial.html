Bull of Heaven

The Bull of Heaven is a beast from ancient Mesopotamian mythology, fought by Gilgamesh, king of Uruk, and his companion Enkidu. Known from a Sumerian poem and the Epic of Gilgamesh, its story triggers the most famous part of that tale.

The myth

In the Sumerian poem, the goddess Inanna sends the Bull against Gilgamesh, though the reasons remain unclear. The fuller Akkadian version, in Tablet VI of the Epic of Gilgamesh, begins when the hero rejects the advances of Ishtar, Inanna's East Semitic counterpart. Enraged, the goddess demands the Bull of Heaven from her father Anu, threatening to smash the gates of the Underworld and raise the dead to devour the living. Anu reluctantly agrees, and the Bull's first breath opens a pit that swallows a hundred men, while its second traps two hundred more.

Gilgamesh and Enkidu kill it: Enkidu pulls its tail while Gilgamesh drives his sword into its neck. They offer its heart to the sun-god Shamash, and Enkidu tears off the Bull's right thigh and hurls it at Ishtar's face. The victory comes at a cost, as the gods decide one of the two heroes must die. They choose Enkidu, who falls ill and dies. His loss awakens Gilgamesh's fear of his own death, which drives the rest of the epic.

Legacy

The Bull is linked to the constellation Taurus, and some Assyriologists suggest the thigh thrown at Ishtar may explain why the constellation seems to lack its hind quarters. Flinging a bull's leg as an insult appears across much of the ancient Near East and recurs in the Odyssey. Walter Burkert connects Ishtar's appeal to Anu with a passage in the Iliad, and argues that Dione is a calque of Antu.

Ancient sources: The Sumerian poem Gilgamesh and the Bull of Heaven and Tablet VI of the Standard Babylonian Epic of Gilgamesh; later parallels in the Iliad and the Odyssey.

Trivia

An ancient insult. Hurling a bull's leg at someone was an insult spread across the ancient Near East, and it reappears in the Odyssey.
An echoing name. According to Burkert, Dione, Aphrodite's mother in the Iliad, would be a calque of Antu, Ishtar's mother in the Epic.
Seven years of famine. The motif of seven years of famine following a hero's death appears in the Ugaritic myth of Aqhat.
