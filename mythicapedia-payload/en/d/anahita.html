Persian goddess of the waters, fertility and wisdom

Anahita

Anahita is a Persian goddess of the waters, associated with fertility, healing and wisdom. She matters because she shows how a deity of Indo-Iranian origin was reshaped within Zoroastrianism and the Persian Empire, eventually merging with a goddess identified with the planet Venus.

The myth

According to the Avesta, the goddess is called Aredvi Sura Anahita, which can be rendered as 'the Moist, the Mighty, the Undefiled'. In the cosmology of the Aban Yasht and Yasht 5, she is the source of all the waters of the world created by Ahura Mazda. Her spring lies at the top of a mountain, Hara Berezaiti, and from there her waters, warm and clear, flow down through a hundred thousand golden channels to a lake. One portion falls as rain over the whole earth, and another flows into Vourukasha, the great sea on which the world rests.

In the Aban Yasht she appears as a beautiful, strong maiden clad in beaver skins, who drives a chariot drawn by four white horses, symbols of wind, rain, cloud and sleet. Ahura Mazda entrusted her with watching over creation, and the supreme god himself prescribed her worship to Zoroaster. Warriors prayed to her for victory, and she is linked with horses, chariots, weapons and household goods.

Her origin is debated. She is thought to have developed from an Indo-Iranian divinity of the mythical river, and some scholars, such as Herman Lommel, proposed that her original name was Sarasvati, the Vedic goddess of rivers. In the Achaemenid period she merged with a western Iranian goddess known to the Greeks as Anaitis, who had taken on traits of Ishtar and was identified with Venus. Herodotus reports that the Persians learned from the Assyrians and Arabians to sacrifice to the 'Heavenly Goddess', who was probably Anahita or her predecessor.

Worship and religion

Her followers gathered at her temple for the most important assemblies, and the most beautiful young women were dedicated to her. At her festival, men and women drank until they were intoxicated. Temples were built beside streams and other watercourses. Her cult was especially important during the Persian Empire, above all under Artaxerxes II, during whose reign numerous statues and temples were raised in her honour. It spread to Armenia and Asia Minor and persisted under the Parthians. The early Sasanian rulers were deeply devoted to her, and according to Mary Boyce the veneration of fire in Zoroastrianism may have begun as a reaction against her cult statues.

Ancient sources: The Avesta, especially the Aban Yasht and Yasht 5; Herodotus mentions her when describing the Persians' sacrifice to the 'Heavenly Goddess'.

Trivia

A river's name. Her name is the same as that of a mythical river, which suggests that she began as two different deities that later became one.
Priests and kings. According to the sources, the heads of the Sasanian dynasty were originally priests of Anahita before becoming rulers of Iran.
