Greek personification of the evening star

Hesperus

Hesperus is the personification of the evening star, the planet Venus seen after sunset, in Greek mythology. His story shows how the Greeks came to recognise two apparently different stars as a single celestial body.

The myth

According to the myths, Hesperus was a son of the dawn goddess Eos, although accounts differ about his father. Some say he was the son of the mortal Cephalus, while others make him the brother of Atlas. The earliest sources, however, describe the stars in general as born of Astraeus and Eos, without individualising them.

Hesperus is the half-brother of Phosphorus, the morning star, though some authors regard the two as different names for the same figure. He is credited with children such as Ceyx and Daedalion, and in some traditions he is also called the father of the Hesperides. Servius tells that he lived on Mount Oeta in Thessaly and loved the young Hymenaeus, who sang at the wedding of Dionysus and Ariadne.

Palaephatus offered a rationalised version in which Hesperus was a man from Miletus in Caria who had two daughters known as the Hesperides.

Legacy

The names Hesperus and Phosphorus were used to translate the Hebrew Helel in the Septuagint and the Vulgate, giving rise to the Latin name Lucifer. In the philosophy of language, the sentence "Hesperus is Phosphorus" became famous: Frege used it to illustrate the difference between sense and reference, and Saul Kripke used it to argue that a necessary truth can be known empirically.

Ancient sources: Servius, in his commentaries on Virgil's Eclogues, is the source that places Hesperus on Mount Oeta and links him with Hymenaeus.

Trivia

One star, two names. The Greeks called the morning star Phosphorus or Eosphorus and the evening star Hesperus, without at first realising they were the same planet, Venus.
Lucifer in the Vulgate. The Latin name Lucifer, meaning bearer of light, comes from the translation of Phosphorus and was used in Jerome's Vulgate to render the Hebrew Helel.
