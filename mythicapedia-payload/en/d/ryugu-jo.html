undersea palace of the dragon god Ryūjin in Japanese folklore

Ryūgū-jō

Ryūgū-jō is the undersea palace of Ryūjin, the dragon god of the sea, in Japanese tradition. It is best known from the tale of Urashima Tarō, who believes he has spent only a few days there and returns to find that centuries have passed.

The myth

According to the legend, the palace lies beneath the sea, and its appearance varies between versions: sometimes it is built of red and white coral, sometimes of solid crystal. Its residents are sea creatures who serve Ryūjin, and each of its four sides shows a different season, so that one year inside equals a century outside.

The most famous story is that of Urashima Tarō, a young fisherman who rescues a turtle and is invited to the palace, where he spends what feels like three years. Back on land he discovers that more than three hundred years have passed, or seven hundred in medieval versions. In the earliest narratives, the place is not under the sea at all but on the floating island of Hōrai.

Ancient texts also describe the palace of the sea god Watatsumi, called in the Kojiki a 'palace of fish scales'. There the story of Hoori, younger brother of Hoderi, unfolds: he visits the sea god's realm, returns victorious and becomes an ancestor of the imperial line.

Worship and religion

In Ryukyuan religion, Ryūgū-jō is the source of the fire that lights the hearths of every family and village. On Cape Nagasakibana in southern Kagoshima stands Ryūgū Shrine, which takes its name from the palace and which tradition links with the place from which Urashima Tarō set out. Local people honor Ryūjin and, as protectors, turtles.

Legacy

Katase-Enoshima Station in Fujisawa, Kanagawa Prefecture, was built to resemble the palace. The giant oarfish of deep waters bears the name Ryūgū-no-tsukai, 'messenger of Ryūgū', and this real fish may have inspired the Edo-period 'prophet beast' that carries the same name.

Ancient sources: The Kojiki (8th century) mentions the palace of Watatsumi, the 'palace of fish scales', and the Nihon shoki records Urashima's disappearance.

In art

Trivia

A station shaped like a palace. Katase-Enoshima Station in Fujisawa has a design reminiscent of Ryūgū-jō, although it is a modern building inspired by the legend.
Three hundred years or more. The Mizukagami (1195) gives a precise date for Urashima's return, in 825, 347 years after his departure.
