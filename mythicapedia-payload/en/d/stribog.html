Slavic god of winds, sky and air

Stribog

Stribog is a god of East Slavic mythology associated with the wind, the sky and the air. His name appears in three East Slavic sources, and scholars have long debated what it means.

The myth

The ancient sources say very little about Stribog: they do not describe his deeds, his family or his functions. He first appears in the Primary Chronicle, written in the 12th century, which lists him among the gods for whom Vladimir the Great ordered statues to be erected.

In The Tale of Igor's Campaign, the winds are called Stribog's grandsons, the only family tie attributed to him in the texts. A work attributed to John Chrysostom also mentions him.

For this reason, the image of Stribog as lord of the winds comes mainly from these brief poetic mentions and from modern interpretations. Some scholars see him as a distributor of riches, complementary to Dazhbog, the giver of riches.

Worship and religion

The sources do not say how he was worshipped or what rites, temples or offerings belonged to him. It has been suggested that his cult may also have existed in Poland, but there is no conclusive evidence. Today he is mainly interpreted as a wind deity, a modern reading not confirmed by ancient texts. His cult does not survive as such.

Legacy

After Christianization, the name survived mainly in place names. It appears in a place in Novgorod Governorate, in a stream and a river in the Kiev region, in a village in Zhytomyr Oblast, and in the Polish village of Strzyboga. In Poland the Striboc stream near Tczew is also recorded in 1282. According to Michał Łuczyński, this persistence shows that the Slavs knew the meaning of the name.

Ancient sources: The Primary Chronicle (12th century), The Tale of Igor's Campaign and a work attributed to John Chrysostom, all from the East Slavic sphere.

Trivia

Grandsons of the wind. In The Tale of Igor's Campaign the winds are called Stribog's grandsons, one of the few family references we have for this god.
A name on the map. After Christianization, the name of Stribog was kept in several names of streams, rivers and villages in Ukraine, Russia and Poland.
An Indian parallel. Roman Jakobson compared Stribog with Vayu, the Hindu wind god, and cited a line from the Avesta about the one who spreads.
