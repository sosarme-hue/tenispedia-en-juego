Germanic goddesses of victory and justice

Alaisiagae

The Alaisiagae were a group of Germanic goddesses worshipped by auxiliary soldiers in Roman Britain. They were linked to victory, justice and fertility, and their cult is known almost only from votive inscriptions.

The myth

Ancient texts say very little about them, so their mythology is reconstructed from their names and the inscriptions. They appear in pairs: Beda and Fimmilena, and Baudihillia and Friagabis. Some scholars think Beda and Fimmilena watched over the Frisian judicial assemblies, the thing, while Baudihillia, whose name evokes battle, may be linked to the princess Beadohild of the Norse sources. Others see them as war goddesses akin to the valkyries, though this view is disputed.

Worship and religion

Their best-known shrine was a small apsidal building with a sacred well, built in the early third century beside the fort of Vercovicium on Hadrian's Wall and dedicated to Mars Thincsus and the goddesses. It was in use until the fourth century. Their cult does not survive today.

Ancient sources: They are not mentioned in ancient literary texts; they are known from Latin votive inscriptions found at Housesteads that name Beda and Fimmilena and Baudihillia and Friagabis.

Trivia

A shrine over a workshop. The shrine was built on the ruins of a Roman workshop destroyed during the barbarian incursions of AD 196.
A misidentification. In 1962, Robin Birley mistakenly identified a fourth-century roundhouse as the temple of Mars Thincsus. The actual shrine was a different building.
