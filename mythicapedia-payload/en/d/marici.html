Buddhist goddess of light and dawn

Mārīcī

Mārīcī (Marishiten in Japanese) is a Buddhist deva and bodhisattva linked to light, dawn and the Sun. Her cult, rooted in India, spread to Tibet, China and Japan, where she became a protector of warriors.

The myth

According to Buddhist tradition, Mārīcī is the queen of Heaven and dwells in one of the stars of the Big Dipper. Her earliest images from India, such as those at Salihundam in Andhra Pradesh, show her on a chariot drawn by seven horses, much like the sun god Surya. Mahayana texts present her as the goddess of dawn, introduced by the Buddha at Shravasti. Scholars think she is a blend of solar goddesses from India and Iran, and may derive from the Vedic dawn goddess Uṣas.

Her forms vary widely. She may have one head or up to six, one of them shaped like a boar, and she rides a charging boar or sow, or a fiery chariot pulled by seven boars. In the fiercest East Asian forms she wears a necklace of skulls, and in other images she sits on a lotus. In Tibet she is honored as goddess of dawn or light, a healer and a guide of all beings toward enlightenment.

Worship and religion

In Japan the Shingon and Tendai schools adopted her, and from the 8th century samurai invoked her at sunrise to secure victory, while martial schools venerated her as a guardian of their lineage. In China she is celebrated on the ninth day of the ninth lunar month and is enshrined in temples beside the other devas.

Legacy

Her figure passed into ninja lore, linked to illusion and invisibility, and in the Edo period she was also worshipped as a goddess of wealth and prosperity. Chinese Esoteric Buddhism identified her with the Taoist goddess Doumu.

Ancient sources: Mahayana texts mention her as the goddess of dawn, and her roots reach back to the Vedas through the goddess Uṣas; three works in the Tibetan Kangyur are devoted to her ritual practice.

Trivia

God or goddess. In some regions of East Asia she is depicted as a male god, revered among the warrior class.
A rejected Christian hypothesis. Some colonial-era writers suggested, on phonetic grounds, that she was inspired by the Virgin Mary, but the idea was rejected after older artworks and texts were found.
