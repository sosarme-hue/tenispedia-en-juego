Greek goddess of the day

Hemera

Hemera is the personification of day in Greek mythology, a primordial goddess who stands for the bright time that follows the night. Her Roman equivalent was Dies, though Dies had a genealogy of her own.

The myth

In Hesiod's Theogony, Hemera was the daughter of Erebus, Darkness, and Nyx, Night, and the sister of Aether, the bright upper sky. As goddess of day, she left Tartarus just as Nyx entered it, and when Hemera returned, Nyx went out again. Day and night thus took turns without ever meeting.

Other authors gave different origins. Bacchylides made her the daughter of Chronus, Time, and Nyx, and in a few rare versions she was the daughter of Helios, the Sun, by an unknown mother. In the Titanomachy, a lost epic poem, she may have been the mother of Uranus by Aether. Euhemerus of Messene explained the Greek word brotós, "mortal," as coming from a man named Brotos, who in Hesiod was the son of Aether and Hemera.

Over time Hemera was often confused with Eos, the Dawn. Pausanias describes images in Athens and Amyclae in which Cephalus is carried off by Hemera, and at Olympia a pedestal showed her pleading with Zeus for the life of her son Memnon, a role that Hesiod gives to Eos. A scholiast on the Odyssey records that, according to Euphorion, Hemera fell in love with Orion and carried him away, whereas Homer credits that abduction to Eos.

Worship and religion

There is little evidence that Hemera received a cult in ancient times. The clearest exception is a small shrine dedicated to her and to Helios on the island of Kos, whose archaeological remains point to a shared veneration with the sun god.

Legacy

Rather than a modern afterlife, the sources record her presence in ancient art: the reliefs and pedestals Pausanias described showed her in scenes with Eos, Cephalus, or Memnon. Her Roman counterpart, Dies, had her own genealogy in Rome; according to Hyginus, she was born of Chaos and Caligo, Mist, alongside Nox, Erebus and Aether.

Ancient sources: Hesiod's Theogony is the main source. Bacchylides, Pausanias, a scholiast on the Odyssey and Euhemerus also mention her, and she may have appeared in the lost Titanomachy.

Trivia

A shared shrine. Archaeological remains of a small shrine on the island of Kos show that Hemera was worshipped alongside Helios, the sun god.
Another name for day. Some sources also give her the name Amar, another Greek word for the day.
Dies, a different genealogy. Her Roman equivalent, Dies, had a different ancestry: according to Hyginus she was the daughter of Chaos and Caligo, and according to Cicero she was the mother of Caelus with Aether.
