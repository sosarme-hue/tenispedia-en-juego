Mesopotamian goddess, consort of Anu

Antu

Antu, also spelled Antum, is a Mesopotamian goddess, the wife of the sky god Anu. She was a minor deity for much of Mesopotamian history, but in Uruk, during the Achaemenid and Seleucid periods, she came to hold a prominent place beside her husband.

The myth

According to the texts, Antu was the partner of Anu, and some traditions identify her with Ki, the first consort of An. Other theories equate her with Nammu, which would make her the mother of Enki. Together with Anu she was said to have produced the Anunnaki, gods or demigods, the Igigi, lesser gods, and the utukku, demons of the underworld.

In the Akkadian poems Enuma Elish and Atrahasis, and in the Sumerian Ziusudra, she joins Enki in creating humankind, shaping clay mixed with the blood of a god. Over time, in Akkad and later in Babylon, she lost prominence, and her cult was taken over by Ishtar or Inanna, whom some accounts make her daughter.

Worship and religion

In Uruk, the city of Anu, a temple called Bīt Rēš, meaning the foremost temple, was built in the Achaemenid and Seleucid periods, where she shared a sanctuary with her husband. Her cella was called Egašananna, the house of the lady of heaven. She appears in incantations against illness and demons, and alongside Anu in the anti-witchcraft ritual Maqlû.

Legacy

Seleucid astronomers identified Anu and Antu with a pair of circumpolar stars called the Great Anu and Antu of Heaven, though such references appear only in texts from Uruk. Erica Reiner suggested that the Great Antu may have been one of the stars of Ursa Major.

Ancient sources: Enuma Elish, Atrahasis, Ziusudra, Maqlû, the god list An = Anum, and Seleucid-period texts from Uruk.

Trivia

Goddess of the earth. Some sources identify her with the earth rather than the sky. Thorkild Jacobsen proposed that rain came from her breasts, imagined as clouds.
Identified with Tiamat. An Assyrian text calls her «Antum who makes offerings for the dead to Anu», and some theological commentaries link her with Tiamat.
