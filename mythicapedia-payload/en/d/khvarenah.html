Persian god of glory and royal fortune

Khvarenah

Khvarenah (xvarənah) is, in Zoroastrianism, the power of glory, splendor and good fortune, personified as a yazata. It was the divine force that legitimized Iranian kings and could abandon a ruler who had become unworthy.

The myth

According to the Avesta, specifically Yasht 19, khwarenah takes two forms: the kind that belongs to the gods, which lets them create and preserve the world and accompanies kings and heroes, and another that gods and heroes pursue tirelessly. Ahura Mazda tells Zoroaster that every mortal should seek it to achieve success.

The legend of Jamshid shows how it can pass from one possessor to another: when he strayed from righteousness, his glory left him in the form of a bird.

Worship and religion

As a yazata, it was a being worthy of worship in Zoroastrianism, and its image appeared on Kushan coins as a tutelary divinity of kings.

Legacy

After the fall of the Sasanians, the idea survived in the literature of Islamic Persia as farr-e elāhī, the glory God grants to a sovereign, and it has been invoked in modern Iranian debates about legitimate rule.

Ancient sources: The Avesta, especially Yasht 19.

Trivia

Kushan coins. Kushan kings minted coins showing an armed, radiant deity identified by the Bactrian inscription ΦΑΡΡΟ (Pharro).
A word with descendants. The Persian adjective farrokh derives from farr, the Persian form of the same word.
