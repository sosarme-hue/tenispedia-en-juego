Freyja's boar in Norse mythology

Hildisvíni

Hildisvíni, meaning 'battle boar' in Old Norse, is the boar of the goddess Freyja and also the name of a boar-crested helmet that protected its wearer. It matters because it links one of the great Norse goddesses to the boar's role as a protector. It appears in several ancient sources, and its story connects the world of the gods with that of warriors.

The myth

According to Hyndluljóð, a poem in the Poetic Edda, Hildisvíni was the boar that Freyja rode when she did not use her chariot pulled by cats. In the same poem the goddess helps her loyal servant Óttar trace his ancestry so he can claim an inheritance, and to do so she turns him into her boar.

In the story of Áli, Hildisvíni was his helmet. After the battle on the frozen Lake Vänern, where Áli died, the Swedish king Adils took it. The helmet thus passed from warrior to king. In Beowulf, an Old English poem, a boar figure on a helmet protects the life of the warrior who wears it.

Worship and religion

The ancient Norse saw the boar as an amulet of protection and a symbol of fertility. This belief explains why the animal appeared on war helmets, where it was thought to watch over the wearer. The sources do not document a specific cult of Hildisvíni.

Ancient sources: Hyndluljóð, in the Poetic Edda, and the Old English poem Beowulf, which mentions a boar on the helmet.

Trivia

A stolen helmet. Áli's helmet passed to the Swedish king Adils after a battle on Lake Vänern, in which Áli died.
A boar amulet. The Norse regarded the boar as an amulet of protection and a symbol of fertility.
