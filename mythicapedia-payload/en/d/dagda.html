chief god of Irish mythology

The Dagda

The Dagda, whose name is usually read as 'the good god' or 'the great god', is the chief deity of Irish mythology and one of the leaders of the Tuatha Dé Danann. He is linked to fertility, magic, wisdom and power over life and death.

The myth

Stories present him as a protective father, king and druid, a large bearded man who often wears a hooded cloak. His magic club killed nine men with one blow, while its other end brought the dead back to life. He also owned a cauldron that never ran empty, a symbol of plenty, and the harp Uaithne, which would only play when called by its two secret names.

During the Second Battle of Mag Tuired, against the Fomorians, the harp was stolen. The Dagda went to the enemy camp with Ogma and Lugh and won it back by playing three tunes that filled its captors with joy, sorrow and sleep, allowing them to escape. According to some accounts, before that battle he lay with the war goddess Morrígan on Samhain in exchange for a battle plan. With Boann, goddess of the River Boyne, he fathered a son, Aengus.

After the Tuatha Dé Danann were defeated by the sons of Míl, tradition says they withdrew into the otherworld, to palaces the Dagda had built. Genealogies vary from source to source, and so do the names of his children.

Worship and religion

Medieval sources such as the Cóir Anmann say the Tuatha Dé Danann venerated him as an earth god because of the scale of his magical power. Several Irish tribal groups, such as the Uí Echach, considered themselves his descendants. The texts describe no specific rites and no later cult.

Legacy

Several places in Ireland bear his name or are linked to him, such as Lough Neagh and Brú na Bóinne (Newgrange), where he is said to dwell. Scholars have compared him with Odin, the Gaulish god Sucellos, and Jupiter or Dīs Pater.

Ancient sources: He appears in the Cath Maige Tuired, the Yellow Book of Lecan, the Cóir Anmann, and the Dindsenchas and Banshenchas, medieval Irish texts.

Trivia

A cauldron that never empties. It is one of the Four Treasures of the Tuatha Dé Danann. It was said that no assembly left unsatisfied after feeding from it.
A harp that obeys its names. The harp would only play when called by its two names. According to the text, when the Dagda called it, it flew to him.
