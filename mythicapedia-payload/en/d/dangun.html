legendary god-king and founder of Korea

Dangun

Dangun, also known as Tan'gun Wanggeom, is the legendary founder and first king of Gojoseon, the first Korean kingdom. According to tradition he was the son of Hwanung and a bear-turned-woman, earning him the title "grandson of heaven", and his story gives Korea a mythical origin for its nation.

The myth

The legend begins with Hwanin, the Lord of Heaven, whose son Hwanung longed to live on earth among the valleys and mountains. With three thousand followers he descended to Taebaek mountain, believed to be Paektu, and founded Sinsi, the City of God. Together with his ministers of clouds, rain and wind, he set down laws and moral codes and taught the arts, medicine and farming. Legend credits Dangun with the development of acupuncture and moxibustion.

A tiger and a bear asked Hwanung to turn them into humans. He gave them garlic and mugwort and ordered them to eat only this and stay out of sunlight for a hundred days. The tiger gave up after about twenty days, but the bear persevered and became a woman. Ungnyeo, the bear-woman, thanked Hwanung with offerings, yet she had no husband and grew sad. She prayed beneath a divine birch tree for a child, and Hwanung, moved by her prayers, made her his wife. Their son was Dangun Wanggeom.

Dangun ascended the throne, built the walled city of Asadal near present-day Pyongyang, and named the kingdom Joseon, now called Gojoseon to avoid confusion with the later dynasty. The tale was also used to legitimize the kingship of Gojoseon, and the king performed yearly rituals for his ancestral god.

Worship and religion

The Dongguk Tonggam dates the start of Dangun's rule to 2333 BC, a date that underpinned South Korea's official Dangi era until 1961. Followers of Daejongism and Cheondoism worship him as a deity and mark 3 October as Gaecheonjeol, or National Foundation Day, now a public holiday in South Korea. North Korea treats him as a real person and built a mausoleum near Pyongyang after excavations in 1994.

Legacy

Dangun is the namesake of the second pattern, or teul, in the International Taekwon-Do Federation syllabus, learned for the yellow belt. During the Japanese colonial period he became a symbol of national identity and independence.

Ancient sources: The 13th-century Samguk yusa gives the best-known version, citing the lost Korean Gogi and China's Book of Wei; similar accounts appear in the Jewang Un-gi and the early Joseon annals.

In art

Trivia

A national calendar. Until 1961, South Korea counted its years from 2333 BC, the traditional date of Dangun's reign.
A taekwondo form. The Dangun tul has 21 steps and represents the founder climbing a mountain; students learn it for the yellow belt.
