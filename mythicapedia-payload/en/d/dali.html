Georgian goddess of the hunt and hoofed mountain animals

Dali

Dali is a goddess of Georgian mythology from the Caucasus, the patron of hoofed wild mountain animals such as ibexes and deer. Hunters who respected her many taboos gained success in the hunt, while those who broke them faced her harsh punishment.

The myth

According to the stories of the Svan people of northwestern Georgia, Dali is a woman of extraordinary beauty, nude and golden-haired, whose skin glows. She lives in a cave high in the mountains, from where she watches over the animals on the cliffs, and sometimes takes the shape of her favored beasts, marked in some way to set her apart from the herd.

The tradition holds that Dali takes human lovers and, driven by jealousy, kills them. From these unions comes the culture hero Amirani. Later she clashes with her rival Saint George, who in Christian versions has the power to overrule her. Some tales show her working alongside other forest deities, accompanied by the legendary hunting dog Q'ursha.

After Christianity took hold in Georgia, her importance declined, and she began to be confused with the ali, a malicious nature spirit.

Legacy

Her story remains part of Georgian cultural memory, and she appears in place names, expressions and literary allusions. Some older hunters still regard her as a real presence one might meet deep in the forest, while younger people treat her as a figure of myth.

Ancient sources: She survives mainly in Svan folklore: myths, ballads and round-dance songs, collected in modern fieldwork.
