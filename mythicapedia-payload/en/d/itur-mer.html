tutelary god of Mari in ancient Mesopotamia

Itūr-Mēr

Itūr-Mēr was the tutelary god of Mari, a Mesopotamian city of the Old Babylonian period. He protected oaths and, according to the texts, could also heal the sick and help solve crimes.

The myth

The texts do not tell a full story about Itūr-Mēr, and his origin is debated. His name means something like '(the god) Mēr has turned to me', and some scholars see him as a form of the god Mēr, while others, such as Stephanie Dalley, consider him a deified king or ancestor.

His roles are best documented: guardian of oaths, healer and, according to two texts from Mari, a helper in solving crimes, such as the disappearance of two oxen.

Worship and religion

He was one of the main deities of the Mari pantheon, second only to Dagan. He had a temple in the city, a clergy including a šangû and a muḫḫûm, and laborers who worked the temple's fields. A festival on the twenty-seventh day of the month Lilliātum honored him, and he received six sheep, as did other major gods. His cult is best documented in the time of Zimri-Lim, and there is no evidence it survives today.

Ancient sources: Cuneiform texts from the royal archives of Mari, from the Old Babylonian period.

Trivia

A throne for the god. After conquering Mari, Shamshi-Adad dedicated a throne to Itūr-Mēr and credited the god with his right to rule the city. The inscription does not mention Dagan.
A reclining god. According to one text, the god reclined over a city gate to determine whether messengers from Babylon had caused a slave's disappearance.
