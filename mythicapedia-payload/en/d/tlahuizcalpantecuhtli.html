Aztec god of dawn and the Morning Star

Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli

Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli, "Lord of the Morning Star", is a principal deity of the Aztec pantheon, representing the planet Venus as the morning star. His name combines the Nahuatl words for dawn and lord, and he is closely linked to Quetzalcoatl, with whom he shares the association with the planet.

The myth

According to the Aztec tradition, Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli is a dangerous force linked to dawn. The Annals of Cuauhtitlan tell that the Toltec ruler Topiltzin Quetzalcoatl, after his death, spent four days in Mictlan making darts before rising as the morning star. From there he shot darts that harmed people, and the day of the calendar on which a person was born determined the kind of harm they suffered.

In the Legend of the Suns, Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli grows angry when Tonatiuh, newly created, does not move across the sky. He shoots at the sun with atlatl darts, but misses and is struck by Tonatiuh's darts, and is transformed into Itztlacoliuhqui, god of obsidian and cold. The other gods sacrifice themselves at Teotihuacan so the sun can move, starting the present era.

Other accounts describe him as a skeletal archer and as one of four gods who held up the sky, associated with the east.

Worship and religion

Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli was one of the thirteen Lords of the Day and presided over the trecena that begins with 1 Snake and ends with 13 Movement, where he was paired with Xiuhtecuhtli, the god of fire. His role in worship was tied to the sacred calendar, the Tonalpohualli. The sources give no reliable information about any living worship of him.

Ancient sources: The Annals of Cuauhtitlan recount his origin after the death of Quetzalcoatl, and the Codex Chimalpopoca preserves the Legend of the Suns. Motolinía's Memoriales also relate the transformation of Quetzalcoatl into the morning star.
