Völsung

Völsung is the eponymous ancestor of the Völsung family, a clan of Germanic and Norse myth that includes the great hero Sigurd. According to tradition, he was the son of King Rerir, and his lineage ended up marked by tragedy.

The myth

According to the Völsunga saga, Völsung was the son of King Rerir of Hunaland, a grandson of Odin. Rerir and his queen struggled to have a child, so they prayed to the gods. Frigg gave an apple of fertility to Hljóð, daughter of the jötunn Hrímnir, who reached the queen in the shape of a crow. The king soon died, and the queen remained pregnant for six years until her health began to fail. She ordered that the child be delivered by caesarean section. Völsung was born already full-grown and was able to kiss his mother before she died. He was proclaimed king at once and, once grown, married Hljóð. They had ten sons and a daughter, Signy, the twin of Sigmund, the bravest and most handsome of the boys.

In the great hall Völsung had built grew an enormous tree, the Barnstokkr. During a wedding feast, a one-eyed stranger, who could only be Odin, drove a sword into its trunk and declared it belonged to whoever could pull it out. Only Sigmund managed it. Siggeir, king of the Geats and Signy's husband, offered its weight in gold for the blade, but Sigmund refused, and Siggeir swore revenge on the family. Months later, Völsung and his sons travelled to his kingdom. Signy warned them of an ambush, but they would not turn back. Siggeir attacked, Völsung fell, and his ten sons were taken captive. Sigmund escaped and eventually avenged his father by burning Siggeir in his own hall.

Legacy

The Völsung story inspired the Nibelungenlied and Richard Wagner's music dramas, especially Die Walküre and Siegfried, in which Siegmund draws the sword from the tree. J. R. R. Tolkien retold it in Old Norse verse as the Völsungakviða en nýja, and Arthur Gilchrist Brodeur novelised it in 1925. The name also gave rise to the English place name Walsingham.

Ancient sources: The Völsunga saga and the Poetic Edda, recorded in medieval Iceland; the Old English epic Beowulf refers to him as Wæls.

Trivia

A sword in the tree. According to the saga, only Sigmund managed to pull the sword Odin drove into the Barnstokkr, the great tree in Völsung's hall.
A name linked to horses. Some scholars suggest the original name, Völsi, evoked the fertility of a stallion, based on a tale about a horse phallus preserved in a pagan context.
