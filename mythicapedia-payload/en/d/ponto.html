primordial sea god of Greek mythology

Pontus

Pontus is the primordial god and personification of the sea in Greek mythology. His name was also used as a poetic word for the sea itself, and he is the ancestor of many sea beings, making him the forefather of much of the watery world.

The myth

In Hesiod's Theogony, Gaia gave birth to Pontus alone, without any father, and with her he fathered Nereus, Thaumas, Phorcys, Ceto and Eurybia. Nereus and Thaumas were known as the Old Men of the Sea, while Phorcys and Ceto became the parents of sea monsters. Hesiod portrays Pontus as a rather hostile force, the barren expanse of restless waves, whereas Homer describes him as a vast, boundless realm teeming with life.

Hyginus gives a different account in his Fabulae: Pontus was the son of Gaia and Aether, the luminous upper sky, and his consort is a figure the poem identifies with Thalassa, the sea. With Mare he fathered all the fish. Apollodorus repeats for Pontus the same brood of children found in Hesiod.

Other traditions link him to the primordial conflicts. In a fragment of the lost Titanomachy, Pontus and Gaia are the parents of Aigaion, a sea giant who fought alongside the Titans against the Olympians. Bacchylides attributes the Telchines to the same couple, and some authors believed the Harpies were also born from them.

Worship and religion

The sources do not describe temples or rites devoted to Pontus. His clearest appearance beyond literature is a 2nd-century AD marble statue showing him beside the goddess Fortuna as a patron deity of Tomis, modern Constanța in Romania, which suggests a civic role in the life of that city.

Legacy

In antiquity the Black Sea was sometimes called the Pontus Euxinus, a direct echo of the god's name. On the Roman mosaic from Mérida, dating to around the late 2nd or early 3rd century AD, Pontus appears among cosmogonic figures, though only fragments of his body survive, including his feet and one hand. Scholars see him there as a navigable, favourable sea tamed by Roman power.

Ancient sources: Hesiod's Theogony, the Homeric poems, Apollodorus' Bibliotheca, Hyginus' Fabulae, and the lost Titanomachy.

Trivia

A mosaic in Mérida. Pontus appears on a polychrome Roman mosaic from Mérida, where only some parts of his body survive, such as his feet and one hand.
A god sharing the sea's name. In Greek, the word pontos simply meant sea, so the god and the sea shared the same name.
