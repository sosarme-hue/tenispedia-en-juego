heroine of Greek mythology, wife of Cupid

Psyche

Psyche is the heroine of a Greek myth, best known through the Roman writer Apuleius, who made her the wife of Cupid. Her story also works as an allegory of the soul, since her name means both soul and butterfly in Greek.

The myth

According to the tradition, Psyche was the youngest of three daughters of a king in Anatolia, and her supernatural beauty stirred the envy of Venus. The goddess ordered her son Cupid to make Psyche fall in love with a hideous man. An oracle at Miletus advised her father to leave her on a mountaintop, from where the west wind Zephyrus carried her to a valley and a marvellous palace served by invisible voices. Each night an unseen husband visited her and forbade her to look at him.

Urged on by her sisters, who envied her fortune, Psyche lit a lamp while he slept and discovered that he was Cupid. A drop of hot oil fell on him, and he woke and fled. Furious, Venus hunted Psyche down and set her four nearly impossible tasks: sorting a heap of mixed seeds, helped by ants; fetching golden wool from fierce sheep, guided by a reed; filling a jar with water from a spring guarded by serpents, helped by an eagle; and going down to the Underworld to bring back a little of Proserpina's beauty.

In the last task, Psyche opened the box she had been given, and a sleep-like vapour overcame her. Cupid, who had forgiven her, woke her and asked Jupiter to make her immortal. Reconciled, the couple married and had two daughters, Voluptas and Hedone, symbols of delight and pleasure.

Worship and religion

The sources describe no cult of her own. Apuleius tells that some mortals worshipped Psyche as Venus or as a new earthly version of her, neglecting the goddess's temples and provoking Venus's anger.

Legacy

Psyche became a favourite subject in art. Pompeian painting shows her as a winged young woman, like a butterfly, playing with the Loves, who are winged too. Her image as a winged girl became one of the most common ways of picturing the soul.

Ancient sources: Apuleius, Metamorphoses (The Golden Ass), in the 2nd century CE, the fullest version of the myth; Homer uses the word psykhē for the souls of the dead.

Trivia

Soul and butterfly. In Greek, psyche means both soul and butterfly, which is why she is often shown with butterfly wings.
Pompeian painting. Pompeian painting shows her as a winged young woman playing with the Loves, who are winged like her.
