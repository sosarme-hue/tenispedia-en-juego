Norse goddess, wife of Odin

Frigg

Frigg is the wife of Odin and queen of the Æsir in Norse mythology. Goddess of the home, marriage, childbirth and prophecy, she is one of the principal goddesses of the Eddas, alongside Freyja, and her name gave rise to the English word Friday.

The myth

According to the Eddas, Frigg is the mother of Baldr and Höðr, and also of Hermóðr. She is stepmother to Heimdall, Týr, Vidar, Váli, Thor and Meili. Her origins are unclear: the Skáldskaparmál presents her as the daughter of Fjörgynn, but the word mær can mean both 'daughter' and 'wife', and some read Fjörgynn as another name for Odin. She lives in Fensalir, the 'Hall of the Marsh'. Alongside Odin, she is the only one allowed to sit on the throne Hliðskjálf and look out over the nine worlds, and although she has the gift of prophecy, she rarely reveals what she knows.

The best-known story is the death of Baldr. Worried by her son's prophetic dreams, Frigg made all things swear not to harm him. She overlooked mistletoe, which seemed too young to swear. Loki, disguised as a woman, coaxed this secret out of her, made a mistletoe dart and placed it in the hands of the blind Höðr, guiding his throw. Baldr died. Odin sent Hermóðr to bargain with Hel, the goddess of the dead: Baldr would return if all things wept for him. Nearly everything wept, but Loki, in the shape of a giantess, refused, and Baldr never came back. According to some versions, mistletoe became sacred to Frigg from that point on.

Other tales show her in a lighter mood. In the Saga of the Ynglings, Odin's brothers Vili and Ve divided his possessions during his long absence and even tried to divide Frigg, until Odin returned. Loki accuses her of infidelity in Lokasenna, though scholars note that this poem was meant to be humorous. In the Historia gentis Langobardorum, Frea, identified with Frigg, settles a war by turning Odin's bed so that, on waking, he sees the Lombard women with false beards.

Worship and religion

Sources describe few temples or festivals dedicated to Frigg. The earliest hint is the Second Merseburg Incantation, a tenth-century charm that invokes Frija to heal a horse. Today she receives veneration in Germanic Neopaganism.

Legacy

Her name survives in the English word Friday, meaning 'day of Frigg'. Some have linked Frau Holle, a figure of German folklore, to the goddess, though the connection is disputed. The constellation Orion was known as Frigg's spindle. Many English translations render her name as Frigga, a spelling that also distances it from a vulgar word.

Ancient sources: The Eddas (Völuspá, Grímnismál, Lokasenna, Baldrs draumar), Gylfaginning, the Saga of the Ynglings, Historia gentis Langobardorum and Gesta Danorum, as well as the Second Merseburg Incantation.

Trivia

Friday. The English weekday Friday comes from Old English Frīġedæġ, meaning 'day of Frig', a Germanic version of the Roman day of Venus.
Frigg's spindle. The constellation Orion was known as Frigg's distaff or spindle, though sometimes it was assigned to Freyja.
