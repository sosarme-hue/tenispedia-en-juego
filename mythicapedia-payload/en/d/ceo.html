Titan of Greek mythology, father of Leto and Asteria

Coeus

Coeus is one of the twelve Titans of Greek mythology, the son of Uranus (Sky) and Gaia (Earth). He barely appears in the stories, but he matters as the father of Leto, mother of Apollo and Artemis, and of Asteria, mother of Hecate.

The myth

According to tradition, Coeus was a son of Uranus and Gaia, and he married his sister Phoebe, with whom he had several daughters. Hesiod's Theogony describes this union and tells how Phoebe, joined with Coeus, bore Leto of the blue veil, ever gentle, and the famous Asteria.

Leto was loved by Zeus, and from their union the twins Apollo and Artemis were born. Asteria, also pursued by Zeus, escaped him; with Perses she became the mother of Hecate. Ovid, for his part, wondered who this Coeus was, whose daughter Leto was, which shows how little was said about him.

When Zeus and the Olympians overthrew the Titans in the Titanomachy, Coeus was imprisoned in Tartarus. A later version says that, driven mad, he broke his bonds and tried to escape, but Cerberus drove him back. Other late authors confused him with a giant or imagined him as the son of other primordial deities.

In rationalizing versions, Tacitus says Coeus was the first inhabitant of the island of Kos, birthplace of Leto, and Pausanias mentions streams and a village bearing his name that may have recalled local heroes.

Legacy

The name of Coeus is linked to the Greek koeîn, meaning to perceive or understand, which led Cornutus to make him a Titan of intelligence. His figure matters more for his descendants than for himself.

Ancient sources: Hesiod's Theogony names him as the father of Leto and Asteria; Ovid, Tacitus, Pausanias, Hyginus and Cornutus mention him in various versions.

Trivia

A Titan of intelligence. Cornutus linked his name to the Greek verb koeîn, meaning to perceive or think, and so saw him as a personification of intelligence.
A father of gods with no spotlight. Although he hardly acts in the stories, he is the grandfather of Apollo, Artemis and Hecate, far better-known figures.
