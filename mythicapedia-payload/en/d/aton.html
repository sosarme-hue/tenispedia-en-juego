Egyptian god of the solar disc

Aten

Aten was the solar disc of the sky in ancient Egyptian religion and was regarded as the spirit that gave life to the Earth. He rose to prominence under the pharaoh Akhenaten, who made him the sole god of the official state cult, an exceptional episode in Egyptian religious history.

The myth

According to tradition, Aten was the life force that animated everything on Earth and the disc that crossed the sky each day. Some early accounts of Egypt's creation name him the supreme creator, yet the surviving sources give him no creation story or family of his own, unlike many other Egyptian deities. His character is therefore defined mainly by his role as the light and energy of the world. He was linked to Ra, in the form of Ra-Horakhty, and in the early years of the Amarna period also to Shu, as a symbol of light.

In the Amarna period Aten was described as a god of infinite goodness who upheld maat, justice and cosmic order, and who favoured all people equally. The pharaoh was his envoy and his prophet on earth, the only one worthy of immortality. Akhenaten presented himself as the son of Aten and took the title Ua-en-Ra, 'One in Ra', or 'His body is Aten'. In his nocturnal form Aten was identified with Thoth and called 'Silver Aten'.

The Great Hymn to the Aten, written by Akhenaten and carved in the tomb of Ay, describes the god rising on the eastern horizon and filling the world with light and life, while his setting leaves the land in darkness. Animals, plants and people all respond to his cycle, and the hymn is considered one of the most beautiful literary works of Egyptian culture.

Worship and religion

Worship of Aten dates back to the New Kingdom, when Thutmose IV and Amenhotep III already venerated him. Under Amenhotep IV, who renamed himself Akhenaten, he became the official and preferred cult, in practice almost exclusive. Its main centre was Akhetaten, modern Amarna, though other sites have been found at Thebes and Heliopolis. Aten's temples were open to the sky so sunlight could enter, and no statues of the god were allowed; instead, scenes showed Akhenaten and his family receiving the ankh, the breath of life. Offerings such as fruit, flowers and incense were placed on open-air altars, and on festival days the royal family travelled by chariot rather than in barque processions. After Akhenaten's death the cult was abandoned: Tutankhamun reopened the temples of the other gods and the traditional system returned. Household worship of the older gods probably never fully stopped. The cult of Aten does not survive today.

Legacy

Aten's most lasting presence is in the scholarly debate over whether Atenism was an early form of monotheism, a question that still divides specialists. The Great Hymn is valued as one of the great literary texts of Egypt. The British archaeologist T. E. Peet was highly sceptical of any spiritual idealism in Akhenaten's reform, arguing that it tended instead towards materialism.

Ancient sources: The Great Hymn to the Aten, carved in Ay's tomb; the Story of Sinuhe from the Twelfth Dynasty, which describes the dead king uniting with the sun-disc; and the Book of the Dead, which mentions him.

Trivia

A sphere, not a disc. The scholar Hugh Nibley argued that 'globe', 'orb' or 'sphere' is a more accurate translation of aten than 'disc', since in reliefs the Aten appears curved.
A silver Aten. By analogy with the sun-disc, the moon was sometimes called the 'silver aten', and in its nocturnal form Aten was identified with Thoth.
