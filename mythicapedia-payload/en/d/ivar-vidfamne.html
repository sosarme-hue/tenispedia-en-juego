semi-legendary Scandinavian king, "Ivar the Widefathom"

Ivar Vidfamne

Ivar Vidfamne, "Ivar the Widefathom", is a semi-legendary king of the Norse tradition, the protagonist of medieval Icelandic sagas that credit him with a vast realm over Scandinavia, Denmark and part of England. He matters most for what he reveals about how royal lineages were built in medieval Scandinavia.

The myth

According to tradition, Ivar was the son of Halfdan the Valiant, ruler of Scania, the southern region of modern Sweden that was then part of Denmark. His father was murdered by his uncle Gudröd, and Ivar had to take refuge. Gudröd was soon killed by his queen, Åsa, who had instigated the crime. On his return, Ivar raised an army and marched against Ingjald, king of Sweden and Åsa's father. According to the Ynglinga saga, Ingjald and the queen chose to die by burning themselves and their drunken retainers in the feast hall. Ivar then took Sweden and went back to Denmark.

The sagas later credit him with a remarkable expansion: Saxony, the eastern lands and a fifth of England. His harshness led many Swedes to emigrate west and settle Värmland. According to the Sögubrot, his daughter Auðr married Hrœrekr, king of Zealand, and they had a son, Harald Wartooth. Ivar brought about the death of Helgi, Hrœrekr's brother, but Auðr led the Zealand armies and drove him out. She then married King Ráðbarðr of Gardariki without her father's consent, and Ivar set out with a great fleet to attack him.

Very old by then, Ivar reached the border of Karelia, where he had a strange dream. His foster father Hord interpreted it by likening him to the Midgard Serpent, and the furious king threw himself overboard to reach him. He was never seen again. Hord also vanished in the waves, and it is implied that he was Odin in disguise. The fleet's chiefs sailed home, and Harald Wartooth reclaimed Zealand and Scania. The Hervarar saga, however, does not mention Auðr, but an Alfhild married to Valdar, whom Ivar made subking of Denmark.

Ancient sources: Heimskringla by Snorri Sturluson, the Ynglinga saga, the Hervarar saga, the Sögubrot and the Hyndluljóð of the Poetic Edda.

Trivia

A nickname explained by the sagas. The nickname Vidfamne is tied to his conquering zeal, and the Ynglinga saga cites it as the reason for his epithet, "the long-armed".
An invented ancestor?. The historian Kirsten Møller suggests Ivar may have been a fictional figure, created around Ivar the Boneless to justify his family's dynastic and territorial ambitions.
