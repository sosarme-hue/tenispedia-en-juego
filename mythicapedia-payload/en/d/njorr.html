Norse god of the sea, winds and fishing

Njörðr

Njörðr is one of the Vanir in Norse mythology, a god of the sea, the winds, seafaring and the fertility of the land. He is the father of Freyr and Freyja, and his story is also that of an unhappy marriage to the goddess Skaði, which makes him a key figure in understanding the relationship between the Vanir and the Æsir.

The myth

According to the tradition, Njörðr was made in Vanaheimr, the world of the Vanir, and was given as a hostage to the Æsir when the war between the two peoples ended. There he became part of the family of the gods, although Loki mocks his status as a hostage in the poem Lokasenna, and Vafþrúðnismál foretells that he will return home with the wise Vanir after Ragnarök. His dwelling is Nóatún, a great wooden hall by the sea, from where he rules the winds and the waters. Snorri Sturluson, in the Prose Edda, presents him as a wealthy and prosperous god who is invoked for sailing and fishing.

His son Freyr and his daughter Freyja were born to an unnamed twin sister. The best-known story about Njörðr is his marriage to Skaði, daughter of the giant Þjazi. She came to the gods seeking redress for her father's death, and was allowed to choose a husband by looking only at their feet. She believed she had picked the feet of Baldr, but they were Njörðr's, cleaned by the sea. The couple, however, did not get along: Skaði wanted to live in the snowy mountains of Þrymheimr, while Njörðr wanted to live by the shore. They agreed to spend nine nights in each place, but the howling of wolves and the screeching of gulls kept them awake. In the end Skaði returned to the mountains and Njörðr stayed by the sea.

Worship and religion

The sources do not describe temples dedicated exclusively to Njörðr, although Vafþrúðnismál says he ruled over many temples and hörgr, the Germanic altars. His veneration survived in Scandinavia: in Norway, in the 18th or 19th century, a folk practice was recorded in which people thanked Njor for a good catch of fish. He was also invoked in oaths, such as the 14th-century Hauksbók ring oath, where he appeared alongside two other deities. Today there is no organised cult, only folklore traces.

Legacy

His name appears in many Scandinavian place names, such as Njarðvík in southwest Iceland, or Njarðey, now Nærøya, in Norway. In Old Icelandic translations of classical texts, the Roman god Saturn is glossed as Njörðr. A word for sponge, njarðarvöttr, means «Njörðr's glove». Scholars connect him with Nerthus, the Germanic goddess described by Tacitus, and with the Proto-Germanic *Nerþuz, from which both names derive.

Ancient sources: The Poetic Edda (Vafþrúðnismál, Grímnismál, Lokasenna, Skírnismál and Þrymskviða), Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda (Gylfaginning and Skáldskaparmál) and Heimskringla.

Trivia

Many spellings. The name is written in many ways, such as Njord, Njordr, Niord or Njördr, and in English it sometimes appears as Njorth.
A hostage exchange. According to the Prose Edda, Njörðr was traded as a hostage in exchange for Hœnir in the peace agreement between the Æsir and the Vanir.
