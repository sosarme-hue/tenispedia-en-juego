Sumerian goddess of love and war

Inanna

Inanna is the Sumerian goddess of love, beauty, war and justice, and the patron of the city of Uruk. The Akkadians, Babylonians and Assyrians knew her as Ishtar, and her figure shaped goddesses across the Near East and the Mediterranean.

The myth

According to tradition, Inanna was the daughter of the moon god Nanna (Sin) and Ningal, and the twin sister of the sun god Utu. Her husband was Dumuzi, a shepherd and hero of Uruk. In one of her best-known stories she sails her boat to the Apsu, the home of Enki, god of wisdom. After getting him drunk, she steals the me, the rules that govern civilization, and carries them off to Uruk. In another tale, Inanna tends a tree that grew from a seed at Huluppu, and Gilgamesh frees it from a serpent, a bird and the goddess Lilith.

Her most famous story is her descent into Irkalla, the underworld ruled by her elder sister Ereshkigal. There the judges condemn her and she dies. After three days, Enki sends two sexless beings who revive her with the water of life. To leave, she must leave someone in her place, and she hands over Dumuzi, who spends half the year below while Inanna rules in spring and summer, a cycle that explains the seasons. In the Akkadian version of the Epic of Gilgamesh, the goddess asks the hero to become her consort; when he refuses, she unleashes the Bull of Heaven upon Uruk.

Worship and religion

Inanna had seven temples, to which eight more could be added, and her greatest was the Eanna in Uruk, shared with the god Anu. Her festivals included rites of a sexual and violent character, although the role of prostitution in them is unclear and still debated by specialists. The Assyrians placed her at the top of their pantheon, close to their national god Ashur. Her worship gradually declined between the first and sixth centuries CE, in the wake of Christianity.

Legacy

She was linked to the planet Venus, and the lion and the eight-pointed star remain her most recognizable emblems. The name of her husband Dumuzi evolved into Tammuz. Her image influenced the Ugaritic goddess Ashtart and the Phoenician Astarte, possibly Aphrodite too, and the Hebrew Bible alludes to her.

Ancient sources: Hymns by the priestess Enheduanna, the Akkadian version of the Epic of Gilgamesh, and the Sumerian tales Enki and Inanna and the Descent of Inanna to the underworld.

Trivia

Three forms in Uruk. In archaic Uruk she was worshipped in three forms: morning Inanna, evening Inanna and princely Inanna. The first two partly reflected the phases of Venus.
A goddess of many names. She has a uniquely high number of epithets and alternative names among Sumerian deities, comparable only to Nergal.
