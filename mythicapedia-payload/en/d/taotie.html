gluttonous beast of ancient Chinese mythology

Taotie

The taotie is a creature of ancient China, listed among the four evil beasts of the world in classical texts, and its fierce mask decorated many ritual bronzes of the Shang and Zhou dynasties. Its exact meaning remains debated by specialists.

The myth

In classical texts, the taotie appears alongside Hundun, Qiongqi and Taowu as one of the four evil creatures, opposed to the Four Sacred Creatures. The term first appears in the Zuo Zhuan, where it names a greedy, gluttonous son of the Jinyun clan from the time of the mythical Yellow Emperor.

Other texts, such as Lü Buwei's Lüshi Chunqiu, describe the creature devouring people, although many of the full myths about it have been lost. To hostile clans it symbolized fear and force, while to its own clan it was a symbol of protection.

Worship and religion

Its image appears mainly on dings, ritual bronze vessels used in ceremonies and offerings, which suggests a religious or ceremonial role. It is not known for certain what the masks meant or whether they depicted shamans, god-kings or sacrificed animals.

Legacy

In the 2016 film The Great Wall, the taotie are alien antagonists. A pig named after it appears in DreamWorks' Kung Fu Panda: Legends of Awesomeness, and the Touhou Project video game features a character inspired by it. Jorge Luis Borges included it in The Book of Imaginary Beings as a double-bodied, dog-headed monster representing greed.

Ancient sources: The Zuo Zhuan and Lü Buwei's Lüshi Chunqiu are the ancient sources that mention it, along with the Classic of Mountains and Seas.

Trivia

A creature with no body. According to the Lüshi Chunqiu, the taotie had a head but no body, and when it ate people it did not swallow them but harmed them.
A warning against greed. Some Southern Song scholars linked the motif to Chiyou and saw it as a warning to those who coveted power and wealth.
