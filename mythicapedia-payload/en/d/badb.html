Celtic war goddess, battle crow

Badb

Badb is a war goddess of Irish mythology who can take the form of a crow and sometimes a wolf. Her name means 'crow', and her role as a herald of death makes her one of the most feared figures in the Celtic pantheon.

The myth

In Irish tradition, Badb is a daughter of Ernmas, a goddess linked to the Tuatha Dé Danann. Her role on the battlefield was to sow fear and confusion among soldiers, tipping the outcome of fights toward her favoured side.

In the First Battle of Mag Tuired, Badb fought alongside the Tuatha Dé Danann and, with her sisters, brought down mist and a rain of fire on the Fir Bolg. In the Táin Bó Cúailnge she does something similar against the forces of Queen Medb. After the Tuatha Dé Danann defeated the Fomorians in the Second Battle of Mag Tuired, she sang a song of victory and peace instead of foretelling doom, and then prophesied the end of the world.

The stories also show her as a herald of death. In the Togail Bruidne Dá Derga she appears as an ugly hag who foretells the downfall of Conaire Mór, and in the Togail Bruidne Dá Choca she takes the form of a washer at a ford, a sign of death for Cormac. Her cries warn of heavy slaughter.

Her genealogy varies between sources. According to the Lebor Gabála Érenn she forms with Macha and the Morrígan the trinity of the Morrígna, daughters of Ernmas, and is one of the wives of the war god Neit. Other accounts link her with the Morrígan or Nemain, or make her a wife of the Fomorian king Tethra.

Legacy

Her name is connected with the modern Irish word badhbh, meaning 'hooded crow' and also 'fairy' or 'scold'. Her figure has been compared with the bean-sídhe, the spirit whose wailing was said to foretell deaths in certain Irish families.

Ancient sources: Lebor Gabála Érenn, Togail Bruidne Dá Derga, Togail Bruidne Dá Choca, Táin Bó Cúailnge and the accounts of Mag Tuired.

Trivia

Battlefield. The battlefield was sometimes called 'the garden of the Badb', a image that sums up her presence among the dead.
Washer at the ford. In the Togail Bruidne Dá Choca, the goddess appears washing a chariot at a ford, a scene considered an omen of death.
