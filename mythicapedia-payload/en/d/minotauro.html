Greek mythical creature, part man and part bull

Minotaur

The Minotaur is a creature of Greek mythology with the body of a man and the head and tail of a bull. Born to Pasiphaë after she mated with a bull that came out of the sea, he was shut away in the Labyrinth of Crete, and his story, which links King Minos, Daedalus, Athens and Theseus, made him one of the most recognisable figures of classical antiquity.

The myth

According to the best-known version, Minos, son of Zeus and king of Crete, prayed to Poseidon for a sign of the god's favour. The sea god sent a magnificent white bull, but Minos kept it among his herd and sacrificed another animal in its place. Poseidon took revenge by making Minos's wife, Pasiphaë, fall in a consuming passion for the bull. With Daedalus's help she climbed into a hollow wooden cow covered with real cowhide, and from that union the Minotaur was born.

The creature fed only on human flesh and grew ever wilder, so Daedalus built the Labyrinth of Crete, a tangle of passages in which only one corridor led to the centre, where the monster was left. Meanwhile Androgeus, son of Minos, died in Athens, and after a war Minos forced the Athenians to send seven youths and seven maidens as tribute to feed the beast. Sources disagree on how often this happened: every year, every three years, or every nine years.

Theseus, son of Aegeus, travelled to Crete as one of the victims, or was chosen by Minos, depending on the account. There Ariadne, the king's daughter, fell in love with him and gave him a ball of thread to trace his way back. Theseus killed the Minotaur, but accounts differ on how: with a sword, with his bare fists, or by driving the creature's own horn into it. He then followed the thread out and fled with Ariadne. On the return voyage he forgot to swap the black sail for a white one, so his father Aegeus, seeing the black sail, threw himself into the sea.

Legacy

Greek artists often showed the contest between Theseus and the Minotaur. Ariadne's thread gave its name to a rhetorical figure for a clue that guides you through a tangle, and the story has been retold many times since. Jorge Luis Borges told it from the monster's own point of view in “The House of Asterion” and came back to the tale in the poem “The Thread of the Fable”. Julio Cortázar's play “Los Reyes” presents a gentle Minotaur, with Theseus as the one who destroys him. Carlo Lapucci linked the myth to stories such as Beauty and the Beast. The creature appears in the 1960 film El monstruo de Creta and in Sinbad and the Eye of the Tiger, where it is a bronze automaton. In the video game Age of Mythology it can be used as a unit and summoned from temples.

Ancient sources: Apollodorus, in his Library, gives the fullest version, and the myth also appears in Ovid, Sophocles, Catullus, Pausanias and Isocrates.

Trivia

A shared name. Asterion was also the name of Minos's foster-father, so the monster and that figure shared the same name.
A coin with the labyrinth. A didrachm from Knossos shows the labyrinth on one side and the Minotaur surrounded by small balls, probably stars, on the other, since one of its names means “star”.
