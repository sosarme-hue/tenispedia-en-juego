Greek goddess of the harvest and grain

Demeter

Demeter is the Olympian goddess of the harvest, grain, and the fertility of the earth in Greek religion. Her story of her daughter Persephone's abduction explained the changing seasons for the Greeks and lay at the heart of the Eleusinian Mysteries.

The myth

According to myth, Demeter was the daughter of Cronus and Rhea and the sister of Zeus, Hera, Poseidon, Hades, and Hestia. Her daughter Persephone, also called Kore, was carried off by Hades while she was picking flowers in the meadow of Nysa, a legendary place.

Grieving, Demeter wandered the earth for nine days without eating or drinking, disguised as an old woman. After learning the truth from Helios, she left Olympus and, at Eleusis, became nurse to the infant Demophon; when she was discovered trying to make him immortal, she revealed her identity. In anger she withdrew the earth's fertility, so crops failed, people starved, and the gods received no offerings.

Zeus ordered Hades to return the girl. But Persephone had eaten pomegranate seeds in the underworld, so she had to spend part of each year below. This explained the cycle of the seasons: the earth blooms while mother and daughter are together and withers in their absence. Demeter taught humans to sow grain and, with the heroes Triptolemus, Eumolpus, and Celeus, established the Eleusinian rites.

Worship and religion

Demeter's chief festival was the Thesmophoria, celebrated by women only, and her most important sanctuary was at Eleusis, where the secret Eleusinian Mysteries promised initiates a happy afterlife. Her priestesses were called melissae. Wheat, barley, poppy, and pomegranate were associated with her. In Arcadia she was worshipped as 'Black Demeter'. The Eleusinian cult lasted for centuries until it was suppressed in the late fourth century AD.

Legacy

Her Roman equivalent, Ceres, gave her name to the word cereal and to the dwarf planet Ceres. Her story has inspired painting, literature, and music, and the myth of Persephone remains one of the most cited accounts of why the seasons change.

Ancient sources: The Homeric Hymn to Demeter is the most extensive source for her myth. Hesiod and the Iliad also mention her as a goddess of grain.

Trivia

A goddess with a horse's head. In Arcadia, according to one tradition, Demeter took the form of a mare to escape Poseidon. She was depicted with a horse's head and called 'Black Demeter'.
Many possible etymologies. The origin of the name Demeter is uncertain. Some scholars connect it with the word for earth and others with a word for grain, so it has been translated as 'earth-mother' or 'grain-mother'.
