legendary lawgiver of Sparta

Lycurgus

Lycurgus is the legendary lawgiver of Sparta, credited with much of its constitution and with reforms that shaped a military-oriented society. Whether he was a real person is doubtful, and ancient authors already disagreed about when he lived. He matters as the ideal model of Classical Sparta.

The myth

According to tradition, Lycurgus was guardian and regent for a young king. In Herodotus he receives his laws from Apollo through the Pythia at Delphi; in a second version, based on Spartan traditions, he takes them from the existing laws of Crete. Some accounts add that he travelled as far as Egypt, Spain and India.

The reforms credited to him touch nearly every aspect of Spartan life: the constitution, with the gerousia and the ephors; military organisation; the common messes called syssitia; the redistribution of land; and a discipline of austerity and frugality. Herodotus and Xenophon remember him as the creator of much of this, and Xenophon also ascribes the dual monarchy to him. Most modern historians, however, think these reforms are later, perhaps from the late sixth century BC, and that some, such as the land redistribution, are fictitious.

The most famous ending says that he made the Spartans swear to keep his laws until his return, and that after leaving the city he took his own life so that they could never be changed.

Worship and religion

In the historical period the Spartans honoured Lycurgus as a god. The sources do not describe his temples or festivals in detail, so his cult is best understood mainly as part of the prestige of the institutions attributed to him. There is no reliable evidence about whether it survives today.

Legacy

Plutarch devoted a Life to him in his Parallel Lives, paired with that of Numa Pompilius, the second king of Rome, and compared the two lawgivers. The Argentine president Juan Domingo Perón later presented him as a model of leadership and as the "first justicialist", stressing his land distribution. He is also credited with the saying that what matters in laws is not whether they are good or bad, but whether they are consistent.

Ancient sources: Herodotus, Xenophon, Thucydides and Plutarch mention him; the poet Tyrtaeus, the earliest surviving Spartan source, does not.

Trivia

A disc of Iphitos. According to Aristotle, an ancient disc linked Lycurgus to the founding of the Olympic Games. It is now considered probably a forgery from the fourth century BC.
Two Lycurguses. The historian Timaeus proposed that there were two people with this name: the lawgiver of the reforms and a later one who was present at the first Olympic Games.
