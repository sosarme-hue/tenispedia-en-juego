Japanese badger yōkai with shapeshifting powers

Mujina

The mujina is a yōkai from Japanese folklore, a mischievous spirit that shapeshifts into people and objects. Although the name mainly refers to the Japanese badger, it has traditionally also been applied to the tanuki, a confusion that even reached the courts.

The myth

According to tradition, the mujina has a raccoon-like face on a badger's body. Its transformations seem almost limitless: it may appear as a seductive woman, a one-eyed hag, a tax official, or a black-robed Buddhist priest, often living beneath a temple with an upside-down lotus leaf on its head. It can also take the form of comets, fence posts, stones or trees. Its disguises may betray themselves through a faint glow or dry clothing in the rain, though these signs are unreliable, and the illusion fades if the animal falls asleep.

Stories portray it as a incorrigible prankster with a grateful heart. In one tale, a family of badgers repays kindness by appearing as giant wrestlers to scare off burglars. It becomes dangerous only when angered, for instance by having its den destroyed or its family attacked.

The Nihon Shoki mentions it in 627, describing mujina in Mutsu that turn into humans and sing.

Legacy

Lafcadio Hearn's story "Mujina" in Kwaidan popularised the image of a faceless ghost. In Tochigi in 1924, a hunting case over an animal mistaken for a mujina reached the Supreme Court, which accepted the hunter's confusion. A hot spring in Nasu bears its name.

Ancient sources: The Nihon Shoki (Japanese chronicles of the 8th century) contains the earliest literary mention of the yōkai.

Trivia

A confusing name. The term applies to the Japanese badger, but traditionally also to the tanuki, and even to an introduced palm civet.
A Supreme Court case. In 1924 a hunter in Tochigi believed he had killed a mujina; the court considered his confusion legitimate, though it acquitted him on another ground.
