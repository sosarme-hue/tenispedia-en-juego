Greek and Roman god of prophecy, light and music

Apollo

Apollo is one of the twelve Olympian gods of Greek religion and mythology, the son of Zeus and Leto and the twin brother of Artemis. He combines light, prophecy, music, medicine and poetry, and his cult was taken over by the Romans, who later identified him with the sun.

The myth

According to tradition, Apollo was born on Mount Cynthus on the island of Delos, the son of Zeus and Leto, and the twin of Artemis. Homer and Hesiod already present him as an archer god and as a son of Zeus, although his figure grew richer over time.

One of the best-known stories tells how the god took possession of the oracle at Delphi. According to Apollodorus, the shrine had once belonged to Themis and was guarded by the serpent Python; after killing the monster, Apollo took over the site. Hyginus and Pausanias give different accounts of who owned the oracle before him, showing that the story was not uniform among ancient authors.

As an archer god, Apollo could punish with plague and sudden death. In the Iliad, the pestilence that strikes the Achaean camp is blamed on his arrows. At the same time, he was seen as the god who averts evil and heals, and as the father of Asclepius, the god of medicine. Some ancient authors linked his name to a Greek verb meaning 'to destroy', while modern scholars often connect it with the idea of warding off or protecting.

Apollo was also the god of music: in the Iliad he delights the immortals playing the phorminx, and he is credited with the invention of the lyre. As Apollo Musagetes he led the chorus of the Muses. According to some accounts he also served as a herdsman, tending the flocks of Admetus in Pherae in Thessaly. The Greeks consulted his oracles before founding cities and colonies.

Worship and religion

His two main centres of worship were Delphi and Actium. At Delphi the priestess, the Pythia, gave the god's oracles, and consultations there shaped political and religious decisions across Greece. As Apollo Kourotrophos he protected young people through their passage into adulthood; boys cut their long hair at coming of age and dedicated it to him. Hymns of supplication for health or of thanksgiving for victory were called paeans, from his epithet Paean, 'the healer'.

Legacy

The adjective 'Apollonian' entered modern thought as the opposite of 'Dionysian', a distinction popularised by Nietzsche. NASA's Apollo programme took its name from the god. His image, linked to youthful beauty and the ideal of the kouros, was a constant reference in sculpture and painting, and from the Renaissance he was regarded as the patron of the fine arts.

Ancient sources: Homer (Iliad, Homeric Hymn to Apollo) and Hesiod present him as the son of Zeus and Leto, an archer god and patron of singers. Apollodorus, Hyginus and Pausanias record accounts of the Delphic oracle.

Trivia

Name of a space programme. NASA's Apollo programme, which sent astronauts to the Moon, took its name from the god.
Uncut hair. Young men reaching adulthood cut their long hair and dedicated it to Apollo, who is shown with long hair as a symbol of eternal youth.
