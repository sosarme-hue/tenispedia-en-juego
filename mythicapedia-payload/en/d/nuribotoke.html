lacquered corpse yōkai of Japanese folklore

Nuribotoke

Nuribotoke (“lacquered Buddha”) is a yōkai from Japanese folklore: a black, monk-like corpse with eyeballs dangling from its sockets, said to emerge from household altars (butsudan) that are left open or poorly kept. It appears in emakimono and illustrated catalogues from the Edo period.

The myth

According to tradition, the nuribotoke emerges at night from butsudan left open or neglected. It often takes the form of a deceased family member to frighten the household, or that of a Buddhist monk who hands out false prophecies to worshippers. It is also said to dance in the nocturnal parade of demons, the Hyakki Yakō. Some pictures give it a catfish-like tail or long black hair, suggesting a corpse controlled by another being.

Worship and religion

It has no cult of its own, but the nuribotoke is linked to the care of the family altar: the belief warns that a neglected butsudan may attract its presence.

Legacy

In the nineteenth century, Kawanabe Kyōsai drew an unnamed yōkai with the same look, and in 1929 Fujisawa Morihiko presented it as a “spirit of objects”. Modern scholars often read its dangling eyes as a warning about untidy household shrines.

Ancient sources: The yōkai emaki and Sawaki Suushi’s Hyakkai Zukan, as well as Toriyama Sekien’s Gazu Hyakki Yagyō.

Trivia

A confusing name. Nuribotoke means “lacquered Buddha”, for its glossy black colour and its resemblance to the Buddha, though the word for Buddha can also refer to any deceased spirit.
No original explanation. The old picture scrolls contain no text explaining what their makers intended to show, so the exact meaning of the creature remains uncertain.
