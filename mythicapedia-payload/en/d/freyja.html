Norse goddess of love, beauty and fertility

Freyja

Freyja is a goddess of Norse mythology associated with love, beauty, fertility, war, gold and the magic known as seiðr. A member of the Vanir, she is, together with Frigg, one of the two principal goddesses of the Norse pantheon, and her name means roughly "the Lady".

The myth

According to the Eddas, Freyja is the daughter of Njörðr, king of the Vanir, and the twin sister of Freyr. After the war between the Æsir and the Vanir, which began with the mistreatment of Gullveig, both sides exchanged hostages, and Njörðr was sent to Asgard, where he fathered the two siblings. The sources do not fully agree on her origins: Heimskringla presents her in euhemerist fashion as a princess of Sweden, while the poetic tradition places her directly among the gods. Her mother was Njörðr's twin sister, who is never named in the sources.

Freyja is married to Óðr, a god who is often absent. She weeps tears of red gold for him and searches for him under assumed names. Together they had two daughters, Hnoss and Gersemi. Among her possessions are the necklace Brísingamen, a cloak of falcon feathers that allows her to take bird form and that she lends to other gods, a chariot pulled by cats, and a boar named Hildisvíni. The poem Völuspá calls her Óðr's bride, and Grímnismál says that every day she allots half of the warriors slain in battle to her hall of Fólkvangr, while Odin receives the other half in Valhalla.

The best-known story about the necklace appears in the poem Húsdrápa: Loki steals it, and Freyja asks Heimdall for help. After a long struggle, in which both turn into seals, Heimdall recovers the jewel. Their rivalry foreshadows their mutual killing at Ragnarök. In Þrymskviða, Loki borrows the feathered cloak to search for Thor's hammer, which the giant Thrym has stolen and will return only if Freyja becomes his bride. The enraged goddess makes the halls of the gods tremble, and the necklace falls from her neck. In the end, Thor and Loki disguise themselves as a bride and her maid, trick the giant, and Thor retrieves his hammer. In Lokasenna she trades insults with Loki, and in Hyndluljóð she helps her loyal servant Óttar discover his ancestry.

Worship and religion

Freyja was one of the most venerated deities of Norse religion, alongside Frigg, and her cult seems to have been the more widespread of the two. People invoked her for happiness in love, for help during childbirth and for good seasons. The Saga of the Droplaugarsons describes the temple at Ölvusvatn in Iceland, where statues of Frigg and Freyja stood in the most prominent positions, in front of those of Thor and Freyr, dressed in elaborate cloths and adorned with gold and silver.

With the Christianization of Scandinavia, the kings Olaf I of Norway and Olaf II the Holy destroyed temples and statues of the old gods, and Freyja was demonized along with many elements associated with her. Traces of her faith survived in secret, especially in rural areas. Some plants in southern Sweden kept her name, such as "Freyja's tears" or "Freyja's hair" (Polygala vulgaris), until they were renamed in honour of the Virgin Mary. Rural Scandinavians still regarded her as a supernatural figure into the nineteenth century. Today, various forms of Germanic neopaganism have reconstructed her as a goddess of their faith.

Legacy

The name Freyja survives in modern forms such as Freya, Freyia and Freja, and her figure has inspired many works of art over the centuries. Scholars have debated whether she and Frigg ultimately stem from a single goddess common to the Germanic peoples, and they have connected her with the valkyries, the female choosers of the slain on the battlefield.

Ancient sources: The Poetic Edda (Völuspá, Grímnismál, Lokasenna, Þrymskviða, Hyndluljóð), the Prose Edda of Snorri Sturluson (Gylfaginning and Skáldskaparmál), Heimskringla and the Saga of the Droplaugarsons.

Trivia

Tears of red gold. In the Skáldskaparmál, gold is described with kennings that link it to Freyja's tears, which she sheds for her husband Óðr.
Plants named after her. Some plants in southern Sweden were named after the goddess, such as Freyja's hair, until they were renamed in honour of the Virgin Mary.
