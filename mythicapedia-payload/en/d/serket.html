Egyptian goddess of protection against scorpion stings

Serket

Serket is an Egyptian goddess linked to protection against venomous stings and bites, above all those of scorpions, and to healing their effects. She is usually regarded as originally the deification of the scorpion, although some scholars suggest that her name may refer to a water insect and to breathing instead.

The myth

The accounts disagree about her parentage. In one version she is the daughter of Ra; in another, the daughter of Neith and Khnum and a sister of Sobek and Apep. Some texts make her the mother of Nehebkau, and at Edfu she appears as the wife of Horus or the mother of Horakhty.

Her most distinctive role was funerary. She protected the pharaoh's sarcophagus, appeared as the mother of the deceased, whom she nursed, and guarded the canopic chest that held the jar of Qebehsenuef, alongside other protective goddesses. She was invoked as “she who makes the throat breathe,” since the scorpion's venom causes suffocation, and also as the one who enables the newborn and the dead to breathe in their rebirth.

In the Middle Kingdom, according to the Book of the Two Ways, she guarded a bend in the path of the afterlife, and she was credited with binding the serpent Apep, the enemy of order. In the New Kingdom she appears with Neith in scenes of birth and of the marriage bed. In the myth of Horus's birth, Serket and Nephthys help Isis protect the child from bites, and seven scorpions, said to be emanations of Serket, watch over Isis and her unborn son. Over time she merged with Isis, until she was seen as merely an aspect of her when Isis's cult became dominant. She was also identified with Seshat.

Worship and religion

Serket was worshipped mainly in Lower Egypt, in the Nile Delta, and in places such as Edfu and Per-Serket. Her priests were physicians and magicians who treated bites from venomous animals. Her cult is attested from the First Dynasty and peaked in the Old Kingdom, when she was important to early kings such as Scorpion I and Scorpion II. No temples are known to have been built in her honour.

Legacy

Her image survives in the temple of Edfu, in the tomb of Nefertari, and in a Late Period bronze figure showing her as Isis-Serket. She remains one of the most recognisable Egyptian deities, shown as a woman crowned with a scorpion.

Ancient sources: The Pyramid Texts mention her son Nehebkau, and the Book of the Two Ways places her in the afterlife.

Trivia
