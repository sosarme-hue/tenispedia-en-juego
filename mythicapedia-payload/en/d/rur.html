Norse goddess, daughter of Thor

Þrúðr

Þrúðr is a figure in Norse mythology, the daughter of Thor and Sif, whose name means "strength" in Old Norse. She also appears as one of the valkyries who bring ale to the einherjar in Valhalla, though it is uncertain whether the two are the same figure.

The myth

According to the tradition, Þrúðr was promised to the dwarf Alvíss, who had forged weapons for the gods. Thor, unwilling to accept the match, challenged him to a contest of wisdom and kept him talking until the sun rose. Because dwarves turn to stone when exposed to sunlight, Alvíss was petrified, and the young woman remained unmarried. The sources say little more about the engagement, and the poem Alvíssmál does not name Thor's daughter.

In Ragnarök, according to Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda, Þrúðr survives along with her siblings Lóriði, Magni and Móði, although other texts name Lóriði as a sister. Separately, the poet Bragi Boddason calls the giant Hrungnir the "thief of Þrúðr", a reference that no other surviving text develops.

Legacy

Her name was used in kennings, the metaphorical compounds of skaldic poetry. On the 10th-century Karlevi runestone on the Swedish island of Öland, a chieftain is called the "tree of Þrúðr", and poets such as Ormr Steinþórsson used her name in kennings for women. In English she is sometimes anglicized as Thrud.

Ancient sources: She is attested in the Poetic Edda (Grímnismál, stanza 36, where she is a valkyrie), the Prose Edda (Skáldskaparmál), Bragi Boddason's Ragnarsdrápa, Þjóðólfr of Hvinir's Haustlöng, and the Karlevi runestone.

Trivia

Father of Þrúðr. In the Prose Edda, Thor can be given the poetic epithet "father of Þrúðr", a phrase the poet Eysteinn Valdason used in a poem about the god.
