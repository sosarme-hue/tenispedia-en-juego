Divine Farmer, culture hero and Chinese god of agriculture and medicine

Shennong

Shennong is a central figure in Chinese mythology, also known as the Yan Emperor, and is regarded as both a culture hero and a deity in folk religion. He is credited with teaching the ancient Chinese agriculture and the medicinal use of herbs, and he remains widely venerated in China and among Chinese communities.

The myth

According to legend, Shennong's mother swallowed the vapor of a dragon, and nine days later she gave birth to him on the banks of the Jiang river. He had an ox's head on a human body. He grew quickly, began speaking after three days, and eventually reached over eight feet in height.

In tradition he obtained a mystical herb book from a Taoist master and then travelled across China recording 365 medicinal herbs and fungi. He tested each plant on his own body; his transparent belly let him see the effects, and organs turning black revealed a poison. He is said to have taken seventy poisons in a single day and discovered tea, whose leaves countered their toxins. He eventually died of poisoning, probably from a plant called Gelsemium elegans, though some versions blame a centipede.

He is also credited with teaching people to sow cereals, with inventing the wooden spade and with the calendar. Some accounts make him father or ancestor of the Yellow Emperor, and later historiography presents him as an ancestor of the Chinese peoples, especially the Han. Other traditions identify him with Hou Ji, the mythical sage whose descendants founded the Zhou dynasty.

Worship and religion

Shennong is worshipped as the Medicine King and as patron of farmers, rice traders and practitioners of traditional Chinese medicine. Temples honour him under several names, such as God of the Five Grains or God of the Earth. His birthday falls on the 26th day of the fourth lunar month, marked with incense and fireworks, and pigs and sheep, not oxen, are the acceptable offerings. His cult remains active in temples in China and Taiwan.

Legacy

His name is linked to the Divine Farmer's Herb-Root Classic, considered the earliest Chinese pharmacopoeia, and he is associated with the guqin. In Japan, where he is called Shinnō, he is often shown tasting herbs, a motif frequent in art.

Ancient sources: Sima Qian's Shiji, the Huainanzi and the Lüshi Chunqiu mention him as the head of the Shennong house.

Trivia

Tea from a falling leaf. According to tradition, tea leaves fell into his pot of boiling water and he tasted the drink, discovering tea.
The crystal body. His transparent belly let him observe how herbs acted inside him, and blackened organs signalled poison.
