Japanese yōkai with an ox-like body and claws

Waira

The waira is a Japanese yōkai that appears in illustrated handscrolls and books from the Edo period, such as Sawaki Suushi's Hyakki Zukan and Toriyama Sekien's Gazu Hyakki Yagyō. It has almost no old stories, so much of what is told about it today is modern.

The myth

Old pictures show it with the body of a giant ox and thick, sharp claws on its front legs, but only the upper half survives in the images, so its full appearance is unknown. No ancient text explains what it is or records legends about it.

The Kiyū Shōran, an Edo-period work, mentions a waira in monster paintings attributed to Kōhōgen Motonobu. From the Shōwa period onward, children's books offer later versions: a being of unknown identity, a mountain monster that digs up moles with its claws, or a yōkai that attacks and eats people.

Legacy

The art historian Nobuo Tsuji describes the waira in the Bakemonozukushi as a green monster resembling a transformed toad, and some modern books present it as a toad that has gained spiritual power with age. The researcher Katsumi Tada links its name to wai (畏, fear) and pairs it with the otoroshi. The idea of moles and of males and females having different colours comes from Norio Yamada's children's series Obake Bunko (1976), though Yamada says he took it from a source he has since forgotten.

Ancient sources: Edo-period handscrolls and books such as Sawaki Suushi's Hyakki Zukan (1737) and Toriyama Sekien's Gazu Hyakki Yagyō (1776), where it appears alongside the otoroshi.

Trivia

A renamed creature. In the Bakemonozukushi scroll from Fukuoka, the otoroshi appears under the name ushi-kawazu, or 'ox frog', and is said to live in ponds and eat people, a detail found in no other source.
A modern invention. The image of the waira eating moles, with brown males and red females, originated in a 1976 children's book, according to the researchers Tada and Murakami.
