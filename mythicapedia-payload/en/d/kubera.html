Hindu god of wealth and king of the yakshas

Kubera

Kubera is the Hindu god of wealth and the king of the yakshas, semi-divine beings who guard treasures and forests. He protects the north as a dikpala and serves as treasurer of the gods, which makes him a key figure of prosperity in Indian mythology. His plump body adorned with jewels, together with his money-pot and club, makes him easy to recognise in Hindu, Buddhist and Jain art.

The myth

The origin of Kubera is one of the most debated points. In the Atharvaveda, where he first appears, and in the Shatapatha Brahmana, Kubera, called above all Vaisravana, is the chief of the evil spirits of darkness, and the Brahmana calls him lord of thieves and criminals. According to the Bhagavata Purana he was the son of the sage Vishrava and Ilavida, while the Mahabharata presents him as the son of Pulastya and Idavida and the brother of Vishrava, and the Puranas as Pulastya's grandson.

His status changed over time. The Puranas and the epics make him a deva and a lokapala, a protector of the world. The Ramayana tells that Brahma granted him this office as a reward for a severe penance, along with the riches of the world, equality with the gods and the flying chariot Pushpaka. He ruled the golden city of Lanka, today identified with Sri Lanka, until his half-brother Ravana displaced him, and he moved to Alaka in the Himalayas, whose glory is described in many scriptures.

Another story explains one of his epithets. Kubera looked with jealousy at Shiva and Parvati and lost one eye. Parvati turned the deformed eye yellow, which gave him the name Ekaksipingala. His friendship with Shiva recurs in the texts, and the Harivamsa calls him chief of the yakshas and Shiva's friend.

Worship and religion

Ritual sources treat him with some ambiguity. Early texts describe a being who should receive offerings, but the Grihyasutras of Shankhayana and Hiranyakesin already consider him a god and recommend offering him meat, sesame seeds and flowers. The Agni Purana states that he should be installed in temples seated on a goat, with a club in his hand, and that his image should be of gold. The sources consulted do not detail specific festivals or present-day devotion to him.

Legacy

His figure passed into Buddhism as Vaisravana, a name that keeps his patronymic, and into Jainism, where he is known as Sarvanubhuti and appears as an assistant of an arhat. In Tibet, the mongoose is a symbol of his victory over the nagas, guardians of treasures, and it appears in Buddhist iconography. His attributes, such as the mace, the pomegranate, the money bag or the pot of coins, make him easy to recognise in art.

Ancient sources: The Atharvaveda, the Shatapatha Brahmana, the Manusmriti, the Mahabharata, the Ramayana and the Puranas, among them the Bhagavata Purana.

Trivia

One yellow eye. His epithet Ekaksipingala means "the one with a single yellow eye". According to the story, he lost an eye for looking with jealousy at Shiva and Parvati.
Three legs and eight teeth. Later Puranic texts give him three legs and only eight teeth, features that reflect the deformed meaning of his name.
