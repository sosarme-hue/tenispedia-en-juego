Vedic and Hindu god of fire

Agni

Agni is the Vedic god of fire and, according to tradition, the messenger who carries offerings from humans to the gods. He belongs to the Vedic religion, which predates Hinduism, and his figure has remained part of Hindu rituals to the present day.

The myth

According to the hymns of the Rig-veda, Agni is the mouth of the gods: he consumes the sacrificial offerings and carries them to their recipients. He is conceived on three levels, as fire on earth, lightning in the atmosphere and the sun in the sky, and this triple presence makes him the intermediary between the divine and human worlds. In the Vedic trinity, alongside Indra and Surya, Agni ruled the earth, Indra the atmosphere and Surya the heavens. The texts describe Indra as Agni's twin brother and Surya as another brother.

The Brahmanas and the Kathaka and Maitrayani Samhitas recount his origin. At first there was neither day nor night, only Prajapati, from whose forehead Agni was born; with him came light, day and night. The Shatapatha Brahmana tells that Agni had been four times: the first three fires chosen as hotri priest died, and the fourth, out of fear, hid in the waters until the gods discovered him and drew him out.

Over time his role changed. In the Upanishads he became a metaphor for the immortal principle within human beings and for any energy that dispels darkness and transforms. His epithets reflect these functions: Pavaka, the purifier; Havyavahana, the carrier of sacrificial butter; Saptajihvi, the one with seven tongues; Vaisvanara, the man of the world.

Worship and religion

Agni was the centre of the Vedic sacrifice, or homa, in which clarified butter was poured into the fire as an offering. He remains present in Hindu religious life: he is the central witness of the traditional wedding (saptapadi), the upanayana ceremony marking the passage into adulthood, and he appears in the diya lamp of festivals such as Deepavali and in the arti of puja. As guardian of the southeast direction, he is placed in the southeast corners of Hindu temples.

Legacy

Agni is one of the five elements (pancabhuta) which, together with ether, air, water and earth, make up material existence in classical Hindu cosmology. In Buddhism he is a frequent term in the texts and in the debate over the Senika heresy. In Jainism, the agni-kumaras, or 'fire children', form a class of beings in the theory of rebirth. His Indo-European root survives in words such as Latin ignis, the source of the English word ignite.

Ancient sources: The Rig-veda, with more than two hundred hymns dedicated to him, and the Brahmanas such as the Shatapatha and Taittiriya, along with the Kathaka and Maitrayani Samhitas.

Trivia

Four Agnis. The Shatapatha Brahmana tells how the first three priestly fires died and how the fourth hid in the waters out of fear, until the gods found him.
The heavenly falcon. In many Indo-European myths a falcon brings fire to humans. Some Vedic hymns call Agni the 'heavenly falcon that flies'.
