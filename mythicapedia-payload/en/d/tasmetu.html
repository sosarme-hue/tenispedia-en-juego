Mesopotamian goddess of prayer, spouse of Nabu

Tashmetum

Tashmetum (also Tasmetu or Tashmit) was a Mesopotamian goddess of prayer and the wife of the god Nabu. She was honoured as the "lady who listens" and the "lady who grants requests," and her name comes from the Akkadian verb meaning "to hear."

The myth

The surviving texts tell few stories about her, so she is known mainly through her role. She acted as an intercessor, listening to those who prayed and carrying their requests to the greater gods. She was paired with Nabu, god of writing and wisdom, and in some sources she is also called the daughter-in-law of Marduk. Some traditions link her with love and with the constellation Capricorn.

Her origin is uncertain. Some scholars suggest she began as a deified ancestor, though there is no evidence that she was imagined as a mortal person within her cults.

Worship and religion

Her worship began in Assyria and reached Babylonia in the Old Babylonian period. She was venerated in Sippar and in Borsippa, where she shared a temple with Nabu as a tutelary deity. Hammurabi dedicated red gold and a precious stone to her, and she is thought to have had a place in Marduk's temple, Esagil. No data survives on any modern worship.

Ancient sources: She appears in Akkadian and Babylonian tablets, including the Weidner god list from Tell Taban, a kudurru from the reign of Marduk-apla-iddina I, and a prayer from the reign of Tukulti-Ninurta I.

Trivia

A meaningful name. Her name comes from the Akkadian verb šemû, "to hear," which fits her role of listening to prayers.
A borrowed name. In a Hittite text her name is used as a logogram for an unidentified deity.
