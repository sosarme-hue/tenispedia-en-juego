Odysseus' oarsman in Greek mythology

Elpenor

Elpenor is, in Homer's Odyssey, a member of Odysseus' crew and the youngest of his comrades. His absurd death and the request of his shade made him one of the poem's most memorable figures.

The myth

Elpenor was the youngest of Odysseus' oarsmen. During the crew's stay on Aeaea, the island of the enchantress Circe, he drank heavily the night before their departure and fell asleep on the roof of the palace. At dawn, woken by his companions preparing to sail for Hades, he forgot where he was, fell, and died after breaking his neck.

No one noticed his absence until later. When Odysseus descended into Hades, Elpenor's shade was the first to approach him and begged him to return to Aeaea to give him a proper funeral. On his return, Odysseus fulfilled the request: he burned the body, buried the ashes with his armour and raised a mound topped with the oar Elpenor had rowed in life.

Legacy

The Elpenor syndrome, a sleep disorder that causes unusual behaviour during the shift between sleep and waking, takes its name from this character. His story served as the model for Palinurus's death in Virgil's Aeneid, and James Joyce turned him into a modern counterpart in the funeral of Paddy Dignam in the 'Hades' episode of Ulysses. Jean Giraudoux wrote the short novel Elpénor (1919), and Ezra Pound, Archibald MacLeish and Giorgos Seferis revisited him in their poetry.

Ancient sources: Homer's Odyssey (books X and XI), where his shade is the first to appear in Hades.

Trivia

An oar over the grave. Elpenor asked that his grave be marked with the oar he had rowed with in life, and Odysseus placed it on the mound as requested.
A disorder named after him. Elpenor syndrome describes a difficult transition between sleep and waking, in which a person may speak or open their eyes without being fully awake.
