Gogmagog

Gogmagog is a giant from the Matter of Britain, tied to Cornwall and to the legendary founding of Britain. His wrestling match with Corineus gave rise to place names and to the famous London figures of Gog and Magog.

The myth

According to Geoffrey of Monmouth, in his Historia Regum Britanniae (c. 1136), Gogmagog was one of the last giants living on the island of Albion. He attacked Brutus's Trojan settlement with twenty companions, but was defeated and captured. Corineus wrestled him: the giant broke three of his ribs, and in fury Corineus hoisted him onto his shoulders, carried him to the top of a high rock and threw him into the sea. The spot where he fell became known as Gogmagog's Leap.

Later versions added a genealogy: the so-called Albina story makes him a descendant of the exiled daughters of a king who settled the island, and describes him as the tallest of the giants.

Legacy

Effigies of him and Corineus were used in English pageantry from the coronation of Elizabeth I and eventually became Gog and Magog, guardian statues at London's Guildhall, still paraded in the Lord Mayor's Show. Rabelais revived him in Gargantua (1534) as Gemmagog. Michael Drayton preserved the tale in his Poly-Olbion.

Ancient sources: Geoffrey of Monmouth's Historia Regum Britanniae (c. 1136), the basis of the later versions by Wace and Layamon.

Trivia

A leap with a name. The rock from which, according to the story, Corineus threw the giant into the sea was known for centuries as Gogmagog's Leap.
From giant to statue. The London figures of Gog and Magog were first made of wickerwork and pasteboard, later carved from pine, and were destroyed in an air raid in 1940.
