Norse goddess, wife of Loki

Sigyn

Sigyn is a goddess from Norse mythology, the wife of Loki and mother of Nari or Narfi and Váli. She is best known for staying by her husband during his captivity, holding a bowl to catch the venom dripping from a serpent onto his face.

The myth

According to Norse tales, Sigyn is an ásynja, a goddess of the Æsir, married to Loki. With him she had two sons, Narfi or Nari and Váli. The sources also name Loki's other children with the giantess Angrboða, such as Fenrir, Jörmungandr and Hel, who would be half-siblings of her sons.

After the death of Baldr, which Loki had instigated, the gods bound him to three rocks using the entrails of Narfi, after Váli, turned into a wolf, had torn his brother apart. The goddess Skaði fixed a venomous serpent above Loki's head. Sigyn stood beside him holding a bowl. When it filled, she had to empty it, and in that brief moment the venom fell on Loki's face, making him writhe so violently that earthquakes followed. The tradition holds that this punishment will last until Ragnarök.

The versions differ in details. In the Lokasenna and the Poetic Edda, the seeress of the Völuspá sees her sitting unhappily beside her bound husband in a grove of hot springs. Snorri Sturluson, in the Prose Edda, presents her as a goddess and repeats her role of comfort. Icelandic tradition places the three rocks in the river Þjórsá, near the volcano Hekla.

Legacy

Sigyn appears on the Gosforth Cross, from the 11th century in Cumbria, England, where a kneeling figure holding an object above a bound figure, with a knotted serpent, has been interpreted as her. Nineteenth-century painters such as Nils Blommér, Mårten Eskil Winge and Oscar Wergeland depicted her with Loki. Her name has also been given to a Norwegian wheat variety, a Marvel Comics character from 1978, a Swedish vessel and an Antarctic glacier, and she appears in the comic Sandman.

Ancient sources: She appears in the Völuspá, the Lokasenna, the Prose Edda (Gylfaginning and Skáldskaparmál) and the 9th-century poem Haustlöng.
