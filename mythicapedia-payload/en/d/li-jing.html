Chinese god of the pagoda, heavenly guardian

Li Jing

Li Jing, also known as the Pagoda-Bearing Heavenly King Li, is a guardian deity in Chinese mythology who is also honored in Buddhism, Taoism and Chinese folk religion. His pagoda can trap any spirit, demon or god, and he appears in the classic novels Journey to the West and Investiture of the Gods.

The myth

According to tradition, Li Jing derives from Vaisravana or Kubera, one of the Four Heavenly Kings of the north and god of wealth, whose cult spread in the Tang dynasty because he was believed to help the nation win wars. Another strand links him to a real Tang general, Li Jing, author of military treatises, whose fame later merged with that of the heavenly guardian.

In the novel Investiture of the Gods, Li Jing is a commander at Old Pond Pass in the Shang dynasty and husband of Lady Yin, with whom he has three sons, Jinzha, Muzha and Nezha, and a daughter, Li Zhiying. The youngest, Nezha, is defiant and provokes a great clash with the Eastern Sea Dragon King, Ao Guang, after killing the dragon's son. To save his father, Nezha sacrifices himself, and Li Jing's grief leads him to burn the temple built for his son's spirit. Reborn and remade by his master Taiyi Zhenren, Nezha confronts his father, who flees underground and eventually attempts suicide. Wenshu rescues him, and the master Randeng teaches Li Jing the golden tower art to trap Nezha in a burning pagoda.

Worship and religion

After the Tang dynasty the people revered him as a deity, and in folk culture he became known as the Pagoda-Bearing Heavenly King. In Yongfu County, Guilin, the Ling Gong Festival is held on the twenty-first day of the tenth lunar month, when the clan gathers in its ancestral hall for rituals to expel filth and evil spirits, with lantern performances called wéndēng and wǔdēng. This tradition gave rise to the cáidiào opera. The festival is listed among Guilin's intangible cultural heritage, so the cult remains alive.

Legacy

He appears in the novels Journey to the West and Investiture of the Gods, and in Chinese popular opera, since the cáidiào opera grew out of the festival honoring him.

Ancient sources: The main sources are the Ming dynasty novels Journey to the West and Investiture of the Gods, and Taoist texts that mention the Tang general Li Jing.

Trivia

A pagoda that traps. His pagoda can capture any spirit, demon or god within its walls.
A festival that gave birth to opera. The Ling Gong Festival gave rise to the cáidiào opera, whose birthplace is Lin Village in Yongfu.
