Lausus

Lausus is the son of the Etruscan tyrant Mezentius, a handsome, brave and loyal young warrior who fights beside his father against Aeneas in Virgil's Aeneid. His death at the hands of the Trojan hero makes him a symbol of filial devotion.

The myth

According to Virgil, Lausus was the only son of Mezentius, a king driven out of Caere (or Agylla) by his cruelty. The young man was himself kind and sensitive, a skilled hunter and a great lover of horses. After the exile, father and son sought refuge with Turnus, king of the Rutulians, who was fighting the Trojans who had landed in Latium.

In battle, Lausus killed Abas, a commander of the Trojan fleet, and guarded his father's back. When Aeneas gravely wounded Mezentius, Lausus silently stepped between them and demanded to fight Aeneas himself. Aeneas tried to dissuade him, but the boy's proud smile angered the Trojan leader, who ran him through with his sword. Aeneas then lifted the body with his own hands and returned it to Mezentius, who died soon after trying to avenge his son.

Legacy

Artists such as Bartolomeo Pinelli, who made the engraving Aeneas with the Body of Lausus, and Louis-Léon Cugnot, who produced a bas-relief of Aeneas, Mezentius and Lausus, have depicted him.

Ancient sources: Virgil presents him in Books VII and X of the Aeneid.

Trivia

A mirror of Pallas. Lausus is often seen as a counterpart to Pallas, son of King Evander: both are young, of royal blood, handsome and strong, and both die at the hands of greater heroes.
Piety as a theme. Virgil praises pietas throughout the poem, and Lausus embodies it by stepping in to protect his father, even at the cost of his life.
