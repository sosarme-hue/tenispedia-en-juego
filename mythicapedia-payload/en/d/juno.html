Roman goddess of marriage and wife of Jupiter

Juno

Juno is the Roman goddess who presided over marriage, childbirth and queenship, and the Roman counterpart of the Greek Hera. As the wife and sister of Jupiter, she was a member of the Capitoline Triad and one of the major deities of Roman religion.

The myth

According to the stories, Juno was the daughter of Saturn and Ops. When Saturn lost the heavens, he wandered the earth with his daughter, whom he left in the care of African nymphs so the journey would not tire her. Married to Jupiter, king of Olympus, she bore him Mars, Juventas and Libertas. Tradition disagrees about Vulcan: some say he was Jupiter's son, while others say Juno bore him without a father, and that he later built a throne that left her hanging in the air.

Her jealous nature appears in many episodes. In the myth, Juno drove Athamas mad so that he killed his son Learchus, and she tricked Semele into asking Jupiter to appear to her in his true form, which ended in her death by lightning. She also pursued Hercules, sending serpents against him as an infant and later afflicting him with madness. After Callisto was turned into a bear, Jupiter placed her among the stars as the Great Bear.

Juno protected Jason and the Argonauts on their voyage, after the hero had helped her cross a river. In Virgil's Aeneid, however, she is the open enemy of Aeneas, whom she pursues throughout the poem.

Worship and religion

Juno belonged to the Capitoline Triad with Jupiter and Minerva, centred on the Capitoline Hill in Rome. She was a major deity of Roman religion and one of the Consentes. She held many epithets for her different roles, including Lucina, protector of childbirth; Regina, Mater or Covella; and Sospita in some towns. According to tradition, Foroneus was the first to offer her sacrifices and to build her a temple.

The Roman calendar gave her rites each month. On the Kalends, the pontifex minor invoked her from the Curia Calabra when he announced the date of the Nones, and the regina sacrorum sacrificed a white sow or a white lamb to her in the Regia. In February, a month of purification, her birthday was celebrated as Juno Sospita in a temple on the Palatine, and at the Lupercalia she took part as Juno Lucina.

Legacy

During the Renaissance Juno was reinterpreted as an allegorical figure of queenship, far from the Homeric image: the queen and mother who secures dynastic continuity. In this view she presides over the bride's passage into her husband's house. Modern scholars have debated her figure; Georges Dumézil and Vsevolod Basanoff linked her to the Indo-European tripartite ideology. Janus, god of passages and beginnings, was called Iunonius because of his association with her.

Ancient sources: Virgil presents her as Aeneas's enemy in the Aeneid; Hyginus records her genealogy and children in the Fabulae; Varro gives an etymology of her name and Livy mentions her as an Etruscan goddess of Veii; Ovid describes her February rites in the Fasti.

Trivia

Sacred bird. The peafowl was her sacred animal, and in some versions Juno had Argus in her service, along with Iris, the messenger she shared with Jupiter.
A constellation with a myth. According to the story, Jupiter placed Callisto among the stars as the Great Bear, which by Tethys's decree may never bathe in the Ocean.
