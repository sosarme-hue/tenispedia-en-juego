Buddhist bodhisattva of hell-beings and children

Kṣitigarbha

Kṣitigarbha is a bodhisattva widely venerated in East Asian Buddhism, known as Dizang in China and Jizō in Japan. He is regarded as the protector of beings in the hells and of children, which makes him one of the most beloved figures of popular devotion.

The myth

According to the Kṣitigarbha Pūrvapraṇidhāna Sūtra, the Buddha told this story to his mother, Maya, from the Trāyastriṃśa Heaven. In an earlier life, Kṣitigarbha was a Brahmin maiden called Sacred Girl, grieving over her mother's death, since her mother had slandered the Three Jewels. Moved by filial piety, she made vows to save all beings.

His pledge has two parts: to instruct beings in the six worlds between the death of Gautama and the rise of Maitreya, and not to attain Buddhahood until the hells (naraka) are empty. That is why he is considered the bodhisattva of hell-beings. He is shown as a monk, with a khakkhara that opens the gates of the realms and a cintāmaṇi jewel that lights up the darkness. In China, a legend says he found his mother reborn as a dog called Diting, whom he adopted as a guardian.

Worship and religion

In Japan he is revered as the protector of children and of aborted fetuses. At Tōsen-ji temple in Tokyo, worshippers tie a rope around his statue when making a wish and untie it once the wish is granted; at New Year the priest cuts the ropes of unanswered requests. His cult remains alive across East Asian Buddhism.

Legacy

In Japan his statues are very popular and are sometimes mistaken for Tang Sanzang, the monk of the novel Journey to the West. The Konjikidō hall of Chūson-ji temple houses six of his figures, representing the six paths of rebirth.

Ancient sources: The Kṣitigarbha Pūrvapraṇidhāna Sūtra, translated into Chinese between 695 and 700 by Śikṣānanda; whether it originated in India or China is still debated.

Trivia

A dog called Diting. In China he is sometimes shown accompanied by a dog, which according to legend was his mother reborn.
Prayer ropes. At Tōsen-ji, the rope tied to the statue is untied when the wish is granted, and those left untied are cut at New Year.
