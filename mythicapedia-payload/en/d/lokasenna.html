poem of Loki's insults in Norse mythology

Lokasenna

Lokasenna ('The Flyting of Loki') is a poem from the Poetic Edda in which the Norse gods trade insults with Loki during a feast. It is one of the liveliest texts of Scandinavian tradition and exposes both the flaws of the gods and the cunning and malice of the trickster.

The myth

The feast is held by Ægir, the sea god, for the Æsir. Among the guests are Bragi and Iðunn, Njörðr and Skaði, Freyr, Freyja, Víðarr and Týr, who had already lost a hand binding the wolf Fenrir, Loki's son. Thor does not come and sends his wife Sif. Jealous of the praise heaped on Ægir's servants, Loki kills Fimafeng, and the gods throw him out of the hall.

Loki returns, threatens the servant Eldir, and forces his way into the hall, invoking the rules of hospitality and an ancient oath sworn with Odin. A run of taunts follows: he mocks Bragi's courage, insults Iðunn and Gefjon, while Odin, Freyja, Njörðr, Týr, Freyr and Byggvir try in vain to calm things. At last Thor arrives and threatens four times to knock Loki's head off with his hammer; Loki leaves, saying only Thor's threats frighten him.

The poem closes with a short prose passage: after a failed attempt to escape disguised as a salmon, Loki is bound with the entrails of his son Nari to three rocks, while a serpent drips venom onto his face. His wife Sigyn holds a bowl to catch it, but when she empties it the venom falls on him, and his writhing is said to cause earthquakes.

Legacy

The poem is a key source for Loki and for the flaws of the gods. Some scholars have argued from its irrelevant descriptions of the gods that it was composed after Christianity arrived, though most reject this. Some researchers suggest his accusations may have contributed to the path toward Ragnarök, told in the Völuspá.

Ancient sources: It belongs to the Poetic Edda, preserved in the Codex Regius; the killing of Baldr is explicitly linked to Loki's binding only in Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda.
