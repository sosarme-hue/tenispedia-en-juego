Greek god of wealth and abundance

Plutus

Plutus (Ploutos in ancient Greek, meaning 'wealth') is, in Greek mythology, the god and personification of wealth. Originally linked to agriculture and the harvest as abundance, he belongs to the retinue of Demeter and Persephone.

The myth

According to Hesiod's Theogony, Plutus is the son of Demeter and the mortal Iasion, with whom she lay in a field ploughed three times. Other accounts make him the son of Tyche, the goddess of fortune. Hyginus says he had a brother, Philomelus, with whom he never got along, since Plutus, the richer of the two, never shared anything. Some traditions place his birth on Crete.

In Aristophanes' comedy, Zeus blinds Plutus so that he can distribute his gifts without prejudice. In this version, wealth reaches people who do not deserve it. Plutus is also lame, because he arrives slowly, and winged, because he leaves quickly. Once his sight returns, he begins to judge who should be rich, and chaos follows. The play satirises the Athenian leaders of the time, many of them wealthy and few virtuous.

In art he appears as a child or youth holding the cornucopia. Later allegorical traditions, discussed by Karl Kerenyi, make him a son of Hades and Persephone, although no ancient primary source attests this.

Worship and religion

According to the sources, Plutus had no major cult of his own. He was associated with the worship of Demeter and Persephone, within the Eleusinian context, where he appears on Greek vases bearing the cornucopia.

Legacy

His name gave rise to words such as plutocracy, plutocrat, plutonomics, plutolatry and plutomania. Erasmus, in The Praise of Folly, satirically made him the father of Folly. Dante placed him in Canto VII of the Inferno as a demon guarding the hoarders and the wasteful. The Roman god Pluto and the dwarf planet Pluto have a different origin, though conceptually linked to underground riches.

Ancient sources: Hesiod (Theogony), Aristophanes (Plutus), Hyginus and Lucian of Samosata (Timon).
