legendary founder of Thebes in Greek mythology

Cadmus

Cadmus is the legendary founder of Thebes in Boeotia, in Greek mythology. He is credited with bringing the alphabet to Greece, and he is considered the first of the Theban kings, a central figure of the Theban cycle.

The myth

In the most common version, Cadmus was the son of Agenor, king of Phoenicia, and Telephassa. When Zeus carried off his sister Europa in the form of a bull, their father sent Cadmus to find her, with orders not to return without her. After failing in the search, Cadmus and his mother settled in Thrace, where Telephassa died. Cadmus then consulted the oracle at Delphi, which told him to follow a cow and found a city wherever the animal lay down to rest.

The cow lay down on the site of Thebes. When Cadmus's companions went to fetch water for a sacrifice to Athena, a dragon guarding the spring killed most of them. Cadmus slew the beast and, on Athena's advice, sowed its teeth. From the soil rose armed men, the Spartoi, who fought one another until only five remained; these helped Cadmus build the Cadmeia, the citadel of the city. To atone for killing the dragon, which was sacred to Ares, Cadmus served the god for a period. He then received Harmonia, daughter of Ares and Aphrodite, as his wife, at a wedding attended by all the gods. His children included Polydorus, who became king of Thebes, Illyrius, and several daughters such as Ino, Agave, Autonoe and Semele.

Cadmus's old age was marked by family misfortunes. He abdicated in favour of his grandson Pentheus and went with Harmonia to Illyria, where, according to tradition, he ruled and founded cities. In the end, the gods turned the couple into serpents and sent them to the Elysian Fields.

Legacy

Cadmus's name is linked to the origin of the Greek alphabet, which tradition attributed to his arrival from Phoenicia. The Cadmeia gave its name to the citadel of Thebes. His story, together with that of Oedipus and the Labdacids, forms part of the Theban cycle that inspired tragic playwrights and later poets.

Ancient sources: Herodotus credits him with introducing the Phoenician alphabet to Greece; Pausanias and the Library of Apollodorus record the founding of Thebes and his search for Europa.
