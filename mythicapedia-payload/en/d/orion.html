Orion

Orion is a giant huntsman in Greek mythology, linked above all to Boeotia, where he received a hero cult. He was placed among the stars as a constellation, and the stories about his life and death vary considerably from one ancient author to another.

The myth

In the most common version, Orion was the son of Poseidon and a daughter of Minos, called Euryale, Brylle or Hyeles by different writers. Of giant stature and great strength, he inherited from his father the ability to walk on the sea. Another tradition makes him spring from the earth: Hyrieus of Boeotia, who hosted Zeus and Hermes, asked for a son, and after the gods urinated into a bull's hide that was then buried, Orion was born. His name was linked to the Greek word for urine.

On the island of Chios, Orion wanted to marry Merope, daughter of King Oenopion, who kept refusing him. Drunk, the hunter broke into the princess's bedchamber and raped her. Oenopion blinded him with the help of Dionysus, whose satyrs put Orion to sleep. Orion regained his sight in the east, guided by Cedalion, a servant of Hephaestus who rode on his shoulders, and then tried to take revenge on Oenopion, who had hidden underground.

The death of Orion also has several versions. In the most common, he hunted on Crete with Artemis and Leto and threatened to kill every beast; Gaia sent a giant scorpion that killed him. Other versions blame Artemis, who shot him by accident, mistaking his head in the sea for a target, after a wager set by Apollo. At the request of Artemis and Leto, Zeus placed him among the stars, with the scorpion beside him. According to the tradition, his shade went down to the Underworld, where he still hunts in the meadow of asphodel.

Worship and religion

In Boeotia Orion received a hero cult. His tomb was probably located at the foot of Mount Cerycius, near Tanagra, where a feast in his honour was held into Roman times. Maurice Bowra argued that he may have been a national hero of the Boeotians, much as Castor and Pollux were for the Dorians. There is no evidence that his cult survives today.

Legacy

As a constellation, Orion is one of the most recognisable figures in the night sky. His two dogs, Canis Major and Canis Minor, and his prey, the Hare, belong to the same astronomical tradition that keeps his memory alive.

Ancient sources: Homer, in the Iliad and the Odyssey, describes him as a constellation and as a hunter in the Underworld; Hesiod mentions him in Works and Days; Ovid, Hyginus and Pseudo-Apollodorus record his main episodes.

Trivia

A giant over the sea. According to some accounts, Poseidon granted him the ability to run across the sea as if it were solid ground, a gift that made his journeys almost unstoppable.
A name that changed. His name was linked to the Greek word for urine, but later generations reshaped it into a more elegant and melodious form.
