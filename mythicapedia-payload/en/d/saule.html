Baltic goddess of the sun

Saulė

Saulė is the goddess of the Sun in Lithuanian and Latvian mythology, the most important solar deity of the Baltic peoples. Her name is also the everyday word for the Sun, and she is regarded as the protector of the unfortunate, especially orphans.

The myth

According to tradition, Saulė was married to Mėnuo, the Moon. Mėnuo fell in love with Aušrinė, the morning star, and for this infidelity Perkūnas, the thunder god, punished him. The accounts differ on the punishment: in one version Mėnuo was cut in two and, having learned nothing, the punishment repeats every month; in another, the couple divorced and both wanted to see their daughter Žemyna, the earth, which is why the Sun shines by day and the Moon visits by night; in a third, his face was disfigured by Dievas or by Saulė herself.

Saulė rides a carriage, sometimes of copper or gold, drawn by white horses, and at night her chariot turns into a boat that sails the sea. The missionary Jerome of Prague recounted that the goddess had been held in a tower by a powerful king and rescued by the zodiac with a giant sledgehammer, an object he swore he had seen venerated by the locals.

Worship and religion

The summer solstice was her great festival. The Lithuanian Rasos and the Latvian Līgo, with flower wreaths, bonfires, dancing, leaping over the fire, the search for the magical fern flower and greeting the Sun at sunrise, were absorbed by Christianity and turned into Saint John's Eve and Jāņi. The winter solstice was celebrated as the return of Saulė, and the Lithuanian Kūčios and the Latvian Ziemassvētki were folded into Christmas.

Legacy

In Lithuanian and Latvian, the words for the world, pasaulis and pasaule, mean roughly 'that which lies under the Sun'. Sources credit Saulė with being the mother of four planets in Lithuanian mythology: Indraja (Jupiter), Sėlija (Saturn), Žiezdrė (Mars) and Vaivora (Mercury).

Ancient sources: The earliest written reference is the Slavic translation of John Malalas's Chronicle (1261), in which a smith named Teliavelis makes the Sun and throws it into the sky; it also appears in the account of the missionary Jerome of Prague (c. 1369–1440).

Trivia

A female sun. In the Baltic languages the Sun is feminine, and in Latvian folk songs she appears as a mother who comforts orphans.
A hundred horses. A Lithuanian riddle describes the Sun as a ball that a hundred horses cannot pull, an image repeated in Latvian songs.
