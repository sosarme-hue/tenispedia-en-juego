Pure Land of the Buddha Amitabha in Mahayana Buddhism

Sukhavati

Sukhavati is the Pure Land of the Buddha Amitabha, a realm of bliss said to lie in the west according to Mahayana tradition. It is the best known of the Mahayana pure lands, thanks to the popularity of Pure Land Buddhism in East Asia. The name means roughly "land of bliss" or "realm of ultimate happiness". It appears in Indian Buddhist texts, but it was most fully developed in China and Japan.

The myth

According to the sutras, Sukhavati was created by Dharmakara, a bodhisattva who, before attaining enlightenment, made 48 vows describing the qualities of the land he wished to establish. His eighteenth vow, the Primal Vow, promised that anyone who sincerely wished to be reborn there and called on his name ten times would be reborn there, provided their intention was genuine and they harboured no doubt. This bodhisattva later became the Buddha Amitabha.

The Larger Sutra describes a place of unimaginable beauty, with crystal-clear waters, jewelled trees and golden ground, where suffering, old age and death do not exist. The shorter Amitabha Sutra speaks of seven rows of balustrades, nets and trees made of the seven precious jewels, and explains that those reborn there enjoy perpetual joy and constant access to the teaching. The Contemplation Sutra, by contrast, sets out sixteen meditations taught by the Buddha Shakyamuni to Queen Vaidehi, moving from the visualisation of the setting sun to the figures of Amitabha and his attendant bodhisattvas Avalokiteshvara and Mahasthamaprapta.

The sutras also distinguish nine levels of rebirth, according to the quality of practice of those who are reborn there.

Worship and religion

In Pure Land Buddhism, calling on the name of Amitabha is the central practice for gaining rebirth in Sukhavati. In Tibetan Buddhism the land is invoked during funerals as a favourable destination for the deceased, often accompanied by the tantric technique of phowa, or transference of consciousness, performed by a lama. In Japan it was believed that at the moment of death Amida appeared on a purple cloud to welcome the departed, and many hanging scrolls depict the Western Paradise. These practices remain alive today.

Legacy

Sukhavati has been a recurring theme in Buddhist art, with paintings and mandalas from China, Tibet and Japan. Its name has been given to temples such as Kek Lok Si in Malaysia, the Jile Temple in Harbin and Shinshōgokuraku-ji in Kyoto. In Japanese, Gokuraku and Anraku are common names for the land, and in Chinese it is called Jílè or Xītiān.

Ancient sources: The three Pure Land sutras (the Sutra of Amitayus, the Contemplation Sutra and the Amitabha Sutra), the Avataṃsaka Sūtra, the Lotus Sutra and the Laṅkāvatāra Sūtra, among other Mahayana texts.
