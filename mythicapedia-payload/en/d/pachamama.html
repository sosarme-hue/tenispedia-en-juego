Andean goddess of earth, fertility and the cosmos

Pachamama

Pachamama is an Andean deity linked to space-time, fertility and life, regarded as the mother of the world and the source of human sustenance. She is central to Inca religion and Andean worldview, and many communities still honour her today.

The myth

In Inca tradition, Pachamama appears in several accounts as the wife of Pachacamac and the mother of Inti, the sun god, and Mama Quilla, the moon goddess. Her role is that of a generative mother: the cosmos and the material and spiritual sustenance of human beings come from her.

The myths describe her as normally loving and generous, yet capable of becoming terrible and destructive when offended or hurt. Her earthly form, Allpamama, often hungers: if she receives no offerings, or is wronged, she can cause an imbalance that disturbs the harmony of all worlds. For this reason people return part of what they receive, in a reciprocal exchange called ayni.

In Andean cosmogony, Pachamama transcends the earthly realm and is linked to the Pachakuna, the different space-times, from Hanan Pacha, the upper one, to Uku Pacha, the inner one. Some accounts also connect her with other figures, such as Allpacamasca, the animated earth, and Sumac Ñusta, who joins heaven and earth.

Worship and religion

Pachamama needed no fixed temples; she was honoured at sacred rocks, legendary tree trunks and springs. Priests offered llamas, guinea pigs and miniature garments, and in earlier times coca leaves and mullu shells were also used. After the Spanish conquest she merged with the Virgin Mary through syncretism. Today she is still alive: people toast her before meetings and festivals, pour chicha on the ground as a libation, and Tuesday of challa is dedicated to giving her thanks.

Legacy

Pachamama is today a common name for Mother Earth across much of the Andes, and many Indigenous peoples in South America link their environmental concerns to her. Her name has also entered popular culture and international environmental discourse.

Ancient sources: Information on her worship comes mainly from colonial chronicles and from the work of Blas Valera, a sixteenth-century mestizo chronicler who mentions Allpacamasca.

Trivia

A name with many forms. She is also known as Mamapacha or Mama Pacha, and her Quechua name combines pacha, meaning world, time and cosmos, with mama, mother.
The four mothers. She was one of the Four Elemental Mothers, alongside Mama Cocha, goddess of waters, Mama Nina, goddess of fire, and Mama Wayra, goddess of wind.
