son of Priam in the Trojan War

Gorgythion

Gorgythion is one of the many sons of King Priam of Troy, a minor figure of the Trojan War who appears in Homer's Iliad. He matters mainly for his early death and for the epithet blameless that the poet gives him.

The myth

According to the Iliad, Gorgythion is the son of Priam and Castianeira, a woman from Aisyme, and is described as beautiful. In Book VIII he dies from an arrow shot by Teucer, which was aimed at his brother Hector, and his death is likened to a poppy bowing its head under the rain.

The Bibliotheca attributed to Apollodorus counts him among the sons Priam had with other women, and Hyginus includes him in his list of Priam's children. In the Dictys Cretensis, however, Patroclus is the one who kills him, not Teucer.

Worship and religion

Jane Ellen Harrison noted that the epithet amymon, blameless, was applied to heroized dead who were venerated at shrines, and suggested that his descendants may have given him a cult. This is a hypothesis, not a documented fact.

Legacy

His name was given to a genus of North American butterflies in the subfamily Pyrginae and to an asteroid discovered in 1977.

Ancient sources: Homer's Iliad, the Bibliotheca attributed to Apollodorus, Hyginus's Fabulae, and the Latin Dictys Cretensis chronicle of the Trojan War, dating from the 4th century AD.

Trivia

Another author, another killer. In the late Dictys Cretensis version, Patroclus delivers the fatal blow instead of Teucer, changing who causes the death.
A death without rebirth. Scholar Susanne Lindgren Wofford points out that the simile's poppy bows but does not die, while Gorgythion's death is final.
