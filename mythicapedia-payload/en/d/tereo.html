Thracian king in Greek mythology, son of Ares

Tereus

Tereus is a Thracian king in Greek mythology, son of the god Ares, whose story of violence, revenge and transformation into birds is best known from Ovid's Metamorphoses. The tale has long been used to explore sexual violence, betrayal and motherhood.

The myth

In the most widespread version, Tereus ruled Thrace and came to the aid of Pandion, king of Athens, who was under attack from barbarian enemies. As a reward he received the hand of the princess Procne, with whom he had a son, Itys. Years later, Tereus asked his father-in-law to let Philomela, Procne's sister, travel with him to keep his wife company. On the journey he raped her, cut out her tongue so she could not denounce him, and held her captive in a lonely house in the woods. He told Procne that her sister was dead.

Philomela wove the story into a tapestry and sent it to her sister, who rescued her. To take revenge, Procne killed Itys and served his flesh to Tereus at a banquet. When he discovered the truth, Tereus chased the sisters with a sword, and the gods, out of pity, turned all three into birds: Tereus into a hoopoe, Procne into a swallow and Philomela into a nightingale. Aeschylus, by contrast, names another wife of Tereus, Metis.

Worship and religion

Pausanias describes a tomb attributed to Tereus in Megara, along with a local cult in his honour, and says that the king took his own life in that city. The sources do not describe a cult that survives today.

Legacy

Aristophanes makes Tereus, already transformed into a bird, a character in The Birds. Shakespeare refers to him in Titus Andronicus and Cymbeline, and John Gower retells his story in his poem The Lover's Confession. In modern times, Timberlake Wertenbaker wrote the play The Love of the Nightingale, which Richard Mills later adapted as an opera.

Ancient sources: Ovid's Metamorphoses is the best-known classical source; Hyginus in the Fabulae and Pausanias also mention him, and Aeschylus names his wife Metis.

Trivia

A nightingale without song. According to Hyginus, the female nightingale has no song, a detail linked to the transformation of Philomela into this bird.
Confusion with Teres. The popularity of Sophocles' play about Tereus led Athenians to confuse him with Teres, another Thracian king and father of the ruler with whom Athens made an alliance in 431 BC.
