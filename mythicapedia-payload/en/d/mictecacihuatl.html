Aztec goddess of death and Lady of the Dead

Mictēcacihuātl

Mictēcacihuātl is the Aztec goddess of death, consort of Mictlāntēcutli and queen of Mictlān, the lowest level of the underworld. She presided over the festivals held in honour of the dead, and her cult is thought by many scholars to have survived the Spanish conquest.

The myth

According to tradition, Mictēcacihuātl is the wife of Mictlāntēcutli, lord of Mictlān, and her task was to watch over the bones of the dead. Accounts differ on her origin. One version holds that she died at birth, which is why she is called the Lady of the Dead; another says she was sacrificed as an infant. Sometimes she works alongside her husband, and sometimes she is set against him.

After the Spanish arrived, her image merged with the European idea of death, with its scythe and skeletal form. In Oaxaca the legend of Matlacihua survives: a beautiful woman dressed in white who seduces philandering and drunken men who wander at night, leads them into mud and thorns, and finally drives them over a ravine where a spiny cactus grows. Some die, and others survive with no memory of what happened.

Worship and religion

She presided over the Mexica festivals honouring the dead. Scholars debate whether this pre-Hispanic cult evolved into today's Day of the Dead. Some consider it the most likely outcome, but it is not settled.

Legacy

She lives on in the Oaxacan legend of Matlacihua and, it is argued, in the Mexican Day of the Dead celebration.

Trivia

Flayed body. She was depicted with a flayed body and an open jaw, as if ready to swallow the stars during the day.
Matlacihua. In Oaxaca her figure reappears in the legend of Matlacihua, a woman in white who punishes men who stray at night.
