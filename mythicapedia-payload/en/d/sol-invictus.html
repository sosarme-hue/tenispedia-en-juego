official sun god of the late Roman Empire

Sol Invictus

Sol Invictus, meaning 'Invincible' or 'Unconquered Sun', was the official sun god of the late Roman Empire, presented as a later form of, or perhaps a replacement for, the old Latin god Sol. Emperor Aurelian revived his cult in AD 274 and made him one of the empire's chief deities.

The myth

Sol Invictus was usually shown wearing a radiate crown and driving a horse-drawn chariot across the sky, an image that appeared often on imperial coinage from Aurelian onward. The question of his origins is debated. The traditional view holds that Rome had two different sun gods: the early Sol Indiges, or simply Sol, of minor importance, and a Syrian Sol Invictus introduced under Elagabalus without lasting success.

The revisionist view, by contrast, argues that Rome had a single, continuous solar cult from the monarchy to the end of antiquity, and that Sol Indiges and Sol Invictus were historical expressions of the same deity. Supporters point to at least three temples of Sol in the city, all active in the imperial period. The eastern identity of the god remains unresolved: some link him to Elagabal of Emesa, others to Malakbel or Shams of Palmyra.

Worship and religion

According to the Historia Augusta, Elagabalus brought the cult image of his patron deity from Emesa to Rome and placed it above the traditional gods until his murder in 222. Aurelian reformed the solar cult after his eastern victories: priests of Sol, once of modest origin, became pontifices drawn from the senatorial elite. He built a new temple, dedicated on 25 December 274, and founded games in the god's honour, held every four years. Constantine kept solar imagery on his coins until 325–326, and on 7 March 321 he decreed the day of the Sun a day of rest, the origin of 'Sunday'. The last known inscription mentioning the god dates to AD 387.

Legacy

The epithet Invictus was also applied to Roman deities such as Jupiter, Mars, Hercules and Apollo. The radiate crown, associated with emperors from Nero onward, is linked to solar iconography, although scholars debate whether it was a solar symbol or a political device that concealed divine connotations. Statuettes of Sol Invictus carried by standard-bearers appear in reliefs on the Arch of Constantine.

Ancient sources: The Historia Augusta describes Elagabalus and Aurelian, and the historian Zosimus mentions the removal of a solar cult statue from Palmyra; Augustine and Leo the Great preached against his devotees.

Trivia

The origin of Sunday. According to the sources, Constantine decreed in 321 that the day of the Sun would be an official day of rest in the Empire. The name of Sunday in several Romance languages derives from this dies Solis.
A god on the coins. A solidus and a gold medallion of Constantine show his profile bust paired with Sol Invictus, bearing the legend INVICTUS CONSTANTINUS.
