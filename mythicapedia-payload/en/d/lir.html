Irish sea god

Lir

Lir is a sea god in Irish mythology whose name means "sea" in Old Irish. Rather than a fully independent deity, he seems to be a personification of the sea and an ancestor figure among sea gods, and he corresponds to the Welsh Llŷr.

The myth

Lir is above all an ancestor figure. According to tradition, he is the father of Manannán mac Lir, the sea god who appears often in medieval Irish literature and who, in the Gaelic myths, seems to have taken over his father's role. Early genealogies call him Allód, a metrical poem lists him as the father of Crofhind the Chaste, and another text makes him a son of Midir and grandfather of Manannán.

In the tale of The Children of Lir, he is the eponymous king and the rival of Bodb Dearg for the kingship of the Tuatha Dé Danann after their retreat into the fairy mounds. To appease him, Bodb gave him his daughter Aobh in marriage. They had four children: a girl, Fionnuala, and three sons, Aed and the twins Fiachra and Conn. After Aobh died, Bodb sent another daughter, Aoife, who grew jealous of the children and cursed them to live as swans for 900 years. The sources do not fully settle whether this Lir is the same father of Manannán or a different figure.

Legacy

His name, meaning "sea", makes him a clear case of the ocean personified. The ninth-century Irish glossary Sanas Cormaic, attributed to bishop Cormac mac Cuilennáin, associates him with the sea when explaining Manannán. Several figures are called "son of Lir", and his story lives on in The Children of Lir, one of the best-known tales of Irish tradition.

Ancient sources: The ninth-century Sanas Cormaic, early genealogies, the poem Baile Suthain Sith Eamhain, the Metrical Dindshenchas, and medieval Irish literature about Manannán and the Tuatha Dé Danann.

Trivia

Son of the sea. The Sanas Cormaic glosses Manannán's name as "son of the sea", since his father Lir was identified with the ocean.
A son under another name. In The Adventures of Art son of Conn, a son of Lir appears under the name Lodan.
