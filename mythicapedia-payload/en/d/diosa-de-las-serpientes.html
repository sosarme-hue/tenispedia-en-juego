Minoan faience figurines from Knossos

Snake Goddess

The Snake Goddess is the name given to a pair of faience figurines found in the palace of Knossos on Crete, dating to around 1600 BCE. They are the most popular icon of Minoan art, yet their identity is still debated: they may depict a goddess, priestesses, or different deities altogether.

The myth

The figures were excavated in 1903 by a team led by Arthur Evans, who named the larger one the Snake Goddess and the smaller one the Snake Priestess. The larger, about 34 cm tall once restored, has snakes over her arms and up to a tall cylindrical crown, and wears a bodice that leaves her breasts bare. The smaller holds two snakes aloft and has a cat or panther on her head. Both are incomplete, and Evans restored them with reference to other pieces, a practice for which he has since been criticised.

Scholars have proposed various links to other traditions. Emily Bonney connects them with Syrian religion, Burkert with Paleolithic traditions concerning women and domesticity, and Barry Powell suggested the snake goddess may have become the folklore heroine Ariadne. Hans Georg Wunderlich linked her with the Phoenician Astarte, and Evans tentatively connected her with the Egyptian snake goddess Wadjet, without pursuing the idea.

Worship and religion

The figures were found in a storage pit near the palace, together with objects no longer in use, perhaps after a fire. Martin P. Nilsson noted that in Minoan religion the snake protected the house, a role that later appears in Greek religion, where in the Dionysiac cult it symbolised wisdom and fertility. Today the Snake Goddess plays a minor part in discussions of Minoan religion, a subject that remains very uncertain.

Legacy

After their publication the figures attracted enormous interest and inspired fakes, such as those now held in Boston and Baltimore. They remain a widely recognised image of Minoan art and are displayed at the Heraklion Archaeological Museum. Some authors see her as a precursor of Athena Parthenos, who is also associated with snakes.

Ancient sources: Homer and Herodotus describe women mourning with bared breasts, a practice some scholars connect with these figures, though neither author mentions them directly.

In art

Trivia

Famous forgeries. In 1914 a Boston museum bought a figure with an ivory body and gold snakes that is now considered a fake. Another piece, at the Walters Art Museum in Baltimore, is also regarded as a forgery.
