Phantasos

Phantasos is, in Greco-Roman myth, one of the somnia or dream spirits, the thousand sons of Sleep who personified dreams. He matters because, according to Ovid, he was the one who brought dreams in the shape of lifeless objects.

The myth

According to Ovid, the only ancient author who describes him, Phantasos appeared in dreams in the form of lifeless things: earth, a rock, a wave, a piece of timber, or anything without a soul. He was one of the chief dream spirits, alongside his brothers Morpheus, who appeared in human form, and Phobetor, who took the shape of beasts.

Accounts of the family's parentage vary between writers. Hyginus and Cicero say that the Dreams and Sleep were born from the union of Night and Erebus. Virgil, for his part, places a huge elm at the centre of the underworld, from whose branches hang the empty dreams, an image suggesting a place where these illusions gather. Phantasos has no episode of his own: his role is to deceive the sleeper with appearances.

Legacy

His name comes from the Greek phantasos, related to phantasía, a word meaning 'appearance' or 'imagination'. From the same root come the English words 'fantasy' and 'fantasia'.

Ancient sources: Ovid's Metamorphoses is the main source, where he appears alongside his brothers. Hyginus, Cicero and Virgil mention the Dreams and the elm of the underworld.

Trivia

The brothers' names. The names Morpheus and Phobetor first appear in Ovid, and some scholars think they may have been his own inventions.
Possible Hellenistic origin. Griffin suggests that the division of dream forms between Phantasos and his brothers may have Hellenistic origins.
