Egyptian protector god of tombs and sleep

Tutu

Tutu, known to the Greeks as Tithoes, was an Egyptian protector god of tombs and sleep, worshipped by ordinary people during the Late Period. His hybrid figure, with a lion's body and human features, made him a feared guardian against demons.

The myth

According to tradition, Tutu was the son of the goddess Neith of Sais, regarded as a dangerous deity, and brother of Shemanefer, the crocodile-headed god. His task was to subdue the demons sent out by other goddesses such as Mut, Sekhmet, Nekhbet and Bastet. The sons of these goddesses, such as Maahes, Khonsu and Nefertem, shared the same role.

He was first a protector of tombs, but later he watched over sleep against nightmares. Some accounts say he took a local form of the god Shu in order to approach Tefnut and marry her.

Worship and religion

Worshippers offered goose and bread on portable altars, with rituals to guard against demons and bad dreams. He was believed to grant a longer life and protect people in the Netherworld. His main temple, dedicated under Antoninus Pius and Pertinax, stands at Kellis, today Ismant el-Jarab, and he also appears in reliefs at Kalabsha, Shenhur and Esna, as well as on amulets and Roman coins.

Trivia

Greek name. The Greeks called him Tithoes, and in Ancient Egyptian his name was written twtw, a term linked to the idea of image.
Roman coins. Tutu appears on Roman coins, a sign that his cult survived under Roman rule.
