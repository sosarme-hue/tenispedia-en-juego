Aztec god of renewal, spring and harvest

Xipe Totec

Xipe Totec is the Aztec god of life, death and rebirth, linked to spring, agriculture and goldsmiths. He was worshipped as the Flayed One, since his new skin symbolized the tender maize that sprouts after winter.

The myth

According to tradition, at the beginning of humanity Xipe Totec sacrificed himself by tearing out his eyes and flaying himself alive so that maize could germinate and people could survive. Because of this gesture, human sacrifices were attributed to him, and the victims' skins became his garments.

His name means "Our Lord the Flayed One". He was also called Yohuallahuan, "the Night Drinker", because good rains fell at night. In the Mexica creation account he appears as the Red Tezcatlipoca, and in Tlaxcalan tradition he is linked to Camaxtli.

Worship and religion

His great festival was Tlacaxipehualiztli, celebrated in spring. Priests sacrificed captives, flayed their bodies and wore the skins, painted yellow and called teocuitlaquemitl. Afterwards, warriors and young men wearing freshly flayed skins fought, symbolizing spring defeating winter, and went from house to house asking for alms. People believed touching these skins could cure skin and eye ailments, and the sick made offerings to him at his temple, Yopico, within the Great Temple of Tenochtitlan.

Legacy

His image survives in stone statues and masks, and representations of him have been found as far away as Tazumal in El Salvador. In 2019, Mexican archaeologists from the INAH confirmed the first known surviving temple dedicated to him, in Puebla, built by the Popoluca people before the Aztec arrival.

Ancient sources: Aztec codices and Tlaxcalan accounts recorded after the conquest mention him, and colonial chroniclers such as Jerónimo de Mendieta describe his worship.

Trivia

Golden garments. The skins of the victims were painted yellow and called teocuitlaquemitl, "golden garments". Priests wore them to represent the god.
A temple in Puebla. The temple found in Puebla was built between AD 1000 and 1260, before the Aztec conquest of the area.
