Titaness and primordial sea goddess of Greek myth

Tethys

Tethys is a Titaness of Greek mythology, daughter of Uranus and Gaia, wife of her brother Oceanus and mother of the river gods and the Oceanids. She embodies the fertility of the waters, and her name later became a poetic word for the sea.

The myth

According to Hesiod's Theogony, Tethys is the youngest of the six Titanesses, daughter of Uranus and Gaia, and sister and wife of Oceanus, the river that encircles the world. Together they had three thousand Oceanids, nymphs who guard the waters of the earth, and three thousand river gods, the streams that feed Oceanus. Among the Oceanids are Metis, Zeus's first wife, Doris, mother of the Nereids, and Styx, goddess of the river of the underworld.

Homer tells it differently. In Book 14 of the Iliad, Hera says that Tethys and Oceanus raised her while Zeus was fighting Cronus, and she calls the pair the parents of the gods. This hints at a tradition in which they held the primeval role that Hesiod gives to Uranus and Gaia. In the Timaeus, Plato tries to reconcile the versions: Uranus and Gaia were the parents of Oceanus and Tethys, and these were the parents of Cronus, Rhea and the other Titans.

Tethys has few stories of her own. One astral myth tells that Hera asked her former nurse to stop Callisto from bathing in the sea, so the Great Bear never sinks below the horizon. In Ovid's Metamorphoses, she turns Aesacus into a diving bird. Over time she was confused with the sea-nymph Thetis, mother of Achilles, and she was placed at the western edge of the world, where the Sun ends its course.

Legacy

Her name lives on in geology: the Tethys Ocean was a Mesozoic sea between Gondwana and Laurasia, before the Indian Ocean formed. Tethys is also one of the moons of Saturn. Some scholars, such as Walter Burkert, link her name to the Akkadian tiamtu, “sea”, and to Tiamat. In late antiquity she appears in mosaics from baths in Antioch, with grey wings on her forehead and surrounded by fish, and in Roman poetry her name stands for the sea.

Ancient sources: Hesiod (Theogony), Homer (Iliad, Book 14), Plato (Timaeus), Ovid (Metamorphoses), as well as the Orphic hymns and Apollodorus.

Trivia

Nurse of the stars. Claudian wrote that Tethys nursed Helios and Selene, the children of her siblings Hyperion and Theia, when their light was still weak.
Two Thetises. The Greeks spelled the Titaness's name (Tēthýs) differently from the Nereid's, mother of Achilles (Thétis), but later sources came to confuse the two.
