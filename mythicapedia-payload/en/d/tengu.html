Tengu

The tengu is a legendary creature of Japanese folklore, regarded both as a type of yōkai (supernatural being) and as a kami (god or spirit). Originally pictured as a bird of prey, it became linked to mountains and forests, to the mountain ascetics known as yamabushi, and to the long red nose that is now its best-known feature.

The myth

Early accounts describe tengu as beings that could take human form while keeping wings or a bird's beak. Buddhism regarded them as disruptive demons and harbingers of war, and the first stories portray them as enemies of the faith: they deceived the faithful with false images of the Buddha, abandoned monks in remote places, seduced women and robbed temples.

In later tradition, tengu came to be thought of as the ghosts of angry, vain or heretical priests who had fallen into the "tengu realm". They possessed people, especially women and girls, and spoke through their mouths. Medieval chronicles also credit them with blinding an emperor and possessing an empress. Their image gradually softened into that of protective, if still dangerous, spirits of the mountains.

According to the tradition, tengu are linked to the Shinto deity Sarutahiko, whose nose the Nihon Shoki gives as about 38 centimetres long, and to the goddess Amanozako, described in the Kujiki with wings, a human body and a beast's head. Many scholars believe these figures shaped the tengu's final form.

Worship and religion

Tengu are tied to shugendō, the ascetic practice of the mountains, and its followers, the yamabushi, wear garb that recalls them. At village festivals, tengu were portrayed by performers wearing red masks with long noses.

Legacy

The tengu appears in Japanese art in picture scrolls such as the Tenguzōshi Emaki, painted in 1296, which mocked high-ranking priests by giving them hawk-like beaks. It is also a frequent figure in Japanese film, manga and video games, and its red nose has become a popular symbol of Japan.

Ancient sources: The Nihon Shoki (720) contains the earliest recorded mention of tengu, describing a great shooting star that a Buddhist priest identified as a "celestial dog".
