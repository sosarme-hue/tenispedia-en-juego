goddess of eastern Iran worshipped in Central Asia

Nana

Nana was a goddess of eastern Iran, head of the pantheons of the peoples of Bactria, Sogdiana and Chorasmia and of the Kushan emperors. She was regarded as a benevolent astral deity, and her cult was strong across Central Asia.

The myth

Stories about Nana are scarce, and the sources barely record specific episodes. She was seen as an astral deity: in Sogdian art she appears holding the sun and the moon, and it was thought she may have maintained the balance between day and night. Some Sogdian murals show her battling demons, a warlike aspect not found in other sources. Her possible spouse was Tish, the Sogdian counterpart of Tishtrya.

Her origin goes back to the Mesopotamian goddess Nanaya, although scholars today reject the old nineteenth-century idea that she was the same as Inanna or Ishtar.

Worship and religion

The Kushan emperors linked Nana with royal legitimacy, and she appears on their coins. In the Rabatak inscription of King Kanishka she is presented as the deity who granted him his position. In Sogdiana she became very popular, with a temple at Panjakent, probably her main cult centre. There are also signs of worship in Chorasmia and Khotan. No living cult is recorded.

Ancient sources: The Rabatak inscription of the Kushan emperor Kanishka, which names her as the deity who granted him power, along with the coins of that same ruler.

Trivia

A Sogdian stirrup. When Nana rides a lion, Sogdian artists show her astride with a visible stirrup, a detail not attested earlier in her iconography.
Four arms. The Kushans sometimes depicted her with four arms, and in later Sogdian art this feature reflects conventions of Mahayana Buddhist art in the region.
