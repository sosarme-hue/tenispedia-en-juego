Chastity belt

A chastity belt is a locking metal garment that, according to popular belief, was forced on some medieval women to prevent infidelity. Most modern historians regard it as a myth born in the Victorian era and rooted in Renaissance jokes, so its interest today is mainly cultural.

The myth

The popular version places its origin in the Middle Ages, when knights left for the Crusades and locked their wives into an iron garment to guarantee their faithfulness. Some fathers were also said to fit the belt to their daughters to keep them virgins until marriage. Internal spikes or plates were supposedly meant to block penetration.

Modern historians question nearly all of this. The first detailed description appears in Konrad Kyeser von Eichstätt's Bellifortis (1405), which shows a clumsy, heavy device attributed to Florentine women, with no supporting evidence, and many read it as humour or irony. Surviving examples, such as those at the Musée de Cluny in Paris, are generally considered 19th-century fakes. Prolonged wear would also have caused infections, wounds and even death, so the belts could only have been worn for a few hours or a couple of days.

An alternative hypothesis suggests that women used them as a shield against rape during billeting, travel or nights in inns, or that nurses and nuns wore them near battlefields. Firm evidence for this is also lacking.

Legacy

From the 18th century until the 1930s, Western medicine widely considered masturbation harmful, and many anti-masturbation devices of similar design were filed with the US Patent Office. Today chastity belts survive as erotic toys, mainly within BDSM, and appear in popular culture: in Robin Hood: Men in Tights (1993), in the Red Dwarf episode 'Stoke Me a Clipper' (1997), in Meet the Spartans (2008), in The Love Guru (2008) and in Mad Max: Fury Road (2015), where the wives remove theirs after escaping.

Ancient sources: Konrad Kyeser von Eichstätt's Bellifortis (1405) is the earliest detailed reference known in the West, although it belongs to the late medieval rather than the ancient tradition.

Trivia

A lost piece. The leather-and-iron belt that Anton Pachinger found in Linz in 1889 has been lost, along with most of his collection.
Adam and Eve in iron. One of the belts in the Musée de Cluny in Paris, said to have been worn by Anne of Austria, bears intricately etched figures of Adam and Eve.
