Chinese protection deity

Nezha

Nezha is a protection deity in Taoism and Chinese folk religion, whose character was influenced by the Indian deity Nalakuvara. He is venerated as a patron of children, filial piety and drivers, and his story of birth, rebellion and redemption is one of the most popular in Chinese folklore.

The myth

According to the novel Fengshen Yanyi, Nezha was born during the Shang dynasty at a military fortress in Chentang Pass, son of the commander Li Jing and Lady Yin. After a pregnancy of three years and six months, his mother gave birth to a ball of flesh; Li Jing, convinced it was a demon, struck it with his sword, and a boy emerged who could talk and walk at once. He was later taken as a student by the immortal Taiyi Zhenren.

The central episode tells how, at seven, Nezha bathed in a stream and his struggle with a yaksha and with Ao Bing, third son of the East Sea Dragon King, left both dead. Ao Guang demanded justice and threatened to flood the pass. To save his family, Nezha took his own life, and his teacher restored him with lotus roots. His conflict with his father then grew until Randeng Daoren forced Nezha to submit. Versions vary: in Journey to the West, his death follows the chaos he caused in the Eastern Ocean, and he is revived by the Buddha.

Worship and religion

In Chinese folk religion Nezha was worshipped as the "Marshal of the Central Altar" and the "Third Lotus Prince". Parents made offerings to him so their children would grow strong, healthy and obedient. He is still venerated today, and many drivers of trucks, taxis and tour buses place a small statue of him in their vehicles to drive safely.

Legacy

Nezha appears in many literary adaptations, operas and films, including the 1979 animation Nezha Conquers the Dragon King by Shanghai Animation Film Studio, which popularised his story on screen.

Ancient sources: He appears in the novel Fengshen Yanyi (Investiture of the Gods) and in Journey to the West, as well as in the 19th-century manuscript Fengshen Bang.

Trivia

Indian origin. His figure was inspired by Nalakuvara, a Hindu yaksha who entered Buddhism through the Jataka tales and reached China.
A film version. The sacrifice of children at Chentang Pass appears only in the 1979 film, not in the original novel.
