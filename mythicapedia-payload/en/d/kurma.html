second avatar of Vishnu, in the form of a tortoise

Kurma

Kurma is the second avatar of the Hindu deity Vishnu, depicted as a giant tortoise. He matters because he supports the mountain used as the churning axis in the Churning of the Ocean of Milk, one of the best-known stories in Hindu mythology. The Garuda Purana counts him as the second avatar, while the Bhagavata Purana lists him as the eleventh.

The myth

According to the myth, after the great flood Vishnu took the form of a tortoise and settled at the bottom of the ocean. The gods placed a mountain on his back, and it served as the axis for churning the Ocean of Milk (Samudra Manthana). The serpent Vasuki, a naga, was used as the rope, and the devas and asuras pulled at each end until they obtained amrita, the nectar of immortality.

Vedic texts present him from a different angle. The Shatapatha Brahmana, one of the earliest to mention him, says that Prajapati took the form of a tortoise to create all beings, which is why his form is called Kurma, from a root meaning to make. Kurma is also identified with the sage Kashyapa, whose name likewise means tortoise, so all creatures are called children of Kashyapa. The Taittiriya Samhita describes burying a live tortoise beneath the fire altar as an act that earns merit for reaching heaven.

Worship and religion

In Hindu tradition, Kurma is venerated within the worship of Vishnu. Ritual practice includes the panikacchapika, a finger positioning of the hand that symbolises the tortoise. The kurmacakra is a tortoise-shaped yantra used as a diagram for worship and meditation. The sources consulted do not describe temples or festivals dedicated to him, but these gestures and diagrams remain part of religious practice.

Legacy

The tortoise posture, kurmasana, is a yoga posture named after him. Some modern writers have read the myth as astronomical allegory: the mountain as Earth's axis of rotation, the serpent as its annual motion, and the tortoise as the support holding Earth in its orbit. Others interpret it as the churning of the mind in meditation toward liberation. In the sequence of avatars, Kurma follows Matsya, the fish, and has been seen as an evolutionary step from water toward the amphibian.

Ancient sources: The Shatapatha Brahmana, Taittiriya Samhita and Taittiriya Aranyaka, within the Yajurveda; and, in later literature, the Garuda and Bhagavata Puranas.

Trivia

A tortoise beneath the altar. The Taittiriya Samhita prescribes burying a live tortoise beneath the fire altar. According to the text, the sacrificer gained merit for reaching heaven.
Garuda and the tortoise. In another story, Garuda, king of birds and mount of Vishnu, eats an elephant and a tortoise that are fighting while he seeks amrita to free his mother from enslavement by Kadru.
