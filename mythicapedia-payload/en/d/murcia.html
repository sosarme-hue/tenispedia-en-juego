early Roman goddess linked to myrtle

Murcia

Murcia was an early Roman goddess of uncertain origin, worshipped in the valley between the Aventine and Palatine hills. She was later identified with Venus, who became known as Venus Murcia because of her association with myrtle.

The myth

Little is known of the original goddess, and by the time of ancient Rome her true nature had already been forgotten. Her name suggested she was a goddess of myrtle, and Roman scholars offered several explanations. Varro linked it to myrtus, the myrtle tree, because the site was thought to have once been covered in myrtles.

Christian writers preferred to connect it with murcus or murcidus, meaning lazy or inactive, in order to discredit pagan worship, and some derived it from the Greek word for tender.

Worship and religion

Her sanctuary was an ancient altar, perhaps surrounded by a low wall, which classical writers called ara, sacellum, fanum or aedes. Pliny the Elder, Servius Honoratus and Plutarch mention a grove of myrtles in front of the temple. When the Circus Maximus was enlarged, the altar was enclosed within it, near the turning-post called meta Murcia, and worship of Venus Murcia continued there.

Legacy

The name gave rise to place names such as vallis Murciae, the Valley of Murcia, remembered today in the Via di Valle Murcia and the Roseto Comunale di Valle Murcia in Rome. In 1621 Francisco Cascales proposed that the Spanish city of Murcia took its name from an ancient cult of this goddess, a hypothesis that has not been confirmed.

Ancient sources: Tertullian, Apuleius, Varro and Augustine of Hippo mention the statue of Venus Murcia.

Trivia

A name with several explanations. Romans and Christian writers gave different interpretations of the name, from myrtle to laziness.
