ancient Egyptian god of fertility and vegetation

Min

Min is one of the oldest gods of Egypt, associated with fertility, vegetation, rain and the Moon. Originating in Coptos, he protected merchants and miners crossing the eastern desert, and his cult spread across the country for thousands of years.

The myth

Tradition gave Min several genealogies. Some texts make him a son of Ra or Shu, while a hymn and a stela in the Louvre describe him as the son of Osiris and Isis. His wives are named as Iabet and Repyt depending on the source, and at Coptos Isis was also remembered as both his mother and his wife.

In the Middle Kingdom he merged with Horus as Min-Horus, and in the New Kingdom with Amun, producing Min-Amun, who inherited many of his attributes. Stories present him as a generative force, and he was called the bull of his mother, an epithet describing a god who fathers himself through his own mother. His titles include Chief of the Sky, Opener of the Clouds and Protector of the Moon.

Worship and religion

His cult was one of the longest-lasting and most widespread, documented from the predynastic period into the Roman era. The Greeks called his city of Ipu, modern Akhmim, Panopolis, where he was especially revered. During the festival of the departure of Min, his image was carried from the temple to the fields at the start of the harvest, the crops were blessed and athletic games were held in his honour. Lettuce, for its milky sap and supposed aphrodisiac qualities, was his sacred plant. In the New Kingdom the first fruits of the harvest were offered to him, and offerings of the first wheat shoots are recorded in the Ptolemaic period.

Legacy

The Greeks identified him with Pan, a deity similarly linked to fertility and the fields. His figure, with an erect phallus, raised arm and flail, is among the most recognisable images in Egyptian art. Some modern writers have claimed that the pharaoh had to prove he could ejaculate to ensure the Nile flood, but Egyptologists Kara Cooney and Jonathan Winnerman note that no hard evidence supports this.

Ancient sources: The Pyramid Texts probably allude to him without naming him, and the Hymn to Min praises him as Lord of the Processions and God of the High Plumes.

Trivia

The sacred lettuce. Its milky sap, which the ancients compared to semen, led to lettuce being linked with him and offered during his festivals.
A predynastic palette. A mudstone palette from the Naqada III period bears the ancient hieroglyph for Min in relief.
