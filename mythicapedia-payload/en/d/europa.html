Phoenician princess abducted by Zeus, mother of Minos

Europa

In Greek mythology, Europa is a Phoenician princess abducted by Zeus in the form of a bull, and the mother of the Cretan king Minos. Her name gave rise to the name of the European continent, which is why her figure has had a lasting presence in Western art.

The myth

According to the myth, Zeus fell in love with Europa while she was gathering flowers in a meadow with her companions. To approach her without frightening her, he took the form of a tame white bull and mingled with her father's herds. Europa stroked the animal, climbed onto its back, and the bull ran to the sea, swam across, and carried her to Crete, where Zeus revealed his true identity. An ancient scholium tells that, to deceive her, the god breathed out a saffron flower from his mouth.

The sources disagree about her parentage. The archaic poems, such as the Iliad and the Catalogue of Women attributed to Hesiod, make her a daughter of Phoenix, eponym of Phoenicia, while later versions name King Agenor as her father, with brothers such as Cadmus, who brought the alphabet to Greece, and Cilix, who gave his name to Cilicia. Her mother also varies from author to author: Telephassa, Perimede or Argiope. In Crete, Europa married Asterion, who adopted the three sons she had with Zeus: Minos, Rhadamanthus and Sarpedon. Minos and Rhadamanthus later became judges of the Underworld.

Zeus gave her a necklace made by Hephaestus and three further gifts: the bronze automaton Talos, the hound Laelaps, which never failed to catch its prey, and a javelin that never missed. Eratosthenes says that the god later set the shape of the bull among the stars, in the constellation Taurus. Herodotus, by contrast, offered a rationalised version in which Europa was kidnapped by Cretans seeking revenge for the abduction of Io.

Worship and religion

In the Phoenician city of Sidon there was a great temple of Astarte which, according to a priest who spoke with Lucian of Samosata in the 2nd century AD, was in fact sacred to Europa. Sidonian coins showed Europa seated on the bull. Some scholars suggest that the myth grew out of a sacred union between the Phoenician deities 'Aštar and 'Aštart, depicted in bovine form. No living cult of Europa survives today.

Legacy

Her name lies behind the name of the continent, though its origin is still debated: one hypothesis links it to Greek euros, 'wide', and ops, 'face', while another connects it to a Semitic root associated with sunset and the west. Her image appeared on postage stamps of the European Coal and Steel Community issued in 1956, on Greek 2-euro coins beside the bull, and since May 2013 in the watermark and hologram of euro banknotes. Painters such as Titian, Rubens, Rembrandt, Goya and Picasso have depicted her abduction.

Ancient sources: She appears in the Iliad and in the Catalogue of Women attributed to Hesiod, and also in the works of Bacchylides, Herodotus and Ovid.

Trivia

A name for the bull. Asterion was also the name of the Minotaur and an epithet of Zeus, probably derived from the name of the goddess Aštar.
A stamp before the euro. Her image appeared on postage stamps of the European Coal and Steel Community issued in 1956, decades before the euro existed.
A bull in the sky. According to Eratosthenes, Zeus placed the shape of the white bull among the stars, in the constellation now known as Taurus.
