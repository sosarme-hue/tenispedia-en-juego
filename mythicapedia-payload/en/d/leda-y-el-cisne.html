Myth of Leda, the Spartan queen seduced by Zeus

Leda and the Swan

Leda and the Swan is a story from Greek mythology in which Zeus, in the form of a swan, seduces Leda, queen of Sparta and wife of King Tyndareus. The encounter produces four children, and the tale became one of the most represented subjects in European art.

The myth

According to the myth, Leda was walking by the river Eurotas when Zeus came down from Olympus as a swan. The bird pretended to be chased by an eagle, and Leda took it into her lap, a moment the god used to couple with her.

That same night Leda had slept with her husband, King Tyndareus, so the children had different fathers. Helen and Polydeuces were children of Zeus and immortal; Clytemnestra and Castor were children of Tyndareus and mortal. In some versions Leda laid two eggs from which the four children hatched. Other accounts make Helen a daughter of Nemesis, goddess who punished pride.

Legacy

The subject became very popular in the Italian Renaissance, when it was painted with an erotic tone that shocked some contemporaries. Leonardo da Vinci painted a version with the four children and broken eggs, whose original is lost, and Michelangelo made another, also lost. Cy Twombly treated it in abstract form in 1962, and the Viennese Actionists brought it to film in 1964.

Ancient sources: Ovid tells the story briefly in the Metamorphoses, without implying rape.

Trivia

A lost original. Leonardo da Vinci's 1508 version was lost, possibly deliberately destroyed, and is known only through copies.
A knife attack. A son of the Regent of France attacked the figure of Leda in Correggio's painting with a knife.
