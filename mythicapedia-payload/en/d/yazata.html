Zoroastrian term for a divinity worthy of worship

Yazata

Yazata is the Avestan word that Zoroastrianism uses for a divinity, or for an epithet of one, and literally means "worthy of worship or veneration". Within this faith, the yazatas are the good powers that act under Ahura Mazda and help keep the world's order against Ahriman.

The myth

According to Zoroastrian tradition, Ahura Mazda created the yazatas to help him maintain the order of the world and to fight Ahriman and his demons. They gather the light of the Sun and pour it onto the Earth, teach people to drive away demons, and help them purify themselves and rise. Those who honour them with ritual offerings receive their favour and prosper.

Many were ancient Iranian deities reduced to an auxiliary role, such as Ātar (Fire), Mithra, Anahita, Rashnu (the Just), Sraosha and Verethraghna. Zoroaster prayed to them for strength in his mission. In the Gathas, the oldest texts, the term applies both to Ahura Mazda and to beings that must be worshipped, in contrast to the daevas, which must be rejected. In later texts the yazatas act as charioteers, as guardians of the Chinvat bridge that all souls must cross, and as protectors of the days of the month.

Worship and religion

Around the late 5th or early 4th century BCE, the Achaemenids instituted a religious calendar in which each day of the month bore the name of a yazata and was placed under its protection. This kept these divinities in public awareness and established a hierarchy among them. In some hierarchies Mithra ranks as the most dignified after Ahura Mazda. Zoroastrianism is still practised today, notably among the Parsis of India.

Legacy

In the 1860s and 1870s, the linguist Martin Haug compared the yazatas to Christian angels, and this reading became so entrenched that "angel" is now almost universally used as a gloss for the word. Among Iran's Muslims, Sraosha survives as the angel Surush, arguably the most popular of the subordinate yazatas and the only one of the whole ancient pantheon still venerated by name.

Ancient sources: The Gathas of Zoroaster, the oldest texts of Zoroastrianism, and the Younger Avesta, where the yazatas appear with unambiguously divine powers and anthropomorphic attributes.

Trivia

An angel by another name. Martin Haug read the yazatas in Christian terms, and his reading spread so widely that they are now almost always translated as "angels".
The calendar of days. Each day of the month carried the name of a yazata, so these divinities accompanied the everyday calendar.
