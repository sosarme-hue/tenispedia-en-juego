sea god of Greek mythology

Phorcys

Phorcys is a sea deity of Greek mythology, one of the earliest divine generations and traditionally the son of Pontus and Gaia. With Ceto he fathered a brood of monsters, including the Graeae, the Gorgons and the serpent guarding the golden apples, so he embodies the dangers of the open sea.

The myth

According to Hesiod's Theogony, Phorcys is the son of Gaia and Pontus, and the brother of Nereus, Thaumas, Ceto and Eurybia. With Ceto he had the Phorcydes, a brood of monstrous beings. Among them were the Graeae, whom Hesiod names only as Pemphredo and Enyo; the Gorgons, Stheno, Euryale and Medusa; and, though the text is unclear, probably Echidna. Their youngest child was a terrible serpent guarding the golden apples at the edge of the world, called the Hesperian Dragon or Ladon.

Homer calls him the old man of the sea and the minister of the barren sea. Some traditions place him among the eldest offspring of Oceanus and Tethys, alongside Cronus and Rhea, as Plato recorded from an Orphic version; others make him one of the Titans born of Uranus and Gaia. According to Apollodorus, Scylla was his daughter, though her father could also be another figure. Apollonius of Rhodes agrees and adds that when Heracles killed Scylla, Phorcys brought her back to life with fire. Sophocles makes him the father of the Sirens.

An Italic version, preserved by Varro and the commentator Servius, tells a different story: Phorcys was once king of Sardinia and Corsica, defeated in a naval battle by King Atlas, and later became a sea deity, perhaps half man and half sea ram.

Worship and religion

The sources say very little about worship. Homer mentions a harbour in Ithaca dedicated to Phorcys, which is the only trace of veneration in the surviving texts. No temples or festivals devoted to him are recorded.

Legacy

Hellenistic-Roman mosaics show him as a hybrid sea monster with features of a fish, a serpent and a crab, covered in spiky scales. The classical scholar Károly Kerényi equated him with the sea gods Nereus and Proteus, whom he called the old men of the sea. Jean-Pierre Vernant read him as an embodiment of the physical danger of the open sea and of what lies outside the divine order.

Ancient sources: Hesiod (Theogony), Homer (Odyssey), Plato (Timaeus), Apollodorus, Apollonius of Rhodes, Sophocles, Varro and Servius.

Trivia

A cyclops's mother. Homer says that Thoosa, mother of Polyphemus, was a daughter of Phorcys, though he does not name her mother.
Brought back with fire. According to Apollonius of Rhodes, Phorcys restored Scylla to life with fire after Heracles killed her during his labours.
