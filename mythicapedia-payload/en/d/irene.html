Greek goddess of peace, one of the Horae

Eirene

Eirene (Irene in English) is the goddess and personification of peace in Greek mythology. She is one of the Horae, daughter of Zeus and Themis, and her cult was especially important in Athens. Her Roman counterpart was Pax. Myth

The myth

Eirene has no myths of her own, and no stories or adventures are told about her. Her place in tradition is defined mainly by her parentage. She is usually said to be the daughter of Zeus and Themis, and the sister of Dike and Eunomia, with whom she belongs to the Horae.

In art she appears as a beautiful young woman carrying a cornucopia, a sceptre, and a torch or rhyton. Sometimes she is shown with a crown of flowers, an olive branch and a dove, or holding Ploutos, the god of wealth, in her arms.

Worship and religion

The Athenians held her in particular regard. After a naval victory over Sparta in 375 BC, they erected altars to her and, after 371 BC, held an annual state sacrifice to commemorate the Common Peace of that year. In the Agora they set up a bronze votive statue by Cephisodotus the Elder, probably the father or uncle of the famous sculptor Praxiteles. The statue was much admired and appears on vases and coins, although the original is lost.

Legacy

The Romans made marble copies of the statue, and the best surviving one is in the Munich Glyptothek. It shows the goddess holding the child Ploutos, gazing at him maternally, as an allegory of plenty prospering under the protection of peace. Her name lives on in other languages, in names such as Irene and Irenaeus. The Munich copy had a turbulent history: it was in the Villa Albani in Rome, was taken to France by Napoleon, and after his fall was bought by Ludwig I of Bavaria.
