Mesopotamian goddess of love, war and fertility

Ishtar

Ishtar, known in Sumerian as Inanna, was the Mesopotamian goddess of love, war and fertility, and one of the most widely worshipped deities of the ancient Near East. Linked to the planet Venus, her contradictory character and her descent into the underworld made her one of the region's most famous mythological figures.

The myth

The stories portray her as a deity of contradictions: a protector of love and of illicit affairs, and at the same time a relentless warrior. According to tradition, the goddess loved Tammuz (Dumuzi in Sumerian), a god of the harvest, and that love cost the young god his life.

The most famous episode is her descent into Irkalla, the land of darkness, to wrest power over life and death from her sister Ereshkigal. At each of the seven gates she had to surrender a garment, until she arrived naked before the queen of the underworld, who killed her and hung her body on a hook. With her death, the fertility of the world faded. Her loyal servant Papsukkal appealed to the gods, who sent the food and water of life to revive her. There was a price, however: Tammuz must spend six months of the year in the realm of the dead, while Ishtar mourns him, and when spring returns he rises again and everything turns green.

In the Epic of Gilgamesh, the goddess asks the hero to become her husband. When he refuses, she unleashes the Bull of Heaven, which leads to the death of Enkidu.

Worship and religion

Ishtar was worshipped in the Babylonian temple E.tur.kalam.ma, and kings dedicated thrones and images to her. Her oldest major centre was Uruk, home to the Eanna temple. In her warlike aspect she was honoured at Agade and Sippar under the name Anunit. Her cult grew considerably in the age of Sargon of Akkad, and the Assyrians ranked her among the highest gods of their pantheon, close to Ashur. Each year in Babylon, the sacred marriage was enacted in the temple as a fertility rite. Her worship declined gradually between the 1st and 6th centuries CE, as Christianity spread, and it has no living continuity today.

Legacy

Her name appears in the Book of Jeremiah, where she is called "the queen of heaven". Her symbol, the eight-pointed star, is associated with Venus, and a continent on that planet bears her name: Ishtar Terra. Several names in the Western tradition, such as Astarte, Astaroth, Esther, Lilith and Hecate, have been linked to her. Joseph Campbell compared her with Aphrodite and Isis, and some scholars see in her legend the origin of the so-called "dance of the seven veils", a hypothesis that is not confirmed.

Ancient sources: The Epic of Gilgamesh features her in some of its best-known episodes, and the hymns of the priestess Enheduanna, daughter of Sargon, praise her by name.

Trivia

A goddess with three faces. In Uruk she was worshipped in three forms: morning Inanna, evening Inanna and princely Inanna. The first two reflected the phases of Venus as morning and evening star.
Seven gates, seven garments. In the story of her descent, each of the seven gates of the underworld strips her of a garment, so she arrives stripped of her power.
