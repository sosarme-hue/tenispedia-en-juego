Hindu goddess, supreme feminine principle

Devi

Devi, meaning 'goddess' in Sanskrit, is the feminine form of the divine in Hinduism. The name is also used as a synonym for Shakti, the feminine and creative aspect of the divine, and she stands at the heart of the Shakta tradition and is a central figure in Shaivism.

The myth

According to the texts, reverence for the divine feminine appears in the Vedas, composed around the second millennium BCE. In the Devisukta hymn of the Rigveda, ultimate reality is presented as a goddess. In that era, however, goddesses played a smaller role than gods, and they are named mostly as cosmic forces, such as Prithvi (earth), Aditi (cosmic moral order) or Ratri (night).

In the medieval Puranas, Devi becomes the center of a vast expansion of myth. All goddesses come to be seen as aspects of one Devi, the Supreme Power. The Devi Mahatmya reveres her as the ultimate truth and supreme power, while the Devi Upanishad declares her to be Brahman, the source of matter (prakriti) and consciousness (purusha), the ruler of the world who grants riches to her devotees.

In mythology, Devi is the active energy of the male deities and is always paired with them: Parvati with Shiva, Saraswati with Brahma, Lakshmi with Vishnu, Sita with Rama and Radha with Krishna. Parvati, daughter of Himavan and Maināvati, performed strict austerities until she became Shiva's consort, and she is the mother of Ganesha and Kartikeya. The texts, however, do not confine her to wife and mother: she is associated with water, mountains, the arts, warriors and dance.

Worship and religion

Worship of goddesses is an integral part of Hinduism, alongside that of the gods. In the Shakta tradition Devi is supreme; in the Smarta tradition she is one of the five primary forms of Brahman. In Shaivism, the Maha Vidyas ('great wisdoms') are understood as aspects of the mother goddess. The Navadurga and the Matrikas are revered, and the worship of young girls called Kumari expresses the belief that women are manifestations of Devi. Her worship remains alive in modern Hinduism.

Legacy

Her imagery adorns Hindu temples across South and Southeast Asia, where statues and iconography of Parvati survive from ancient and medieval times. In Indian art, the figure of Ardhanarishvara, half Shiva and half Parvati, expresses the ideal couple as two halves of a whole. In Hinduism, the Tridevi (Lakshmi, Parvati and Saraswati) is the feminine counterpart of the Trimurti.

Ancient sources: Rigveda (Devisukta hymn), medieval Puranas, Devi Mahatmya and Devi Upanishad.

Trivia

A name for every face. Parvati has over a thousand names in the regional mythologies of India, and each one expresses a different aspect of the goddess, such as Gauri.
A shared epithet. Bhagavatī is an honorific title for goddesses used in both Hinduism and Buddhism; in Hinduism it is applied mainly to Lakshmi and Durga.
