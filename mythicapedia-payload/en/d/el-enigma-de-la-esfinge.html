The Riddle of the Sphinx

The Riddle of the Sphinx is the puzzle that, in Greek mythology, this monster posed to those who arrived at Thebes, and whose solution freed the city. It became the archetype of the riddle in Western culture.

The myth

According to the story, Hera sent the Sphinx to punish Thebes during the reign of Creon. With a woman's face and breasts, a lion's body and bird's wings, the monster waited on Mount Phicium, where it had learned a riddle from the Muses. An oracle declared that the Thebans would be rid of her once the riddle was solved, but those who tried failed and were devoured. After the death of Haemon, son of Creon, Creon offered the kingdom and Laius's widow to whoever solved it.

Oedipus answered that the riddle concerned man: as an infant he crawls, as an adult he walks upright, and in old age he leans on a staff, like a third leg. Hearing this, the Sphinx threw herself from the acropolis, Oedipus gained the kingdom and married his mother without recognizing her. Some later scholarly traditions credit the Sphinx with a second riddle, about two sisters, whose answer is day and night.

Legacy

The episode became the archetype of the riddle in Western culture: a model of intelligence defeating a monster with a question.

Ancient sources: Attic tragedians and later compilers treat the episode as a paradigm, that is, an exemplary case of human intelligence against a monster.

Trivia

Two different sphinxes. The Greek Sphinx is a proper name and should not be confused with the Egyptian sphinx, a common name for certain creatures.
Day and night in Greek. In Greek, the words for day and night are both feminine, which is why the second riddle is solved with these two alternating realities.
