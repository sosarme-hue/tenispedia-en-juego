supreme god of the Canaanite pantheon

El

El is the supreme god of the Canaanite pantheon and of the wider ancient Levant, and also a generic Northwest Semitic word for 'god'. Father of many deities and of humankind, he was seen as creator and judge, and his figure shaped later ideas about the god of Israel.

The myth

According to the Ugaritic tales, El presided over the council of the gods on the mountain of assembly, where he lived with his consort Asherah (Athirat) in a tent on a mountain top, at whose base all the world's fresh waters rose. Sources do not fully agree on his offspring: he is credited with seventy or eighty-seven sons, among them Baal, Yam and Mot, who share traits with Zeus, Poseidon and Hades.

Texts also give him a second wife, Anat, sister of Baal, while other stories show him as a bearded old patriarch, fond of wine and rather frail. One well-known episode describes a banquet where he gets drunk, causes a scandal and falls after picking a fight with a guest; the text ends with a charm, possibly against a hangover. In other myths El heals his son Kirta and acts as the judge who decides what gods and humans must do.

Worship and religion

In Canaan El was the head of the pantheon and king of the gods, yet no temple dedicated to him is recorded at Ugarit, which had large temples to Dagon and Hadad. He was represented as a bull, with or without wings, a symbol of strength and creative power, and in some places he appears carved on rocks as the 'Ancient of Days'. Canaanite kings were called 'servants of El', expressing a theocratic view of rule. His cult does not survive today.

Legacy

The name survives in many personal and place names with the element 'El-', such as Elizabeth, Israel, Michael or Bethel, most of them theophoric names. Some scholars, such as Francesca Stavrakopoulou, argue that the biblical Yahweh absorbed traits of El in a process she calls 'pantheon reduction'. Later Greek writers identified him with Uranus, Cronus or Poseidon.

Ancient sources: The tablets of Ugarit, from the 14th-13th centuries BCE, are the main source, and the Hebrew Bible preserves echoes of his figure in divine names and in Deuteronomy 32.

Trivia

Generic name. In Semitic languages, the word el simply meant 'god', and the same element appears in names such as Israel or Michael.
Father of the gods. Ugaritic texts describe him as a bearded, wine-loving elder, very different from the warrior image of Baal.
