Babylonian god of war, identified with Marduk

Belus

Belus (Greek Bêlos) is the name by which Greek and Latin authors knew the Babylonian god Bel-Marduk, lord of war. Because he was linked to Zeus and Jupiter, and because of the famous tower attributed to him, his name became a lasting echo of Babylon in classical literature.

The myth

Classical writers preserved two versions. In one, Belus is a god whom the Greeks identified with Zeus (Zeus Belus) or the Romans with Jupiter (Jupiter Belus). In the other, he becomes an ancient king who founded Babylon and built its ziggurat. According to a account preserved by Eusebius of Caesarea, giants who lived in Babylonia were destroyed by the gods for their impiety, but one of them, named Belus, escaped, settled there and lived in the tower he had built.

The historian Abydenus records that the site was originally a sea, until Belus drained it, divided the land, surrounded the city with a wall and vanished at the appointed time. Many scholars see in this story a rationalized version of Marduk's defeat of Tiamat in the Enuma Elish, followed by Belus's deification.

Worship and religion

According to these sources, Belus was worshipped as the god of war. Greeks called the Babylonian ziggurat the "Tomb of Belus"; Strabo and Diodorus Siculus report that it was demolished by the Persians and that Alexander the Great was asked to restore it.

Legacy

The name of Belus has become linked with the Tower of Babel in Western tradition, and in classical and later literature it survives as that of a king who founded Babylon.

Ancient sources: Eusebius of Caesarea (Praeparatio Evangelica), Abydenus, Diodorus Siculus and Strabo, who connect him with Zeus, Jupiter and the Babylonian ziggurat.

Trivia

The Tomb of Belus. Classical authors used this name for the Babylonian ziggurat. Strabo states that Xerxes demolished it.
A king and a god. In the rationalized version, Belus passed from founding king to god after vanishing.
