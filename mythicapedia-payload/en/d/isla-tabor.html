phantom island of the South Pacific

Tabor Island

Tabor Island, also known as Maria Theresa Reef, is a supposed islet that appeared on some South Pacific charts for more than a century, between the Tuamotu islands and New Zealand, yet no one has managed to find it. It is one of the best-known examples of a phantom island.

The myth

The story begins on 16 November 1843, when the American captain Asaph P. Taber, in command of the whaler Maria Theresa, recorded breakers in that part of the ocean. His surname, misspelled as Tabor, became attached to the place. The first report reached the press in 1853, in the Sydney Morning Herald, and doubts soon arose, because the ship's logbook alternates the words breaker, meaning a surf-line over a reef, and breacher, the spout of a whale.

Later searches failed one after another. In 1957 nothing was found, and in the 1970s the New Zealand navy swept the area without finding shoals or islands, over waters about 5,000 metres deep. In 1983 the coordinates were recalculated more than a thousand kilometres further east, but nothing turned up there either, not even with high-resolution satellite photographs. In 1966 an amateur radio magazine published a photo of a man transmitting from the reef, which was later shown to be a hoax.

Legacy

The island appears under its own name in two novels by Jules Verne, In Search of the Castaways and The Mysterious Island, where it serves as a stop in the search for Ayrton. Ernest Legouvé Reef is linked to the imaginary Lincoln Island of the second novel, and the island's legend still appears on some modern maps.

Trivia

A misleading word. According to analysis of the logbook, Taber alternated between breaker and breacher, two words that are easily confused and that may have produced a reef that never existed.
A radio hoax. The 1966 photo published by an amateur radio magazine showed a supposed operator transmitting from the reef, but the staging was later proven.
