heroine of Byzantium, wife of Byzas

Phidalia

Phidalia is a female figure of Greek mythology linked to Byzantium and to Byzas, its legendary founder. She is remembered as a defender of the city, and her name is attached to several places along the Bosporus.

The myth

According to one account, Phidalia was the wife of Byzas, founder and king of Byzantium. While he was away, pushing enemies back towards Thrace, the Scythian king Odryses crossed the Ister and besieged the city. Phidalia gathered the snakes near the city, kept them under guard and, appearing suddenly before the attackers, hurled them like arrows or javelins. Many were infected and the city was saved. From then on, snakes found near Byzantium were not to be killed, as they were considered benefactors.

Another tradition links her to the Gynaikon Limen, or Women's Harbor. While the men were away, she and a group of women repelled an assault led by Stroibos, brother of Byzas, and chased the attackers as far as the harbor, which was named after the event.

Malalas credits Phidalia with the first founding of Byzantium: her father Barbysius, guard of the emporium, asked her on his deathbed to build a wall to the sea, and she later married Byzas. A rock near the Bosporus, shaped like eagle's wings, also bears her name. In one version, after sleeping with Byzas and feeling shame, she threw herself into the sea, and Poseidon, moved by pity, broke off a piece of the mainland that became her tomb.

Worship and religion

Tradition holds that Phidalia founded a sanctuary of Aphrodite in Byzantium, near a sanctuary of Artemis built by Byzas. Under Severus, a large kynegion, or hunting ground, was added opposite the Artemis sanctuary, and a theatre opposite the Aphrodite one.

Legacy

Her name gave rise to the place name Phidalia, applied to a site near the Women's Harbor and to the bay called Limen Phidalias. She also appears in the Greek Anthology, in two inscriptions on a statue of Byzas and Phidalia.

Ancient sources: Nonnus of Panopolis calls her "the serpent-thrower" in his Dionysiaca, while Malalas and the Greek Anthology preserve her story.

Trivia

Protected snakes. The tradition arising from her deed required that snakes found near Byzantium not be killed, as they were considered benefactors.
A women's harbour. The Gynaikon Limen owes its name to the defence of the city by Phidalia and a group of women while the men were away.
