river of Greek mythology

Eridanus

Eridanus is a legendary river of Greek mythology, located in a distant and vague part of the far northwest of the known world. Over time it was identified with the Po and also placed in the sky as a constellation.

The myth

In Hesiod's Theogony, Eridanus, the "deep-eddying" river, was a son of Oceanus and Tethys and one of the river gods. Virgil called him the king of the rivers. Heracles reached his banks after crossing Illyria, and there nymphs, daughters of Zeus and Themis, told him where to find Nereus and the way to the Garden of the Hesperides.

The best-known episode concerns Phaethon, son of the Sun, who lost control of his father's chariot and fell into the river after Zeus struck him down. His sisters, the Heliades, wept so bitterly that they turned into poplars, and their tears hardened into amber carried away by the waters. According to Ovid, Cygnus mourned Phaethon along the banks until he was transformed into a swan.

Greek authors placed the river at the far western edge of the world, while Virgil included it among the rivers of the underworld. Herodotus doubted that it existed and thought its name was Greek, probably coined by some poet. In later times it was identified with the Po, near the route of the amber trade.

Legacy

Eridanus is one of the 48 constellations listed by Ptolemy in the 2nd century and remains among the 88 modern constellations, depicted as a river. An ancient river near Athens was also given that name and was rediscovered during the construction of the city's metro.

Ancient sources: Hesiod (Theogony), Herodotus, Euripides (Hippolytus), Apollonius of Rhodes (Argonautica), Virgil (Aeneid), Ovid (Metamorphoses) and Nonnus (Dionysiaca).

Trivia

A river on fire. According to tradition, after Phaethon's fall the river was scorched by Zeus's thunderbolt, and in the sky it still burns among the stars.
A river that does not exist. The geographer Strabo held that the Eridanos did not exist anywhere on earth, although it was said to lie near the Po.
