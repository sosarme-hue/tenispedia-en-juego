serpent dragon of Hittite mythology

Illuyanka

Illuyanka is a serpentine dragon from Hittite mythology, slain by the storm god Teshub. Its story, preserved on cuneiform tablets from Hattusa, is one of the sky-god-versus-monster combats most often compared with Greek myths.

The myth

In Hittite tradition, Illuyanka first defeated Teshub, who sought advice from the goddess Inaras. In return for her help, Teshub promised to sleep with a mortal named Hupasiyas. She devised a trap: she brought the dragon large amounts of food and drink until he was drunk, then bound him with a rope. Teshub then appeared with the other gods and killed him.

In a second version, Illuyanka tore out Teshub's eyes and heart. To take revenge, the god married the semi-divine Hebat, and they had a son, Sarruma. When Sarruma grew up, he married the dragon's daughter, and Teshub asked him to request the stolen eyes and heart as a wedding gift. Restored, the god fought Illuyanka again. Just as he was about to win, Sarruma learned he had been used and demanded to die along with the dragon. Teshub killed them both with a storm of rain and lightning.

Worship and religion

The texts survive in a ritual of the Hattian spring festival of Puruli, held at the former Hittite capital. The myth formed part of that ceremony, which staged the sky god's victory over the monster.

Legacy

The name is considered a compound of Indo-European words for "snake", and it has been compared with Latin anguilla ("eel") and Sanskrit ahi. Since 1930, scholars have compared Teshub's battle with Zeus's fight against Typhon, though the idea won few supporters at first.

Ancient sources: Hittite cuneiform tablets, catalogued as CTH 321, preserve two consecutive versions of the narrative.

Trivia

A stone relief. One version of the story appears on a relief from Malatya, dated between 1050 and 850 BC, now displayed in Ankara.
A name with distant relatives. Its name has been analysed as a compound of two Proto-Indo-European words for "snake".
