Buddhist bodhisattva of purification

Vajrasattva

Vajrasattva is a bodhisattva in Buddhism, honored especially in Tibetan, Newar and East Asian traditions (Chinese Buddhism and Japanese Shingon). He matters because he embodies purification and stands at the heart of Buddhist tantric practice.

The myth

In the Mahavairocana Sutra, Vajrasattva leads a host of beings who visit the Buddha Vairocana to learn the Dharma. He asks about the cause, goal and foundation of all-embracing wisdom. Since the audience cannot grasp the teaching, the Buddha turns to the mandala. Later Vajrasattva asks why rituals and objects are needed if truth lies beyond form, and Vairocana replies that they are skillful means that bring practitioners to awakening more readily.

According to the Shingon tradition, Vajrasattva passed the esoteric teachings to Nagarjuna, whom he met in an iron tower in southern India. For this reason he is considered the second patriarch of esoteric Buddhism, after Vairocana. His iconography shows him white and luminous, with a vajra over his heart and a vajra-bell in his left hand.

Worship and religion

His hundred-syllable mantra is recited in Chinese tantric rites such as the Yujia Yankou, meant to nourish hungry ghosts and prolong the lives of the living. In Tibetan Buddhism it forms part of the ngondro, the preliminary purification practices, and Newar priests of the Kathmandu Valley invoke him often. His practice continues today across these lineages.

Legacy

After the attacks of September 11, 2001, Dzogchen Ponlop Rinpoche launched Prayer 4 Peace, a project to gather one billion recitations of the six-syllable Vajrasattva mantra.

Ancient sources: The Mahavairocana Sutra and the Vajrasekhara Sutra, along with the Ghanavyuha sutra and the Kulayaraja Tantra of Dzogchen.

Trivia

A name with two meanings. Vajrasattva translates as Diamond Being or Thunderbolt Being. The vajra, a symbol of insight, is associated with Esoteric Buddhism.
A shortened mantra. The six-syllable mantra (om Vajrasattva hum) is a simpler version of the hundred-syllable one and keeps its essential points, according to the lama Jamgon Kongtrul.
