Egyptian symbol of the unification of Upper and Lower Egypt

Sema-tawy

The Sema-tawy is an Egyptian symbol expressing the union of Upper and Lower Egypt. It is made of the lotus and the papyrus, the heraldic plants of each region, tied together around the sema sign, which meant union.

The myth

In Egyptian iconography, two gods were usually shown performing the act of binding the two plants. Most often this was Hapi, the god of the Nile, shown in a double figure, though Thoth and Horus, or the opposed gods Horus and Seth, could also appear. As an amulet placed on the deceased, it ensured that the body would remain whole and enjoy eternal life.

Worship and religion

It formed part of the pharaoh's enthronement, the second of three ceremonies, held after proclaiming his titulary and performing purifications. A wooden sema with interlaced papyrus and lotus stood for the joining of the two lands and placed the ruler under the protection of both plants.

In art

Trivia

Very old. The earliest known depiction of the symbol dates from the reign of Pharaoh Den, of the First Dynasty.
On royal thrones. The symbol appeared on the sides of the thrones of seated statues of pharaohs such as Khafre or Menkaure.
