Inuit goddess of the sea and marine animals

Sedna

Sedna is the Inuit goddess of the sea and of marine animals, also called Mother of the Sea. Her origin myth explains how she came to rule Adlivun, the Inuit underworld.

The myth

According to the stories, a young woman refused her human suitors and ended up married to a dog or a bird, depending on the version. When her father threw her overboard, she clung to the edge of the boat, and he cut off her fingers so she would let go. Her fingers turned into seals, walruses and whales, while she sank to the sea floor and became a powerful mistress of marine animals. In some versions she lives in an undersea house and holds back game when hunters break taboos.

Worship and religion

Angakkuit, or shamans, travelled to her undersea home to negotiate and calm her anger. Sometimes the rite meant brushing and braiding her hair, which she could not do without fingers. Inuit women traditionally tattooed lines across their fingers, a nod to her story.

Legacy

Her image appears on a Greenlandic postage stamp, in a statue in Nuuk and on a 2024 Canadian two-dollar coin.

Trivia

A name popularised. Outside Inuit languages, the name Sedna became common thanks to Franz Boas, who recorded her stories.
Many names. Depending on the community she has different names, such as Nuliajuk or Takánâluk, since in some areas her proper name was avoided out of respect.
