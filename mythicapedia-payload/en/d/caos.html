primordial deity of Greek mythology

Chaos

Chaos is the first being in Greek cosmogony, the state that existed before the gods and the elemental forces. Its name, linked to the idea of a gap or abyss, presents it more as an open void than as a clearly defined figure.

The myth

In Hesiod's Theogony, Chaos was the first thing to exist, and afterwards Gaia, Tartarus and Eros came into being. The poet does not explicitly say that these were born from Chaos, although many scholars take this for granted, since Chaos was the only reality at the start. What he does say is that Erebus, Darkness, and Nyx, Night, were born from Chaos, and that from Nyx came Aether and Hemera, Day.

The genealogies vary between authors. The Orphics made Chaos a son of Chronos, Time, and Ananke; the Romans linked him with Caligo, Mist. In Aristophanes' Birds, Chaos and Eros produced the races of birds. Other versions make him the parent of Aether and the cosmic egg.

Hesiod also describes Chaos as a distant, gloomy, underground place, located below the Earth and above Tartarus, and reached by Zeus's thunderbolts when he fought the Titans.

Legacy

The word entered English and other European languages with the sense of complete disorder, although its original Greek meaning was gap or abyss. In philosophy, Anaximander and Heraclitus treated ideas close to chaos as the foundation of reality. In medieval and Renaissance alchemy, the first stage of the great work, the nigredo, was associated with chaos, and Paracelsus linked it with the formless mass of the elements. In the Bible, some modern scholars relate the tohu wa-bohu of Genesis to the chaos of Babylonian myths.

Ancient sources: Hesiod's Theogony is the main source; Ovid describes it in the Metamorphoses as a formless mass; Aristophanes mentions it with Eros and the birds in Birds.

Trivia

A name shaped like an abyss. The Greek χάος is related to a verb meaning to gape or open wide, so its original sense is an opening rather than disorder.
An uncertain gender. Hesiod uses it in the neuter, Aristophanes hints it could be feminine, and Boccaccio said he did not know its gender.
