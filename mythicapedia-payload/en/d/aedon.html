Greek heroine turned into a nightingale

Aedon

Aedon (or Aedón) is a figure of Greek mythology, daughter of Pandareus, best known for the story that explains the origin of the nightingale, whose song is heard as a lament for her child. Her story survives in two different versions, one set in Thebes and one in Asia Minor, and the Asia Minor version closely mirrors the myth of Procne.

The myth

In the Theban version, Aedon is the wife of Zethus, king of Thebes, and the mother of Itylus. Jealous of Niobe, whose large family contrasted with her single son, she plotted to kill Niobe's eldest child, Amaleus. One night Itylus slept in the wrong place in the bed, and Aedon killed him by mistake. In another variant she did it knowingly, to avoid Niobe's vengeance. Zeus turned her into a nightingale, and ever since the bird has mourned her son.

In the tradition from Asia Minor, preserved by Antoninus Liberalis, Aedon is married to Polytechnus, a craftsman from Colophon. The couple boasted that they loved each other more than Hera and Zeus loved one another, and Hera sent Eris to stir up trouble. They competed at a task, and Aedon won. Polytechnus, bound to give her a slave as the prize, raped her sister Chelidon and handed her over as the reward. The sisters took revenge: they killed Polytechnus's son Itys and served his flesh to his father. They fled to their father's house, where he bound Polytechnus and left him to the insects. Aedon pitied her husband, and her kin prepared to kill her. Zeus turned them all into birds: Polytechnus into a woodpecker, Aedon into a nightingale and Chelidon into a swallow.

This version seems to have grown out of simple etymologies, and it is nearly identical to the myth of Philomela and Procne.

Ancient sources: Homer mentions her in the Odyssey (book XIX), in Penelope's words; a scholiast attributes to Pherecydes the rivalry with Niobe, and Antoninus Liberalis preserves the Asia Minor version, which he attributes to Boios.

Trivia

Male song. Despite the tradition of the lament, in reality only the male nightingale sings; the female does not.
Name and meaning. Her Greek name, Άηδών, means nightingale and shares a root with the verb meaning to sing or to praise.
