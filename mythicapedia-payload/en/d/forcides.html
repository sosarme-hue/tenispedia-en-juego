daughters of Phorcys, sea monsters of Greek myth

Phorcydes

The Phorcydes are, in Greek myth, the "daughters of Phorcys," a pre-Olympian sea god. The name is a patronymic that mythographers apply mostly to the Graeae and, at times, to the Gorgons, female monsters linked to the sea with serpent-like features.

The myth

According to Apollodorus, Phorcys and Ceto fathered the Phorcydes and the Gorgons. The Phorcydes were sisters of the Gorgons and old from birth, and they shared a single eye and a single tooth. Perseus went to meet them in one episode of his story.

In Hesiod's Theogony, the daughters of Phorcys, without using the patronymic, are the Graeae, the Gorgons, the serpent-like creature guarding the golden apples and, doubtfully, Echidna. Other mythographers widen the list to include Ladon, Scylla, the Hesperides, the Sirens, Toosa and the sea monster of Troy. The sources vary in names and in maternal parentage.

Ancient sources: Hesiod's Theogony and Apollodorus's Library, along with later mythographers who use the term as a synonym for the Graeae.

Trivia

A lost tragedy. The term Phorcydes also gives its name to a lost tragedy by Aeschylus.
A rarely used masculine. The masculine equivalent, Phorcidas, does not appear in mythography; the exception is in Goethe's Faust.
