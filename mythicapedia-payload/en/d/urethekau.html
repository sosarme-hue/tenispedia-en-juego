Egyptian goddess of magic

Urethekau

Urethekau is the Egyptian goddess of magic and supernatural powers, whose name roughly means "the great one of magic." She matters because her power was seen as inherent in Egypt's royal crowns and because she watched over the dead, mothers and kings.

The myth

Surviving depictions show Urethekau either as a lioness-headed woman or as a serpent with a woman's head. As the wife of Ra-Horakhty she wore his solar disk on her head, along with the shuty crown and a cobra on her brow. Her main role was protection. Her name appeared on funerary objects, especially weapons, so the deceased could defend themselves against the dangers of the underworld, and on ivory knives that served as charms for pregnant and nursing mothers.

In Tutankhamun's tomb, a scene shows the goddess Isis in the process of becoming Urethekau: a serpent with human arms, breasts and head, nursing the young pharaoh to infuse him with divine power and authority. In this image Urethekau acts as nurse and protector of kings, and the inscription calls the pharaoh "beloved of Urethekau."

Worship and religion

As goddess of the crowns, she was imagined as a serpent or a lioness-headed woman who dwelt in the state sanctuaries. Her power was identified with the royal regalia, so the crown was felt to carry her protection to the ruler. Her name was also a frequent epithet given to other goddesses, such as Isis, Sekhmet and Mut, who received it as a title.

Trivia

Charm for mothers. Her name appeared on ivory knives used as charms to protect pregnant and nursing mothers.
Beloved of the goddess. In Tutankhamun's tomb, the inscription accompanying the nursing scene calls the pharaoh "beloved of Urethekau."
