Egyptian god of the Earth

Geb

Geb is the Egyptian god of the Earth, a member of the Ennead of Heliopolis and the personification of both fertile soil and barren desert. He was regarded as a source of life and fertility, and his figure is linked to the throne of Egypt's earliest rulers.

The myth

In the Heliopolitan account of creation, Geb was the son of Shu, god of air, and Tefnut, moisture. He married his sister Nut, the sky, and together they formed the lasting boundary between the primeval waters and the newly made world. Some tellings say the two were joined before Shu forced them apart, which is why he is often shown reclining, sometimes with his phallus still pointed toward Nut. Their children were Osiris, Seth, Isis and Nephthys, and in some versions the bird Bennu also emerged from a great egg they produced.

According to tradition, Geb inherited the throne from Shu and later handed authority over the Earth to Osiris. After the struggles with Seth, who had killed Osiris, power passed to Horus and then to the pharaoh, regarded as Geb's heir. The Book of the Dead presents him as watching over the Hall of Judgment during the weighing of the heart, and holding back those who had not lived justly. He was also guardian of the gates of the Duat. His laughter was said to cause earthquakes, and barley was believed to grow from his ribs.

Worship and religion

The sources note that Geb had no temple of his own, but he was worshipped at Heliopolis, Thebes, Edfu, Kom Ombo and Serabit el-Khadim in Sinai. As a creator god he was identified with Atum. The texts do not describe his festivals in detail, and there is no clear evidence that his cult survives today.

Legacy

In the Greco-Roman period he was equated with the Titan Cronus, since both were fathers of the gods. At Tebtunis in the Fayum, his priests were called priests of Soknebtunis-Geb in Egyptian texts and priests of Soknebtunis-Cronus in Greek ones. Some Egyptologists once linked Geb to a creator goose, but that idea is now considered a confusion with the name of a different bird.

Ancient sources: The Book of the Dead and the Coffin Texts, as well as the Book of the Heavenly Cow, which place him among the ancient divine rulers of Egypt.

Trivia

A god without a temple. Despite his importance, Geb never received a temple of his own. His oldest known image is a fragmentary relief from the Third Dynasty, found at Heliopolis.
The goose that was not his. The goose linked to Geb in some modern theories comes from confusing his name with that of a white-fronted goose, a bird that was never a symbol of this god.
