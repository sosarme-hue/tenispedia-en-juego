demoness of ancient Mesopotamia

Lamashtu

Lamashtu, also called Dimme or Labartu, is a feared female deity of ancient Mesopotamia and the daughter of the sky god Anu. Although often described as a demon, she was treated as a goddess in her own right, blamed for miscarriages, cot deaths and disease.

The myth

According to Mesopotamian lore, Lamashtu preyed mainly on newborns and pregnant women. She was said to touch the mother's belly seven times to kill the unborn child, or to snatch the baby from the wet nurse's lap. Her victims could also include adolescents, adults and the elderly. She brought nightmares and illness, and was linked to the withering of plants and the poisoning of waters.

Unlike many demons, who acted on the gods' orders, Lamashtu acted of her own accord. The Atra-Hasis myth, however, places her within the divine order: Enki mentions her as a way to check the growth of humankind, under the name Pashittu, "the Exterminator". Her seven names appear in incantations, where she is sometimes identified with Inanna. To protect themselves, people called on Pazuzu, a demon who guarded mothers and infants against her.

Worship and religion

The sources do not mention temples or festivals devoted to Lamashtu. Her presence in daily life appeared in bronze, clay and obsidian amulets worn on the body or placed near beds and doorways, and in exorcistic rituals in which specialists tried to drive her out of the sick. These practices are no longer alive today.

Legacy

Her image, with a lion's head, donkey teeth and bird's feet, appears on plaques and amulets from the first millennium BCE. In modern culture she features in the video game Vampire: The Masquerade – Bloodlines (2004), the Pathfinder roleplaying game, the TV series Constantine (2014) and the film Still/Born (2017), and she gives her name to a song by the band Necrophobic.

Ancient sources: Mesopotamian incantations and amulets, and the Atra-Hasis myth, where she appears as Pashittu.

In art

Trivia

A plaque in the Louvre. The Neo-Assyrian "Plaque des Enfers" in the Louvre shows Lamashtu on a donkey in a boat, with a dog and a pig suckling at her breasts, while Pazuzu appears as an opposing protective force.
One demon against another. Pazuzu was himself a demonic being, yet his image was used to repel Lamashtu. His head was worn as a pendant or built into plaques to drive her away.
