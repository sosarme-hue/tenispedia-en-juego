centaur of Greek mythology

Nessus

Nessus is a centaur from Greek mythology, son of Ixion and Nephele, best known for his role in the death of Heracles. His story, that of the Tunic of Nessus, is one of the most famous tales of the deadly power of the Hydra's blood.

The myth

According to the tradition, Nessus was a centaur who worked as a ferryman on the river Euenos. When Deianeira, the wife of Heracles, had to cross it, Nessus carried her on his back and, halfway across, tried to abduct her. Heracles saw this from the far bank and struck him with an arrow poisoned with the blood of the Lernaean Hydra, a monster he had slain as one of his labours for Eurystheus.

As he lay dying, Nessus told Deianeira that his blood would keep Heracles faithful to her forever. Years later, fearing she was losing him, she soaked a robe in that blood and sent it to her husband. The poison burned his skin, and Heracles endured a slow and agonising death, until, according to the account, he had a pyre built on Mount Oeta. Sophocles retold this story in Trachiniae. Some versions weave it into the story of Medea, and others give Nessus a different father, Centauros, so the genealogies vary.

Legacy

Dante places him in the Inferno among the centaurs who guard the Phlegethon, and T. S. Eliot alludes to the episode in Little Gidding, one of his Four Quartets. He also appears, loosely, in the television movie Hercules in the Underworld (1994). The phrase "the shirt of Nessus" has been used as an allusion to settlers who gave infected blankets to Native Americans.

Ancient sources: Sophocles, in Trachiniae, retells the story of Heracles' death and the poisoned robe.

Trivia

Possible origin of the name. One proposed etymology for "centaur" links it to the meaning "water-whipper" or "water spirit". Nessus, a ferryman on a river, fits that reading.
Earlier origins. According to some sources, before the Greeks told the story of Nessus, the Kassites already used centaurs as guiding spirits.
