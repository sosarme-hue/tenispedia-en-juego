Etruscan goddess of wisdom and war

Menrva

Menrva is the Etruscan goddess of wisdom, war, art and commerce, a member of the supreme triad with Tinia and Uni. She came before the Roman Minerva and was identified with Athena, though she kept traits of her own, such as her link to lightning.

The myth

According to Etruscan tradition, Menrva was born from the head of her father Tinia, already armed and armoured, just like the Greek Athena. Etruscan art shows her in many of the same adventures as her Greek counterpart: she protects heroes such as Hercle (Heracles) and Pherse (Perseus), backs the Achaeans in the Trojan War and joins Jason against the dragon guarding the Golden Fleece.

Some episodes are distinctly her own. On a 4th-century BC bronze mirror she attacks the giant Akrathe with an arm she has torn from his body, an image found only in Etruria. Unlike the Greek tradition that presents Athena as a childless maiden, several mirrors show her paired with Hercle, sometimes holding a child, which has led some scholars to think they were Epiur's parents or foster parents.

Worship and religion

Her sanctuaries had altars and structures for libations, some built partly into the ground, which suggests a chthonic aspect. Worshippers left terracotta votive offerings shaped like parts of the body to seek healing, and they also prayed to her for the protection of children, the military defence of their communities and the legitimacy of their rulers. The sources do not say whether her cult survives today.

Legacy

Her name may derive from the Indo-European root *men-, 'mind', and is linked to Greek words for memory such as mnestis. Her image as a lightning thrower passed to the Roman Minerva, something the Greeks never attributed to Athena.

Ancient sources: Martianus Capella lists her among nine Etruscan lightning deities; much of what we know comes from Etruscan bronze mirrors, reliefs and other works of art.

Trivia

A severed arm as a weapon. On a 4th-century BC Etruscan mirror, Menrva fights the giant Akrathe using the arm she has torn from him. This image appears only in works from Etruria.
A goddess of lightning. Menrva often appears holding a thunderbolt, an attribute the Greeks never linked to Athena. The Roman Minerva seems to have inherited this aspect.
