Ethiopian princess and wife of Perseus in Greek myth

Andromeda

Andromeda is the daughter of the Ethiopian king Cepheus and queen Cassiopeia, and the wife of Perseus. Her rescue from a sea monster is one of the best-known Greek stories in art, and it gave rise to the princess-and-dragon motif.

The myth

According to the tradition, Cassiopeia boasted that she, or her daughter, was more beautiful than the Nereids. Poseidon, their protector, punished the kingdom by flooding its coast and sending the sea monster Cetus. The oracle of Ammon told Cepheus that the only way to appease the gods was to sacrifice his daughter, and Andromeda was chained naked to a rock by the sea, at Joppa in some versions.

Perseus, returning from killing Medusa, saw her and fell in love. He asked for her hand in exchange for saving her, killed the monster by showing it Medusa's head, and turned it to stone. Andromeda, however, had been promised to her uncle Phineus, who attacked the wedding. Perseus defeated him and his followers with the same head and married her.

The couple went to Seriphos and then to Argos or Tiryns, depending on the account. According to Apollodorus, they had six sons, Perses, Alcaeus, Heleus, Mestor, Sthenelus and Electryon, and a daughter, Gorgophone. Perses was said to be the ancestor of the kings of Persia, and Electryon of the rulers of Mycenae, including Heracles through his mother. After her death, Athena placed her among the stars as a constellation, near her husband and mother.

Legacy

The constellation Andromeda, which contains the Andromeda Galaxy, keeps her name in the northern sky. Her story has inspired art since antiquity, from frescoes and mosaics to painted pottery, and the motif of the princess chained to a monster has returned in later plays, poetry, opera and painting.

Ancient sources: Apollodorus, Ovid's Metamorphoses, Hyginus, Conon, and Ptolemy, who mentions the constellation in the 2nd century; also the tragedies of Sophocles and Euripides.
