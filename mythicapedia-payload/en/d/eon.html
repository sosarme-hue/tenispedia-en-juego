Greek god of eternity and cyclical time

Aion

Aion is a Hellenistic deity, later adopted by the Romans, who personifies eternal, cyclical and unbounded time, in contrast to the linear time represented by Chronos. His image spread across the Roman world as a symbol of the lasting power of the empire.

The myth

Euripides calls Aion a son of Zeus, while other ancient accounts describe him as the son of Time. Unlike many gods, he has no sequence of adventures; his importance lies in what he stands for. He is a deity without beginning or end, who governs the long cycles of the year, the zodiac and the ages of the world.

In later tradition he was linked to other gods. Martianus Capella identifies him with Cronus (Saturn) and presents him as the consort of Rhea, identified with Physis. Nonnus of Panopolis, in the Dionysiaca, describes him with the Horae, renewing himself like a snake shedding its skin. Christian and Neoplatonic writers identified him with Dionysus, although there are no pre-Christian references to that identification. In Alexandria, Serapis was called Aion Plutonius, and local tradition celebrated Aion's birth on 6 January.

Worship and religion

Aion appears in mystery cults that promised life after death, such as those of Cybele, Dionysus, Orphism and Mithraism. He is often shown alongside a mother or earth goddess, such as Tellus or Cybele. In Alexandria his birth was celebrated at the close of the New Year festivities. His worship does not survive today.

Legacy

His best-known image is the young nude man within a zodiac circle, found in Roman mosaics from Sentinum and Hippo Regius and on the Parabiago plate. Together with the serpent biting its tail, the ouroboros, it symbolises cyclical time. In Rome, coins of Antoninus Pius bore his legend, and his female counterpart, Aeternitas, appeared on coins linked to the phoenix as a symbol of rebirth.

Ancient sources: Euripides calls him a son of Zeus, Nonnus of Panopolis describes him in the Dionysiaca, and Horapollo mentions him in his work on hieroglyphs.

Trivia

A snake with two meanings. Horapollo distinguishes between the snake that hides its tail under its body, which represents Aion, and the ouroboros, which devours its own tail and represents the cosmos.
A birth on 6 January. According to Epiphanius, in Alexandria Aion's birth was celebrated on 6 January, a date that coincided with Epiphany and closed the New Year celebrations.
