hammer of the god Thor in Norse mythology

Mjölnir

Mjölnir is the hammer of the thunder god Thor in Norse mythology, serving both as a devastating weapon and as a divine tool that bestows blessings. Its name is commonly read as "the grinder" or "crusher", and the hammer remains one of the most recognisable symbols of Norse and Viking culture.

The myth

According to the tradition, Mjölnir is the most fearsome weapon in the gods' arsenal, used to destroy anyone who challenges the supremacy of the Æsir. Sources describe it as a club, an axe or a hammer, and its magic let Thor strike with whatever force he chose, from a gentle tap to a blow that could shatter mountains. Thunder and lightning were said to follow its blows, and the hammer could shrink small enough to be tucked inside a tunic.

The best-known account of its making appears in the Skáldskaparmál of Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda. After Loki cuts off the hair of Sif, Thor's wife, he must seek the sons of Ivaldi, skilled dwarf craftsmen. Loki wagers his head that the dwarves Sindri and Brokk cannot match them. While Sindri steps away from the forge, Loki, disguised as a fly, bites Brokk, who keeps working the bellows. The finished hammer has a handle that is a little short, but Loki cannot claim the wager, so Brokk sews his lips shut.

Other tales show Thor using the hammer against giants. He crushes the stone that the giant Hrungnir hurls at him, then strikes the giant's skull. In the fishing trip for Jörmungandr, the Midgard serpent, he hurls the hammer at the monster, though it does not kill it. In Þrymskviða, the giant Þrymr steals Mjölnir and demands Freyja as his bride, and Thor must dress as a bride to win it back.

Worship and religion

According to the sources, Thor was especially venerated by farmers, and during the Viking Age the hammer was commonly worn as a pendant across the Scandinavian world. The 11th-century Kvinneby amulet calls on Thor and his hammer for protection. Today the symbol is again worn as a pendant by various groups, including adherents of modern Heathenry.

Legacy

Mjölnir appears in countless works of art, comics, films and video games, and its shape has become a popular emblem of Norse mythology. Some scholars have proposed that its name relates to words in many European languages meaning "grind" or "hammer", such as Latin malleus and English mill, though the connection remains debated among linguists.

Ancient sources: The hammer is attested in the 11th-century Kvinneby amulet, in the Poetic Edda poems Vafþrúðnismál, Hymiskviða, Lokasenna and Þrymskviða, and in Gylfaginning and Skáldskaparmál of Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda.

Trivia

A handle that is too short. In the story of its forging, Mjölnir's handle is a little short and not set at a right angle to the head, because it was meant to be used with one hand.
A weapon that returns. According to some tales, when Thor threw it, the hammer always found its way back to his hand.
