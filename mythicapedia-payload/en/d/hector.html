prince and hero of Troy in Greek mythology

Hector

Hector was the Trojan prince, eldest son of Priam and Hecuba, and the greatest defender of Troy during the war told in Homer's Iliad. His duel with Achilles and his death at Achilles' hands make him one of the central figures of Greek epic and the human opponent at the heart of the poem.

The myth

Hector was the brother of Paris and Cassandra and was married to Andromache, with whom he had a son, Astyanax. In some traditions his true father was the god Apollo. Homer presents him as a warrior feared by the Greeks but also as a thoughtful man who did not approve of the war. When he saw Paris avoiding combat with Menelaus, he scolded him, and this led to a proposed duel between the two that would have settled the conflict, although the fight was cut short when Aphrodite whisked Paris away.

The Iliad tells how, during the siege, Hector challenged any Greek warrior to single combat. Ajax accepted, and the duel lasted a full day without a winner. In the end the two men showed mutual respect and exchanged gifts: Hector gave his sword to Ajax, and Ajax gave him his belt. Afterwards the Trojans drove the Greeks back to their ships, and Hector tried to set them on fire. When Patroclus entered the fight wearing Achilles' armour, Hector killed him and took those weapons for himself.

Patroclus' death brought Achilles back into the war. Hector, who had withdrawn inside the walls on the advice of Polydamas, was left outside the gates when the Trojans retreated. According to the story, Athena took the form of his brother Deiphobus and urged him to face Achilles. After a chase of three laps around the city, Hector proposed a deal to return the body of whichever man fell, but Achilles refused. He killed Hector with a spear thrust to the base of the neck, the one spot his armour did not cover.

Achilles tied the corpse to his chariot and dragged it around Troy while the Trojans wept from the walls. According to tradition, Apollo protected the body for twelve days from animals and decay. In the end King Priam, guided by Hermes, went to Achilles' tent and begged for his son's return. Achilles agreed in exchange for a ransom, and Hector received his funeral rites in Troy.

Worship and religion

The texts cited do not describe a wide cult of Hector in Greek religion, although he is known to have had a historic cult in Thebes. Some scholars, such as Dümmler and Finley, have suggested that Homer's hero drew partly on an earlier Theban hero of the same name, and that the Troy story may have begun as a tale of fighting among Greeks before moving to the Anatolian coast.

Ancient sources: His role is chiefly recorded in Homer's Iliad, and his physical description comes from the sixth-century chronicler John Malalas, who drew on the account attributed to Dares Phrygius.

Trivia

The horse tamer. Hector was known by the epithet hippodamoio, meaning «horse tamer».
A name on Mycenaean tablets. The name Hector appears on a Linear B tablet referring to a servant, showing it was already in use in Mycenaean times.
