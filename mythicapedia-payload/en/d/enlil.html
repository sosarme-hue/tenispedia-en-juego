Sumerian god of wind and storm

Enlil

Enlil is the lord of wind, air, and storms in Sumerian and Mesopotamian mythology. For much of Mesopotamian history he was regarded as the supreme god of the pantheon, and his cult centred on Nippur until Marduk took his place.

The myth

According to Sumerian tradition, Enlil was the son of the sky god An and the earth goddess Ki. At his birth he separated heaven from earth, and together with An and his half-brother Enki he formed the supreme triad of the pantheon. He kept the Tablets of Destiny, on which the fate of everything that exists was recorded.

The myth of Enlil and Ninlil tells how the god seduced the goddess as she bathed in the marshes, despite her warnings. For this act he was expelled from Nippur, but Ninlil followed him, and they had three more unions that produced gods of the underworld.

In the Atrahasis account, Enlil tried three times to destroy humanity, annoyed by the noise people made. In the last attempt he flooded the earth, but his half-brother Enki warned Atrahasis, who built a boat to save seeds and animals. The other gods rebuked Enlil, since they depended on the sacrifices humans offered them for their food. In the Sumerian flood story, Ziusudra survives and is granted immortality.

Worship and religion

His main centre of worship was the Ekur, the Mountain House, in Nippur. It was believed to have been built by Enlil himself and to serve as the link between heaven and earth. Priests cared for his cult statue and offered it food, which was afterwards distributed among the priests. Kings travelled to Nippur to have their rule legitimised. The city was the only Sumerian city-state that never built a palace, since its true ruler was the god himself. His cult declined after the Elamites sacked Nippur around 1230 BC, and it is not practised today.

Legacy

His name appears in forms such as Elil or Ellil in Akkadian, Hittite, and Canaanite texts. Some scholars suggest it derives from the Semitic root il, which also gave rise to El and Allah. The horned cap, a symbol of his divinity, remained almost unchanged for millennia.

Ancient sources: He appears in the Code of Hammurabi and the Epic of Gilgamesh, and in Sumerian and Akkadian texts such as the myth of Enlil and Ninlil and the Atrahasis account.

Trivia

A city without a palace. Nippur was the only Sumerian city-state that never built a palace, because its king was considered to be Enlil himself.
Horned crown. His symbol was a cap with up to seven superimposed pairs of ox horns, a sign of divinity used since the third millennium BC.
