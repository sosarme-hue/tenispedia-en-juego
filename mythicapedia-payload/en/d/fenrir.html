Fenrir

Fenrir is a monstrous wolf of Norse mythology, son of Loki and the giantess Angrboda, and one of the creatures the gods feared most. He plays a central role in Ragnarök, the end of the world, where he is destined to kill Odin.

The myth

According to myth, Fenrir was raised alongside his siblings Jörmungandr, the serpent, and Hel. The gods knew prophecies that foretold great trouble from this brood, so Odin threw Jörmungandr into the sea that surrounds all lands and sent Hel to Niflheim, giving her authority over nine worlds. Fenrir, however, was brought to Asgard, where the Æsir kept him under watch, and only Týr dared approach to feed him.

Fenrir grew rapidly, and the gods tried to restrain him. First they used the fetter Leyding, which the wolf snapped with a single kick, and then the stronger Dromi, which he also broke. The gods then commissioned the dwarves to forge Gleipnir, a ribbon as smooth and soft as silk, yet made from six impossible ingredients: the footstep of a cat, the beard of a woman, the breath of a fish, the roots of a mountain, the sinews of a bear and the spittle of a bird. Fenrir distrusted the gods and agreed to be bound only if one of them placed a hand in his mouth as a pledge. Only Týr volunteered. When the wolf discovered he could not break free, he closed his jaws and bit off the god's right hand.

In the Poetic Edda, a völva tells Odin that Fenrir will devour him at Ragnarök. Víðarr, Odin's son, will then avenge his father by driving a sword into the beast's heart. The Prose Edda adds that Fenrir is the father of the wolves Sköll and Hati.

Legacy

Fenrir has inspired many artistic depictions and appears in literature and video games. Some ancient objects bearing images of wolves have been linked to him, and scholars have proposed connections with other canine beings in Norse mythology.

Ancient sources: He is attested in the Poetic Edda (Völuspá, Vafþrúðnismál and Lokasenna), the Prose Edda and the Heimskringla.

Trivia

Popular sayings. According to the Prose Edda, 'to loose from Leyding' or 'to strike out of Dromi' became sayings for an achievement won with great effort.
The wolf-joint. The Prose Edda places the bite at a spot called the wolf-joint, that is, the wrist.
A god without a hand. The loss of his hand meant Týr was not considered a promoter of agreements between peoples.
