Etruscan god of the sun

Usil

Usil is the sun god of Etruscan religion, usually shown as a young man with a halo or radiate crown rising from the sea. After the Romans conquered Etruria, he was identified with the Roman sun god Sol.

The myth

The surviving texts say little about his story, so most of what we know comes from images. On bronze mirrors he appears rising from the sea with a ball of fire in each hand, often beside Thesan, the dawn goddess, sometimes in a chariot.

Some depictions link him to Catha, sometimes called Solis Filia, or daughter of the Sun, although scholars disagree about the nature of that relationship.

Worship and religion

He appears in the Liber Linteus and the Tabula Capuana, texts that may have served as ritual calendars with festivals and worship practices. His name also appears on the Liver of Piacenza, a bronze model possibly used in divination, next to that of Tiur, the moon. Yet he is not named in any votive offering, so his worship is poorly known.

Legacy

The Romans equated him with Sol and the Greeks with Helios. His images appeared on the burial chariots of Etruscan elites, fastened to funeral processions.

Ancient sources: The Liber Linteus (c. 3rd century BCE) and the Tabula Capuana (c. 470 BCE) mention him.

Trivia

The Liver of Piacenza. This bronze model of a sheep's liver carries the names of 27 deities. Usil's name is on its convex side, next to that of Tiur.
Usually male. Although he is usually shown as a man, some artworks depict him in female form.
