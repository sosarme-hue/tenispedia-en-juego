Mordred

Mordred is a figure of the Arthurian legend of Britain, best known as the traitor who fights King Arthur and dies with him at the Battle of Camlann. His origins lie in early Welsh tradition, but his role as Arthur's villainous son is largely a creation of medieval romance.

The myth

The earliest Welsh sources say little about him. The Annals of Wales record the battle of Camlann under the year 537, naming Arthur and Medraut as its casualties, without saying whether they fought together or against each other. In the twelfth century, Geoffrey of Monmouth makes Modredus Arthur's nephew and a traitor: he leaves him in charge of the kingdom while the king campaigns against Lucius Tiberius, and Mordred crowns himself and marries Guinevere. Arthur returns, both fall at Camlann, and the king is carried off to Avalon.

In thirteenth-century French romance, the Lancelot-Grail cycle builds the version that became famous: Mordred is Arthur's son by his half-sister, an incest that is usually unknowing. A prophecy warns that a child born on May Day will destroy the kingdom, so Arthur orders all the May newborns of the nobility set adrift in a rickety ship. It sinks, but Mordred survives, is rescued and returned to his parents, though the king never accepts him. When he learns the truth, Mordred exposes Guinevere's affair with Lancelot and, taking advantage of the civil war, makes himself king until the battle ends both their lives.

Legacy

Mordred is a stock villain in modern adaptations. Dante places him in the lowest circle of Hell, among the traitors, recalling the wound Arthur gave him. Modern versions vary widely: in Marion Zimmer Bradley's The Mists of Avalon his mother is Morgaine; in John Boorman's film Excalibur and the BBC series Merlin he appears as a villain or as a young druid protected by Merlin; in T. H. White's The Once and Future King he is a bitter populist. Other works present him as a victim of fate, such as Rosemary Sutcliff's Sword at Sunset or Elizabeth Wein's The Winter Prince.

Ancient sources: From the Welsh tradition, the Annales Cambriae, the Historia Brittonum, the Welsh Triads and the Mabinogion; from the medieval tradition, Geoffrey of Monmouth's Historia Regum Britanniae and the Lancelot-Grail cycle.

Trivia

A narrator dog. The Victorian poet George Augustus Simcox wrote the poem My Master Mordred, narrated from the point of view of the character's dog.
A traitor in Hell. Dante mentions him in Canto XXXII of the Divine Comedy, describing him as the one whose chest and shadow were torn apart by Arthur's hand.
