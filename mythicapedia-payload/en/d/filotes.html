Greek goddess of affection, friendship and tenderness

Philotes

Philotes is the Greek personification of friendship, tenderness and sexual intimacy, a figure who remains more an abstraction than a god with stories of her own. She appears mainly in Hesiod's Theogony and in later authors, and helps show how the Greeks thought about feelings and the forces that move the world.

The myth

In Hesiod's Theogony, Philotes is one of the children of Nyx (Night), with no father named. Her siblings include Apate (Deceit), Nemesis (Indignation) and Eris (Strife). Hesiod presents her as the bond of sexual and social union between people.

Roman authors offer other genealogies. Hyginus calls her Amicitia, Friendship, and has her born of Nox and Erebus. Cicero names her Gratia, Charm, also as a daughter of Nox and Erebus. Other accounts make her a child of Jupiter and Autonoe, or of Liber and Venus, showing that the ancients did not agree on her origin.

Empedocles gave her a cosmic role. For him, Philotes is paired with Eris: she is the force behind good things, while Eris drives the bad. He identifies her with Aphrodite and says she is hurt by offerings that destroy life, so he demands abstention from animal sacrifice.

Ancient sources: Hesiod places her among the children of Night in the Theogony, and Empedocles includes her among the cosmic forces of his doctrine of Love and Strife.

Trivia

A butterfly named after her. The name Philotes was given to a genus of butterflies native to North America, which contains a single species, Philotes sonorensis.
Friendship, Charm or Tenderness. Latin authors change her name depending on the virtue they stress: Amicitia for friendship and Gratia for charm or grace.
