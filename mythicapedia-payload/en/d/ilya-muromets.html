legendary bogatyr of Kievan Rus'

Ilya Muromets

Ilya Muromets is the most famous bogatyr of Russian epic poetry, a legendary hero of Kievan Rus'. The son of a peasant, he is counted, together with Dobrynya Nikitich and Alyosha Popovich, among the three greatest bogatyrs.

The myth

According to the byliny, epic poems collected in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, Ilya was born in the village of Karacharovo, near Murom. He could not walk until the age of thirty-three, when two pilgrims healed him and the dying knight Svyatogor gave him superhuman strength. On his way to Kiev to serve Prince Vladimir, he single-handedly defended Chernigov from invaders and refused to stay on as the city's knight. In the forests of Bryansk he slew the robber Nightingale, whose deadly whistle killed travelers.

In Kiev he became chief of the bogatyrs and defended the land from raids out of the steppe. Generous and straightforward but quick-tempered, he once destroyed the church steeples of Kiev after being left out of a celebration, and he calmed down as soon as the prince sent for him. Scholars note that his figure may blend several real or imagined people from different eras, such as Vladimir I, Batu Khan, or a Byzantine emperor called Constantine the God-Loving.

Worship and religion

Ilya Pechersky, a twelfth-century monk at the Kiev Pechersk Lavra, is proposed by some as the historical model for the hero. Canonized in 1643, his relics are kept in that monastery. He is the only epic hero canonized by the Orthodox Church, and his veneration continues today.

Legacy

He appears in Viktor Vasnetsov's painting Bogatyrs (1898) and in Reinhold Glière's Symphony No. 3 (1911). Aleksandr Ptushko brought him to the screen in 1956, and Catterino Cavos wrote an opera about him in 1807. Several icebreakers bear his name, and Liz Williams brought him into modern science fiction in Nine Layers of Sky (2003).

Trivia

A curious nickname. According to hagiography, before becoming a monk, Ilya Pechersky was nicknamed "Chobotok", meaning "(small) boot", after an incident in which he fought off enemies with only his boot.
Examined remains. In 1988, Soviet archaeologists exhumed and studied the remains attributed to Ilya Pechersky. They found signs of a spinal disease in youth and marks from numerous wounds, one of them fatal.
