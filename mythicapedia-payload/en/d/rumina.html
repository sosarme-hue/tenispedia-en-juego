Roman goddess of breastfeeding

Rumina

Rumina, also called Rumilia or Rumia, was a goddess of Roman religion who protected breastfeeding mothers and possibly nursing infants themselves. Her protection also extended to the mothers of animals, and she matters because she shows how the Romans honoured the everyday functions of life.

The myth

Rumina has no elaborate myth. She belonged to the indigitamenta, minor and almost abstract deities who presided over one specific function, and she has no stories of her own or a personality like that of later Roman gods.

Her best-known link is topographical. Her temple stood near the Ficus Ruminalis, the fig tree at the foot of the Palatine Hill where, according to tradition, a she-wolf nursed Romulus and Remus. The goddess of milk was thus tied to Rome's legendary origins.

Worship and religion

At her temple, milk was offered instead of wine, the usual libation in other Roman rites. According to the sources, around AD 58 the fig tree began to die, which was read as a bad omen. The cult does not survive today.

Ancient sources: Her name appears in texts of ancient Roman religion describing the indigitamenta, though they offer few narrative descriptions of her.

Trivia

Milk instead of wine. In a context where wine was the most common libation, her temple received milk as an offering, a detail that reflects the nature of the goddess.
A sacred fig tree. The Ficus Ruminalis was linked to Rome's founding myth. Its death, around AD 58, was seen as a negative omen.
