Greek goddess of divine retribution

Nemesis

Nemesis is the Greek goddess who personified divine retribution, punishing hubris and restoring balance when it was disturbed. Linked to the sanctuary at Rhamnous in Attica, she helps explain how the Greeks thought about fortune, pride and guilt.

The myth

According to Hesiod's Theogony, Nemesis was born from Nyx alone, as a scourge for mortals, and she counts among the many children of that goddess, alongside Doom, Strife and Death. Other authors gave her different parents: Pausanias called her a daughter of Oceanus, while Hyginus named Nyx and Erebus. What the sources share is that she is an ancient deity, older than the Olympian order.

Her role was to measure happiness and misery. Those who received too many favours from Fortune drew her attention, and she sent losses so that they would learn humility. Herodotus and Pindar present her this way, and the most cited example is Croesus, who was led into an expedition against Cyrus that brought about his ruin.

The most popular myth links her to Zeus. In the lost epic Cypria, attributed to Stasinus, Zeus chases her over land and sea, and she changes shape again and again to escape. Finally, taking the form of a swan, he overpowers her, and from the egg she lays Helen is born, whose story sets off the Trojan War. Other versions turn her into a goose, and Apollodorus mentions only a single transformation.

Worship and religion

Her cult grew out of fear of divine anger. Her main sanctuary was at Rhamnous in Attica, where she was worshipped as the Goddess of Rhamnous. Her statue, made by Pheidias after the battle of Marathon, was carved from Parian marble that the Persians had brought to raise a monument to a victory they expected. She was named alongside Aidos, Shame, as both shared a respect for the law. At Smyrna there were two forms of Nemesis, closer to Aphrodite than to Artemis, perhaps one for the old city and another for the new city refounded by Alexander. In Rome she was one of the protective deities of the drill-ground, and she seems to have stood for a kind of imperial Fortune. Nothing indicates that her cult survives today.

Legacy

Ovid, in the Metamorphoses, has Nemesis punish Narcissus for his vanity, luring him to a pool where he falls in love with his own reflection. Nonnus, in the Dionysiaca, uses her to avenge Artemis against Aura, a companion who boasted of her slender figure. In art she appears winged, holding a whip, scales, a sword or a chariot pulled by griffins. The poet Mesomedes wrote her a hymn in the early second century AD, calling her the winged balancer of life. Modern scholars such as Stafford and Kosachova read her more as a regulator of justice and moral balance than as a simple avenger.

Ancient sources: Hesiod's Theogony, the Cypria, Herodotus, Pindar, Ovid's Metamorphoses, Nonnus' Dionysiaca and Pausanias, among others.

Trivia

A statue of Persian marble. The statue at Rhamnous was carved from Parian marble that the Persians had brought to raise a monument to their expected victory. In the end, the stone was used to honour the goddess instead.
The punished Aura. In Nonnus' Dionysiaca, Nemesis arranges the punishment of Aura, who mocked Artemis' virginity. Eros makes Dionysus fall in love with her, and he ends up raping her while she is unconscious.
