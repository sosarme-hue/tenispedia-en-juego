Roman god of seasons, change and gardens

Vertumnus

Vertumnus is the Roman god of the seasons, of change and of plant growth, as well as of gardens and fruit trees. His origins are linked to the Etruscan deity Voltumna, and his name is connected with the Latin verb vertere, "to change".

The myth

According to the stories, Vertumnus could take any shape he wished. In Ovid's Metamorphoses he used this power to approach Pomona, the goddess who tended the orchard. Disguised as an old woman, he gained entry to the garden and, through the tale of Iphis and Anaxarete, a warning about the dangers of rejecting a suitor, persuaded her to listen to him. Afterwards he revealed his true form and won her over. For this reason he is shown alongside Pomona, since he protected vegetation and she protected fruit trees.

Tradition also links him with Etruria. The Etruscan Voltumna was said to protect the city of Volsinii, and his sanctuary, Fanum Voltumnae, was the religious centre of the league of twelve Etruscan cities. After Volsinii fell in 264 BC, his cult reached Rome, where a temple was built for him on the Aventine. According to Propertius, his bronze statue, attributed to the legendary Mamurius Veturius, stood in the Vicus Tuscus near the Forum and was dressed according to the seasons.

Worship and religion

His festival, the Vertumnalia, was held on 13 August. He had a statue in the Vicus Tuscus near the Roman Forum and a temple on the Aventine. The sources give no further details of his rites.

Legacy

His name gave rise to words such as diversion, perversion and version, all derived from vertere. In early modern art, the episode with Pomona was hugely popular with painters and sculptors from the 16th to the 18th centuries, who used it as a pretext for veiled eroticism. Camille Claudel carved a marble version in 1905.

Ancient sources: Ovid narrates him in the Metamorphoses and the Fasti, and Propertius mentions him in his Elegies.

Trivia

A god who changes shape. His power to take any appearance is the trait that defines him in the stories, and it reflects his role as a god of change.
A portrait of an emperor. Around 1590 Arcimboldo painted Rudolf II as Vertumnus, showing how the god inspired portraits in early modern Europe.
