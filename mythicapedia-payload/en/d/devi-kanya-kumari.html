Hindu goddess of the virgin maiden at India's southern tip

Devi Kanya Kumari

Devi Kanya Kumari is a manifestation of the Hindu goddess Mahadevi as an adolescent girl, identified in various traditions with Parvati and, among Shaktas, with Bhadrakali. Her temple at the southernmost tip of Tamil Nadu makes her one of the region's most venerated deities.

The myth

According to local legend, the demon Banasura ruled the land of Kanyakumari. After long penance, Brahma granted him a boon: only an adolescent girl could kill him. Armed with it, he grew fearless, drove Indra from his throne and scattered the devas, and the balance of the elements collapsed. To restore order, the goddess manifested at the subcontinent's southern tip as Kumari.

Kumari was devoted to Shiva, who had resolved to marry her. But the sage Narada made a rooster crow before the auspicious hour, and the wedding did not take place. Hurt and enraged, the goddess destroyed everything around her: she threw away the food and broke her bangles, and the scattered grains are said to have become the coloured sands of the coast. She then withdrew into penance. When Banasura tried to approach her without recognising her, she slew him; before dying, he recognised Adi Parashakti and asked forgiveness. Afterwards she resumed her form as Parvati and was reunited with Shiva.

Worship and religion

The Bhagavati Kumari Amman Temple is one of the 52 Shakta pithas, and tradition places the spine of Sati's body here. Its eastern door opens only on certain dates, such as new moons, Navaratri and the month of Kaartikai. She is associated with virginity and penance, and in earlier times many received sannyasa initiation here. Worship continues today, with devotees offering red saris and ghee lamps.

Legacy

The figure is linked to the legend of Kumari Kandam, a lost continent in Tamil tradition. Swami Vivekananda meditated on a rock near her shrine, now known as Vivekananda Paara.

Ancient sources: She is mentioned in the Ramayana, the Mahabharata, Sangam works such as the Manimekalai and Purananuru, and the Narayana Upanishad; the Periplus of the Erythraean Sea (1st century CE) describes her worship at India's southern tip.

Trivia

Coloured sands. The legend attributes the coloured sands of the coast to the food grains the goddess scattered.
Closed eastern door. The temple's eastern door opens only on special days, such as new moons and Navaratri.
