Corinthian hero of Greek myth, tamer of Pegasus

Bellerophon

Bellerophon is a Corinthian hero of Greek mythology, famous for taming the winged horse Pegasus and slaying the Chimera. His story combines exile, false accusation and divine punishment, and it remains one of the great models of the heroic tale in ancient myth.

The myth

According to tradition, his original name was Hipponous ("horse-knower") or Leophontes, but he changed it to Bellerophon after accidentally killing a Corinthian named Belleros. Other versions speak of a brother he killed by mistake, called Deliades, Peiren or Alcimenes. His genealogy varies: Homer makes him the son of Glaucus and grandson of Sisyphus, while Hesiod gives him a divine father, since Poseidon lay with Eurynome, daughter of Nisus, and from that union Bellerophon was born. Poseidon later gave him the horse Pegasus.

Exiled to Tiryns to be purified, he was received as a guest by King Proetus. The king's wife, Stheneboea (or Anteia), fell in love with him and, when he rejected her, falsely accused him of trying to seduce her. Proetus would not kill a guest, so he sent Bellerophon to Lycia with a sealed letter asking his father-in-law, King Iobates, to put him to death. Iobates feasted him for nine days before opening the letter, and then assigned him the task of killing the Chimera, a monster with a goat's head that breathed fire, a lion's body and a serpent's tail, which ravaged the fields.

For this feat, the seer Polyeidos advised him to capture Pegasus. Athena appeared to him in a dream with a golden bridle, and Bellerophon found the horse drinking at the spring of Pirene on the citadel of Corinth. Mounted on Pegasus, he shot arrows at the Chimera, then thrust a spear tipped with a lump of lead into its jaws. The monster's fire melted the lead, which ran down its throat and killed it. Iobates then sent him against the Solymi and the Amazons, and after he defeated the men who tried to ambush him, the king gave him his daughter Philonoe in marriage and half his kingdom.

According to Homer, his end was tragic: he was hated by the gods and wandered alone. In other versions, he tried to ride Pegasus up to Olympus, and Zeus threw him down.

Ancient sources: Homer (Iliad, book VI), where his story is told by his grandson Glaucus, and Hesiod, who presents him as the son of Poseidon.

Trivia

A telling name. His name is usually translated as "slayer of Belleros", after the Corinthian he killed by accident. Some scholars propose other explanations, such as a link to the Greek word for dart.
A naming custom. Among the Jantians, according to the account, people took their mother's name rather than their father's, in memory of the women who calmed Bellerophon's anger.
