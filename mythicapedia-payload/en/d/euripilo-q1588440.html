Thessalian hero of the Trojan War

Eurypylus

Eurypylus is a hero of Greek mythology, a Thessalian leader who fought in the Trojan War at the head of forty ships. His later story, marked by madness and the end of a human sacrifice, ties him to the founding of Patras.

The myth

According to tradition, Eurypylus was the son of Euaemon, though other accounts make him the son of Hyperochus. A former suitor of Helen, he went to Troy with his contingent. In the Iliad he distinguishes himself in battle: he accepted Hector's challenge but was eliminated in the drawing of lots, and he went to help Ajax, whom he protected by killing Apisaon before being wounded in the thigh by one of Paris' arrows. Patroclus tended his wounds in his tent.

After the war he received a chest with an image of Dionysus; when he opened it, he went mad. He consulted the oracle at Delphi, which told him to settle wherever he saw men performing an unusual sacrifice. He came to Aroe, where youths and maidens were being sacrificed to Artemis, and, according to the story, his arrival brought that practice to an end.

Worship and religion

After his death he was buried in the city and honored as a hero at the festival of Dionysus.

Legacy

The asteroid 4501 Eurypylos, a Jupiter trojan, bears his name.

Ancient sources: He appears in Homer's Iliad, and Pausanias describes his story in his account of Achaea.

Trivia

A cursed chest. According to Pausanias, the chest that drove Eurypylus mad had been abandoned by Aeneas when he fled Troy, or left deliberately by Cassandra, who cursed it against whichever Greek opened it.
A sacrifice that ended. The people of Aroe recognized Eurypylus as the leader an oracle had foretold, and from then on they stopped sacrificing people.
