personifications of battlefield slaughter in Greek mythology

Androctasiai

The Androctasiai were abstract figures in Greek mythology who personified the slaughter of men in battle. They appear in Hesiod's Theogony and Homer's Odyssey, and are closely associated with the Keres.

The myth

According to Hesiod's Theogony, the Androktasiai were daughters of Eris, Strife, who bore them by herself along with a multitude of malignant spirits. They were sisters of the Hysminai (Battles), the Machai (Wars), and the Phonoi (Murders), all of similar nature. They went to the battlefields together with those spirits and with the Keres, whom they closely resembled.

Homer lists the same four abstractions in the Odyssey, when Odysseus describes the decorations on Heracles' golden belt. Later, the Shield of Heracles, a poem in the Hesiodic tradition, places Androktasia among the figures wrought on the shield, alongside Pursuit, Retreat, Tumult, and deadly Fate, as they drag a living warrior by the feet.

Ancient sources: Hesiod (Theogony, line 228), Homer (Odyssey), and the Shield of Heracles, in the Hesiodic tradition.
