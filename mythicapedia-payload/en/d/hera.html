Greek goddess of marriage, wife of Zeus

Hera

Hera is the wife of Zeus and queen of the Olympian gods in Greek mythology, goddess of lawful marriage, married women and childbirth. She matters because she embodies marriage as an institution at the heart of the Greek city-state, and because her cult was among the most widespread in the classical world.

The myth

According to tradition, Hera is the daughter of the Titans Cronus and Rhea and the sister of Zeus, whom she married. The two are Cronids, 'children of Cronus', sharing the highest dignity among the gods, which makes Hera the foremost of the goddesses.

The stories portray a strained marriage. Homer shows the couple constantly quarrelling, while Hera responds with jealousy and ruthless revenge against Zeus's infidelities and his illegitimate children, a trait that makes her the model of the wicked stepmother. Hesiod credits her with Hebe, Ares and Ilithyia, and says she conceived Hephaestus alone. Other traditions give her more offspring, such as Eris or Hecate.

In the heroic cycle, Hera protects Jason and the Argonauts, relentlessly pursues Heracles and favours the Achaeans during the Trojan War. She is shown as a solemn figure, enthroned and crowned with the polos, the tall tiara of great goddesses, accompanied by the peacock, the cow, the pomegranate and the cuckoo. Argos Panoptes, the hundred-eyed, served her.

Worship and religion

Archaeological evidence suggests Hera may have been the first deity to whom the Greeks dedicated a roofed temple sanctuary, at Samos around 801 BCE. Her great pan-Hellenic centre was the Heraion of Argos, between Argos and Mycenae, where the Heraea festivals were held. Her most beloved cities, as the texts have her declare, were Argos, Sparta and Mycenae. There were also temples at Olympia, Corinth, Delos and Paestum, and in Boeotia the Daedala festival was celebrated every sixty years. Votive offerings found at Samos, from Armenia, Babylon, Iran, Assyria and Egypt, show the great number of pilgrims who visited her sanctuary.

Legacy

Hera's name survives in words and names such as Heracles, Heraclitus or Herodotus, which some ancient etymologies derive from it. The Romans identified her with Juno, whose name gave rise to words such as June. In Egypt she was linked with Isis, Mut and Hathor, and in the Phoenician world with Astarte and Tanit. Walter Burkert read her as the religious symbol of institutional marriage, and Karl Kerényi as the cosmic archetype of the wife.

Ancient sources: Hesiod, in the Theogony, makes her a daughter of Cronus and Rhea and credits her with Hebe, Ares and Ilithyia; Homer, in the Iliad, shows her quarrelling with Zeus and favouring the Achaeans.
