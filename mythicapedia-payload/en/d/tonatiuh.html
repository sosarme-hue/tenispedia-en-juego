Aztec sun god and patron of warriors

Tonatiuh

Tonatiuh is the sun god of the Mexica (Aztec) people, lord of the daytime sky and patron of warriors. According to the Mexica, he was the Fifth Sun, the star of the era in which they believed they lived.

The myth

According to the Mexica myth, after the fourth sun was destroyed, the gods gathered around a great pyre to choose a new sun. Tecuciztecatl, a proud but cowardly god, and Nanahuatzin, a humble and poor god, offered themselves. Tecuciztecatl flinched and was left with scorch marks, which explain the spots of the jaguar. Nanahuatzin threw himself into the flames, and a spark rose into the sky as the fifth sun, Tonatiuh.

Envious, Tecuciztecatl then leapt into the fire and rose as a second sun, but the lesser gods knocked him down with a rabbit. He became the Moon, where the rabbit can still be seen. Nanahuatzin, now Tonatiuh, demanded human sacrifices to feed himself and keep his journey across the sky.

Worship and religion

Tonatiuh received daily human sacrifices, as it was believed his journey across the sky required feeding on the hearts of victims. During the day he was accompanied by the Cihuateteo, women who had died in childbirth. The Mexica watched the sun closely, and his most famous image is the Sun Stone, where the god appears with a sacrificial knife for a tongue.

Legacy

The Spanish conquistador Pedro de Alvarado was called Tonatiuh by the Mexica, owing to his fair hair and sun-like appearance. According to a legend, Tonatiuh created the cempasúchil flower, now linked to the Day of the Dead, by helping the lovers Xóchitl and Huitzilin.

Ancient sources: In the sixteenth century, Bernardino de Sahagún recorded versions of the creation of the fifth sun, in which the wind god Ehécatl sets the star in motion.

Trivia

A conquistador with a solar name. According to accounts of the conquest, the Mexica called the Spaniard Pedro de Alvarado Tonatiuh, because of his fair hair and a appearance that reminded them of the sun.
The flower of the dead. The legend of Xóchitl and Huitzilin credits Tonatiuh with creating the cempasúchil flower, now tied to the Day of the Dead.
