Hindu goddess of time, death and destruction

Kali

Kali is one of the major goddesses of Hinduism, primarily associated with time, death and destruction, and also with transcendental knowledge. As the shakti of Shiva, she is a central figure in the Shakta and Tantric traditions, and in the twenty-first century she has become a widely recognised symbol of female empowerment.

The myth

According to the Devi Mahatmya, a Sanskrit text usually dated to the sixth century CE, Kali emerges from the forehead of the goddess Durga, or from Kaushiki, during the battle against the demons Chanda and Munda. Her appearance is dark and gaunt, she wears a necklace of human heads and a tiger skin, and she defeats both demons almost at once.

In the most famous episode, the demon Raktabija could multiply himself from every drop of blood that touched the ground. Kali defeats him by drinking his blood before it falls and devouring his duplicates, then dances on the corpses. Other traditions link her with Parvati. In the Linga Purana, Parvati merges into Shiva's body and reappears as Kali to kill the asura Daruka, but her fury grows until Shiva calms her. The Vamana Purana tells that Shiva called Parvati 'the dark blue one', which offended her; she performed austerities to become the golden Gauri, and from her dark sheath came Kaushiki, who in turn created Kali.

Classical images show her standing on the fallen body of Shiva, in cremation grounds or battlefields. Tantric texts give her a deeper metaphysical meaning. The Nirvana-tantra presents her as the ultimate reality, in which Brahma, Vishnu and Shiva appear and vanish like bubbles in the sea.

Worship and religion

Bengal is where she is most widely worshipped as the Divine Mother, and devotional songs and poems praising her motherly nature are popular there. Her chief temple is Kalighat in Calcutta, the city of which she is patron; animal sacrifices were once performed there, and in other places human sacrifices too. A common practice among her devotees is yapa, the repetition of her names, aimed at concentration and union with the goddess. Shakta poets such as Ramprasad Sen (1723-1775) and the Bengali saint Ramakrishna (1836-1886) were devoted to her.

Legacy

Kali appears in nineteenth- and twentieth-century fiction and film. Jules Verne includes her cult in Around the World in Eighty Days (1872), where worshippers of the goddess plan a human sacrifice. Emilio Salgari portrays her as a savage deity in his Sandokan series. In Mexico, the superhero Kaliman, directly linked to her, had adventures from the 1960s to the 1980s. In Help! (1965) by The Beatles, a cult devoted to her chases the band across England. Mick Jagger commissioned John Pasche to design the Rolling Stones logo, a pair of lips and a tongue inspired by the image of Kali, first used on the inner sleeve of Sticky Fingers. Ray Harryhausen brought her to life in The Golden Voyage of Sinbad (1973), and Judy Chicago included her in The Dinner Party.

Ancient sources: The name first appears in the Rig Veda as one of the tongues of Agni, while the Devi Mahatmya gives the most detailed account of her battles against the demons.
