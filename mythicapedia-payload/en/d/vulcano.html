Roman god of fire and the forge

Vulcan

Vulcan is the Roman god of fire, especially the practical fire of the forge, the kiln and metalworking. Early on he was identified with the Greek smith god Hephaestus, and his name gave rise to the word volcano.

The myth

According to tradition, Vulcan is a son of Jupiter and Juno, though ancient writers did not agree on his parentage. Cicero speaks of as many as four gods called Vulcan, and Hyginus claims he was born of Juno without a male parent. The best-known Vulcan ran a forge on the island of Lemnos and made weapons, thrones and marvellous objects for the gods. His works include the armour that Thetis requested for her son Achilles and the adamant knife that Perseus received to defeat Medusa.

His wife was Venus, and the most famous episode tells of her affair with Mars. Vulcan forged an invisible, unbreakable chain and set it around the marriage bed to trap the lovers. He then summoned the gods to see them naked and caught, and the shame made Mars fear repeating the offence. Harmonia was born of their union.

Not all his stories are gentle. When Juno was left suspended in the air on a throne he had built, Vulcan, angry at having been thrown out of heaven, claimed he had no mother. Later, Liber brought him back drunk to the council of the gods, and he agreed to obey. Neptune suggested he court Minerva, but she defended herself with weapons, and from their struggle sprang Erichthonius, serpent from the waist down. Other traditions make him father of Cacus, the giant who fought Hercules, and of Caeculus, founder of Praeneste.

Worship and religion

His oldest shrine, the Volcanal, stood in the Forum at the foot of the Capitoline, and tradition credited its founding to the Sabine king Titus Tatius. Another temple stood on the Campus Martius, and his cult was also important at Ostia. His priest was the flamen Vulcanalis.

His great festival, the Vulcanalia, was held on 23 August, when summer heat threatened crops and granaries. Bonfires were lit into which live fish or small animals were thrown in place of humans. His worship aimed above all at averting harmful fires, so he was invoked with epithets such as Mulciber, the softener of metals, or Quietus and Mitis, meaning calm. After the great fire of AD 64 he was appeased, and Domitian set up a new altar on the Quirinal. The sources do not indicate that his cult survives today.

Legacy

His name gave rise to the word volcano, which today describes mountains of fire and eruptions.

Ancient sources: Cicero, Hyginus in his Fables and Varro, who cites the pontifical annals, mention him among Rome's most ancient deities.

Trivia

The Donkeys. A part of the constellation Cancer is called the Donkeys. According to the story, when Jupiter declared war on the Giants, Liber, Vulcan, satyrs and silenoi answered his call riding donkeys.
Working by candlelight. On the day of the Vulcanalia, a custom required starting work by candlelight, probably to win the god's favour for a beneficial use of fire.
