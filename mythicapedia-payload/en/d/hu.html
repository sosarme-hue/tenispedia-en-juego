Egyptian god of the creative word

Hu

Hu is, in Egyptian mythology, the personification of the creative word, the first utterance with which Atum brought things to life. He was paired with Sia, intelligence, and accompanied the deceased pharaoh and Ra on their journey through the afterlife.

The myth

According to Egyptian tradition, Hu is the creative word personified. When Atum, in the account of the Ennead, brought things into being, he uttered a first word that called them into existence: Hu was the deification of that word. In another version found in the Book of the Dead, Ra cuts off his penis and from his spilled blood two gods arise, Sia and Hu, who embody the mind and the power of the creative word. Some scholars have linked the mutilation to a possible reference to circumcision, although the text does not make this clear.

Over time, Hu became closely associated with Sia, the intelligence that resides in the heart. In Memphite theology, Ptah created the universe by thinking things into being and speaking their names; along these lines, Hu was seen as the tongue of Ptah, while Sia was his thought. Both appear in the retinue of Thoth, with whom they are sometimes identified.

In the Pyramid Texts, Hu accompanies the deceased pharaoh, and in the Coffin Texts he helps Ra on his solar barque: together with Heka, he prevents the serpent enemy from interrupting the nightly voyage through the underworld. Hu thus acknowledges the king's supremacy and lets him cross the waters of the afterlife. In the New Kingdom he is one of the fourteen creative powers of Amun-Ra, and in the Ptolemaic period he was apparently merged with Shu, god of air.

Ancient sources: The Pyramid Texts, the Coffin Texts and the Book of the Dead, where he appears alongside Sia and Heka.

Trivia

Forms of depiction. Hu was shown as a man, as a hawk, or as a man with a ram's head.
Few images. Hu was rarely depicted visually; when he appeared, he usually had a human form.
