Mesopotamian god of justice and protector of Der

Ishtaran

Ishtaran (also Ištaran or Gusilim) was the god of justice and tutelary deity of Der, a Sumerian city-state east of the Tigris near the border with Elam. His association with the serpent made him one of the most recognisable figures on the kudurru boundary stones.

The myth

Sources present him above all as a divine judge. His upright character was so proverbial that Gudea of Lagash and Shulgi of Ur compared themselves to him in their inscriptions to show they were equally just. One god list makes him a son of Anu and Urash, while another links him to Tishpak. His consort was Sarrat-Deri, the Queen of Der, and his messenger was the serpent god Nirah.

According to an ancient account, the lugal Mesilim asked him to settle a land dispute on the frontier between Umma and Lagash. Some scholars suggest that his authority in this case may have come from the border location of his own city.

Some texts link him to the underworld and to the mourned dying gods such as Dumuzi. A late ritual from Assur describes his death in summer, with his corpse beaten and his blood reaching the underworld.

Worship and religion

His cult at Der is documented from the Early Dynastic IIIa period to the middle of the Babylonian period. Gudea recorded a shrine to Ishtaran in the great temple of Ningirsu at Girsu, probably as an act of gratitude. The Temple Hymns describe the entrance to his sanctuary decorated with intertwined serpents. The sources do not document a living cult today.

Ancient sources: The Temple Hymns, god lists such as An = Anum and the Nippur god list, and royal inscriptions such as those of Gudea.

Trivia

A god of serpents. His most frequent symbol on the kudurru stones is the serpent, and some texts suggest he could be shown with a partly serpentine body.
A name with several readings. His name was written with several logograms, some of which also designated other deities, such as Nirah or Inshushinak.
