Athenian princess in Greek mythology

Herse

Herse is an Athenian princess in Greek mythology, daughter of King Cecrops and sister of Aglaurus and Pandrosos. Her story is tied to the birth of Erichthonius and to Cephalus, and her name lent itself to an Athenian festival held in her honour.

The myth

Athena entrusted Herse and her sisters with a closed basket holding the infant Erichthonius, born from the semen of Hephaestus that fell on the earth. She forbade them to open it, but Herse and Aglaurus disobeyed, saw the child intertwined with a serpent, and threw themselves off the Acropolis. In another version, Athena dropped a rock on them, which became Mount Lycabettus.

Ovid tells that they survived: Hermes fell in love with Herse, and Aglaurus, moved by envy, blocked his way to her. The god turned her into black stone and seduced Herse. Cephalus was born of that union and was later carried off by Eos. Sources differ about the offspring of Eos and Cephalus.

Worship and religion

Herse and her sisters were honoured in Athens during the Arrephoria, also called the Hersephoria or Ersephoria in her honour. She was associated with dew, and shrines were built to her on the Acropolis.

Legacy

The Arrephoria ritual is linked to this myth, and scenes of Mercury, Herse and Aglaurus were painted by Rubens, Jordaens and Jean-Baptiste Pierre (1763).

Ancient sources: Ovid recounts her story in his Metamorphoses, and the Bibliotheca and Marcellus of Side also record it.

Trivia

Dew. Her name means dew, which is why she was revered as a protector of agriculture.
Mount Lycabettus. According to one version, the rock Athena threw onto the sisters formed Mount Lycabettus in Athens.
