Greek bandit defeated by Theseus

Periphetes

Periphetes is a bandit from Greek mythology who preyed on travellers on the roads around Epidaurus. He is usually called a son of Hephaestus, although Hyginus also makes him a son of Poseidon, and he matters because he was one of the foes Theseus overcame on his way to Athens.

The myth

According to the tradition, Periphetes lived around Epidaurus and attacked and killed passers-by with a massive bronze club. In one version he had a deformed leg, like his father Hephaestus, which is why he used an iron club. This gave him the nickname « Clubman », from the Greek word for club.

Theseus had to cross the area on his journey from Troezen to Athens, and Periphetes was the first of the deadly foes he met after leaving his hometown. Periphetes tried to attack him, but the hero wrestled him down and killed him. Theseus then took the dead bandit's club and used it from then on, just as Heracles had done with the skin of the Nemean lion.

Legacy

Periphetes is rarely shown in ancient Greek art, especially on Attic vases, unlike many other exploits of Theseus. His struggle appears on the metopes of the Athenian Treasury at Delphi, a detail considered striking given his absence from other Theseus artwork. Some scholars suggest the Athenians may have used his story to draw a parallel between their hero and Heracles, who also carried a club. He has also been proposed as a figure on the metopes of the Temple of Hephaestus in Athens, though that identification remains debated.

Ancient sources: Hyginus mentions him, at times as a son of Neptune (Poseidon) and elsewhere among the sons of Vulcan (Hephaestus).

Trivia

A club inherited. Theseus kept Periphetes' club for himself, a gesture that recalls Heracles and the skin of the Nemean lion.
A rare scene. Periphetes is almost absent from Greek pottery, while the Minotaur and other exploits of Theseus appear often.
