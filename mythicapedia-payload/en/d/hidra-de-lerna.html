many-headed serpent of Greek mythology

Lernaean Hydra

The Lernaean Hydra is a huge serpentine lake monster of Greek mythology, famous for its many heads, which grew back when cut off. Its defeat by Heracles was the second of his Twelve Labours, and it remains one of the best-known monsters of classical myth.

The myth

According to Hesiod, the Hydra was the offspring of Typhon and Echidna, and its breath and blood were so poisonous that even its scent could kill. Other accounts make it born of Styx and Pallas or Pirante. Sources disagree on how many heads it had: Alcaeus fixed the number at nine, with a central one that was immortal, while others mention three, six, fifty or a hundred. Later versions added that every severed head was replaced by two new ones, which made the monster nearly unbeatable.

Eurystheus ordered Heracles to kill it. Hera, hostile to the hero, had raised the beast in the swamp of Lerna, near a spring and an entrance to the Underworld. With the help of his nephew Iolaus, who scorched the stumps of the necks with burning brands, Heracles prevailed. According to Apollodorus, the immortal head was buried under a rock, and Heracles dipped his arrows in the monster's poisonous bile. Hera also sent a crab to distract him; Heracles crushed it under his foot, and the goddess later set it among the stars as the constellation Cancer.

Legacy

In ancient Greece, the proverb "cutting off a hydra" described hopeless, endless tasks. The constellations Hydra and Cancer, which lie close together in the night sky, preserve the legend in astronomy. The monster also appears throughout art, from Greek vases and Roman mosaics to paintings and sculptures of the modern era.

Ancient sources: Hesiod's Theogony gives the oldest surviving narrative, and the earliest images of the monster are bronze fibulae from around 700 BC.

Trivia

One immortal head. According to the sources, the Hydra's only immortal head was buried under a great rock beside the road from Lerna to Eleutes.
A disputed labour. Eurystheus did not count the second labour because Heracles had received help from Iolaus, which some see as an attempt to reconcile ten and twelve labours.
