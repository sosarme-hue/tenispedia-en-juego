King Arthur's mystical table at Camelot

Round Table

The Round Table is the mystical table at Camelot in the Arthurian legend, around which King Arthur and his knights gathered as equals. Its importance lies in giving a collective name and identity to Arthur's knights and in serving as the model for the great chivalric orders of the late Middle Ages.

The myth

The Round Table first appears in writing in 1155, in Wace's Roman de Brut, where Arthur creates it to prevent quarrels among barons who would not accept a lower place than their peers. Layamon later added a Cornish carpenter who built a portable table after a brawl at a Yuletide feast.

Wace credited the idea to the Bretons, though some scholars doubt this. Around 1200, Robert de Boron told how Merlin made the table in imitation of the Last Supper table and Joseph of Arimathea's Grail Table. It had twelve seats and one empty place, the Siege Perilous, reserved for the knight who would achieve the Grail; in the thirteenth-century prose cycles, Galahad fills it. In different versions the table passes from Uther Pendragon to Leodegrance, and Arthur receives it as a wedding gift when he marries Guinevere. In the Post-Vulgate, King Mark destroys it after Arthur's death.

Worship and religion

It was not an object of religious worship, but across medieval Europe festivals called Round Tables imitated Arthur's court with jousts, dances and feasts, and some participants took the names of Arthurian knights. The Winchester Round Table, a painted tabletop hanging in Winchester Castle, dates from about 1275.

Legacy

Today 'round table' also names a meeting of experts who discuss a subject. A Round Table held in England in 1345 led to the founding of an order of 300 knights, which later became the Order of the Garter.

Ancient sources: Wace's Roman de Brut (1155), Robert de Boron's Merlin and Layamon's Brut, as well as Geoffrey of Monmouth's Historia Regum Britanniae, which describes Arthur's court but does not mention the table.

Trivia

A Tudor rose on the table. The Winchester Round Table was repainted in 1522 by order of Henry VIII, who had his portrait placed where King Arthur's would be, with a red Tudor rose at the centre.
Edward I, an Arthur enthusiast. Edward I held a Round Table in 1284 to celebrate his conquest of Wales and organised several more up to 1304.
