sacred mountain of Hindu, Buddhist and Jain cosmology

Mount Meru

Mount Meru is a sacred mountain in Hindu, Buddhist and Jain cosmology, regarded as the centre of the physical and spiritual universe. It matters because these traditions organise the whole cosmos around it, and it has shaped religious architecture across much of Asia.

The myth

According to Hindu texts, Meru rises at the centre of the world, and four mountains surround it: Mandrachala to the east, Suparshva to the west, Kumuda to the north and Kailasha to the south. The Sun, the Moon and the planets revolve around it, and the mountain links the earth with the underworld and with heaven. Shiva dwells at its summit, on Kailasha.

Accounts differ between sources. The Mahabharata describes a golden mountain that gleams in the sunlight, with forests, lakes, healing herbs and precious stones, and says that only a person free of sin could climb it. Kubera lives there beside a golden gate, and the river Mandakini rises from its lakes. In the same epic, the Pandavas try to reach the summit, but only Yudhishthira, accompanied by a dog, succeeds. The Vishnu Purana presents Meru as a pillar of the world at the heart of six ranges arranged like a lotus, with four faces made of crystal, ruby, gold and lapis lazuli. The Bhagavata Purana places Kailasha south of Meru.

In another story, Meru serves to churn the ocean of milk: it rests on the shell of the tortoise Kurma, an incarnation of Vishnu, and gods and demons turn it to obtain the nectar of immortality.

Worship and religion

Meru is represented in temples and places of worship. The idea of a holy mountain surrounded by circles entered Hindu temple architecture, where the spire-like crown is called shikhara, meaning peak in Sanskrit. Angkor Wat in Cambodia is built as a symbolic representation of Meru. In Vajrayana Buddhism, mandala offerings often include it, and it is believed to be the home of the Buddha Chakrasamvara. In Jainism, according to tradition, Indra carries each Tirthankara to its summit shortly after birth to bathe him. These practices remain alive in temples and rituals in India and beyond.

Legacy

The name survives in language: meru means high in Sanskrit, and it is also the name of the central bead of a mala. On Java, Mount Semeru, the island's tallest peak, is linked to a fragment of Meru said to have been moved there, according to the Tantu Pagelaran. Its form inspired the Sumeru Throne, a common feature of Chinese pagodas, and the finial of the Burmese pyatthat, a multi-tiered roof.

Ancient sources: The Mahabharata, the Ramayana and the Puranas (Vishnu, Bhagavata, Matsya, Vayu and Skanda) describe Meru and its surroundings, and the Tantu Pagelaran, a fifteenth-century Old Javanese manuscript, recounts its move to Java.

Trivia

A Javanese volcano. According to the Tantu Pagelaran, the gods moved part of Meru to Java to steady the island, which was drifting on the ocean. That fragment is said to be today's Mount Semeru, the tallest peak on Java.
A prayer bead. On the mala, the prayer beads used in India, the central bead is called meru, sharing the mountain's name.
