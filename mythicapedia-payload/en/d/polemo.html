Greek personification of war

Polemos

Polemos is the Greek personification of war, a figure used in allegory and philosophy rather than a god with a cult of his own. Poets and thinkers gave him a family and a role in their works, and his name stayed in philosophical debate long after antiquity.

The myth

Ancient writers treated him more as a poetic figure than as a deity with a history of his own, so his family changes from one author to another. Pindar makes him the father of Alala, the war-cry, without naming a consort. Quintus of Smyrna, a late antique poet, calls him the brother of the war goddess Enyo, who shares his bellicose nature.

Babrius tells in a fable from the Aesopic tradition how Polemos won Hubris, insolent arrogance, as his wife in a marriage lottery. The two became so attached that they are now inseparable, which is why Babrius warns that wherever Hubris enters a city, war soon follows.

In Aristophanes' comedy Peace, Polemos appears briefly at the end of the prologue. He has buried Peace under stones in a cave, with Tumult (Kudoimos) as his henchman, and he threatens to grind the cities of Greece in a giant mortar. He then sends Tumult to find a pestle strong enough and withdraws to the house of Zeus, never reappearing, though his possible return hangs over the play. In Acharnians he is set against Dicaeopolis, the champion of peace, and is banned from the festivities for burning vineyards and disrupting the songs.

Legacy

His figure entered philosophy as well. Heraclitus called him “both the king and father of all”, with the power to bring things into being and to destroy them. Heidegger read him as the principle of setting apart and of struggle, while Hegel linked the idea to his notion of the unity of opposites. Aristophanes, for his part, turned him into a comic character on the stage.

Ancient sources: Pindar, Quintus of Smyrna, Babrius, Aristophanes, Heraclitus and Virgil, who places him in the underworld.

Trivia

A mortar against Greece. In Aristophanes' Peace, Polemos threatens to grind Greece's cities in a mortar, but the play puns on the cities' names until the threat sounds like a relish for a feast.
King and father of all. Heraclitus calls him “king and father of all”, a phrase that leaves open whether he thinks of a god, an abstraction or war itself.
A marriage by lottery. In Babrius' fable, Polemos wins Hubris in a lottery of marriages, and the couple becomes so close that they are inseparable.
