Persian god of covenants and the sun

Mithra

Mithra, also called Mitra, is an ancient Indo-Iranian and Persian deity of contracts, oaths, light and the Sun. His cult later spread through the Roman Empire, where it became Mithraism, one of the main rivals of the emerging Christianity.

The myth

According to the Avestan tradition, Mithra is an exalted figure in the Iranian ahuric triad, alongside Ahura Mazda and Apam Napat. Guardian of covenants, he cannot be deceived, is infallible and never rests. He protects cattle, his stock epithet being "of Wide Pastures", and watches over the waters so that the pastures receive enough.

The Mihr Yasht, the tenth of the Yashts, describes him as a powerful lord with a thousand ears and ten thousand eyes who drives a chariot across the sky and punishes those who break their oaths. Together with Rashnu and Sraosha, he is one of the three judges of the Chinvat Bridge, which every soul must cross. His slaying of a bull is compared with Indra's killing of the dragon Vritra.

Worship and religion

Before Zoroaster's influence, Mithra was one of the most important gods of the Iranians, invoked in oaths and treaties, which were sealed with a shared meal. The main ceremony was the sacrifice of a bull. Under the Achaemenid dynasty, hymns honouring the ancient gods were composed, including a yasht dedicated to Mithra. In Rome, legionaries practised his mysteries for centuries, and the sources do not indicate that his cult survives today.

Legacy

His name lives on in the near-Earth asteroid 4486 Mithra. The Persian form mihr gave rise to the modern Persian word mehr, and also to the Armenian Mihr and Mher.

Ancient sources: The Rig Veda mentions the Vedic god Mitra, and the Avesta preserves his figure chiefly in the Mihr Yasht (Yasht 10), though he is not named in the Gathas.

Trivia

An asteroid named after him. The near-Earth asteroid 4486 Mithra is named after him.
A name meaning bond. It derives from the root *mi-, "to bind", so the name means "that which causes binding", that is, the covenant and mutual obligation.
