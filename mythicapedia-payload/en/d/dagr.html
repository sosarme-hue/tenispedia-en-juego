Norse god of day

Dagr

Dagr is the divine personification of the day in Norse mythology, son of the god Delling and of Nótt, the goddess of night. He matters because he is distinct from Sól, who embodies the sun as a celestial body, while Dagr represents daylight itself.

The myth

In the Poetic Edda, Dagr appears in the poem Vafþrúðnismál. There Odin, disguised as Gagnráðr, asks the giant Vafþrúðnir where day and night come from, and the giant answers that Delling is the father of day and that Nótt was born of Nörvi. Other passages mention the horse Skinfaxi, whose gleaming mane draws day over mankind, and the valkyrie Sigrdrífa, who after waking from her sleep curse recites a prayer that names the sons of Dagr.

In the Prose Edda, Snorri Sturluson presents him in the Gylfaginning: Dagr is the son of Delling, one of the Æsir, and of Nótt, and is as bright and beautiful as his father. Odin takes Dagr and his mother, gives each of them a horse and a chariot, and places them in the sky to ride around the Earth every twenty-four hours. Dagr receives Skinfaxi, whose mane lights up the sky and the Earth.

The sources do not always agree. In manuscript U, Dagr's mother is Jörð, the Earth, rather than Nótt. Scholar Otto Höfler also proposed that Dagr might be the same figure as the hero Svipdagr, whose name means the day that is soon to dawn.

Legacy

The name survives as an ordinary word. In Old Norse, dagr means day, and from the same root come the modern Danish and Norwegian dag and Icelandic dagur, which still mean day today.

Ancient sources: The Poetic Edda (Vafþrúðnismál, Sigrdrífumál, Hrafnagaldr Óðins) and Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda.

Trivia

Two mothers. According to manuscript U of the Prose Edda, Dagr's mother would be the Earth, Jörð, rather than the night, Nótt. The other manuscripts keep Nótt.
A hero named for dawn. The name Svipdagr means the day that is soon to dawn, and some scholars think he may be the same figure as Dagr.
