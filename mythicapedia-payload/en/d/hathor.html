Egyptian goddess of the sky, love and joy

Hathor

Hathor was one of the major goddesses of ancient Egyptian religion, mistress of the sky, the stars and joy. She embodied the Egyptian view of femininity, combining a protective side with a maternal and festive one, and she was among the most invoked deities in the religious life of the whole country.

The myth

Tradition presents her as mistress of the sky, dwelling with Ra and the other sun deities. She could be the mother of Horus or the wife of Ra, and in that role she was the symbolic mother of the pharaohs, the earthly representatives of both gods. She was also thought of as the cosmic cow who gave birth to the sun god and placed him between her horns, and, like Nut, she gave birth to him again each dawn.

Hathor had two faces. As the Eye of Ra, the feminine counterpart of the solar disk, she took a vengeful character that protected Ra from his enemies. As a beneficent goddess she represented music, dance, joy, love and maternal care. She also helped the souls of the dead cross the boundary into the afterlife. Her many forms are explained by the way the court goddess absorbed many local goddesses, who came to be seen as manifestations of her; texts speak of the Seven Hathors, or of many more, up to 362.

Egyptians most often pictured her as a cow, although her most common image was a woman wearing cow horns and a sun disk. She could also appear as a lioness, a cobra or a sycamore. Her roles as mother, wife and daughter of Ra mirrored the daily cycle of the sun: at sunset the god entered the body of the sky goddess and was reborn at sunrise.

Worship and religion

Hathor had one of the greatest numbers of temples of any goddess, and her most prominent temple was at Dendera in Upper Egypt. She was also worshipped in the temples of her male consorts. Her cult spread to foreign lands such as Nubia and Canaan, linked with incense and gemstones, and some peoples there adopted her worship. In Egypt she was one of the deities most often invoked in private prayers and votive offerings, especially by women hoping to have children. During the New Kingdom, Mut and Isis overshadowed her in royal ideology, yet she remained widely venerated until ancient Egyptian religion died out in the early centuries AD.

Ancient sources: The Pyramid Texts of the late Old Kingdom connect her with the king's apron.

Trivia

The Seven Hathors. Egyptian texts often speak of the goddess's manifestations as the Seven Hathors, and sometimes of many more, up to 362.
The Golden One. She was commonly called the Golden One, in reference to the radiance of the sun, and texts from her temple at Dendera say her rays illuminate the whole earth.
