Greek god of fire and metalworking

Hephaestus

Hephaestus is the Greek god of fire, smithing and metalworking, the blacksmith of the Olympian gods. He belongs to Greek mythology and is the model of the divine craftsman: a lame god whose work places him at the edges of the Olympian world.

The myth

According to Hesiod's Theogony, Hera bore him alone, in retaliation for Zeus having fathered Athena without her. Homer presents him as Hera's son without clarifying whether Zeus is his father, although the Odyssey speaks of his two parents. Because of his lameness, Hera threw him off Olympus; in another version, Zeus flung him down for taking his mother's side. He fell into the sea and was taken in by Thetis and Eurynome, or, after a fall lasting a full day, he landed on Lemnos, where the Sintians cared for him and taught him the craft of the smith.

Back on Olympus he built a palace with a workshop, twenty bellows that obeyed his commands, and automatons, including tripods with golden wheels that moved on their own. He forged much of the gods' equipment: the thrones of the palace, the aegis, the winged helmet and sandals of Hermes, Aphrodite's girdle, Eros's bow and arrows, and Helios's chariot. Some traditions also credit him with creating Pandora. In the Iliad he shows his mastery of fire when he dries the waters of the Scamander to force its river god to retreat.

The best known episode is his return to Olympus. He had made a golden throne that trapped Hera, and the gods begged him to free her. Ares failed, and it was Dionysus who brought him back, drunk, on a mule. In another version, Hera was freed in exchange for Zeus granting him Aphrodite as his wife, the most common union, though the poets mocked him for it. Some sources say he asked for Athena's hand, which ended in an attempted assault that the goddess rejected.

Worship and religion

His cult was limited in Greece, though important in Attica, where he had a temple in the Kerameikos, near the artisans' workshops. The Hephaisteion, also called the Theseion, is the best-preserved Doric temple of the Greco-Roman world, largely because it was later converted into a Christian church. Pausanias describes a statue of Athena nearby linked to Erichthonius, and Cicero mentions a famous statue of Vulcan by Alcamenes, shown with a slight limp. The Chalkeia, held on the last day of Pyanepsion and linked to craftsmen, and the Hephaistia, with its torch race, honoured him. On Lemnos, his main centre of worship, he was revered as lord of the island. At Olympia he shared an altar with the river god Alpheus, and in Sicily and the Lipari islands he was associated with the volcano.

Legacy

The Romans identified him with Vulcan, a name that became tied to the idea of a volcanic fire god in Italy. Modern scholars have read him in different ways: Walter Burkert sees him as the ritual taming of dangerous fire; Jean-Pierre Vernant as a symbol of the tension between the aristocratic polis and manual labour; Marcel Detienne as a distinct form of technical intelligence. Greeks also equated him with Ptah in Egypt and Adranus in Sicily. His hammer, anvil and tongs are his defining attributes, and the pilos, a felt cap, marked the workers who honoured him.

Ancient sources: The Iliad and Odyssey of Homer, Hesiod's Theogony, the Homeric Hymns, Diodorus Siculus, Pausanias, Apollodorus and Cicero.

Trivia

A well-preserved temple. The Hephaisteion in Athens survived almost intact because it was converted into a Christian church, which explains its good condition today.
A god brought back drunk. His return to Olympus was made by Dionysus riding a mule, a scene that appears on Attic and Corinthian painted pottery.
