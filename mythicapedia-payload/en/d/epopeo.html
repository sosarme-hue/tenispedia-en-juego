mythical king of Sicyon

Epopeus

Epopeus is a mythical king of Sicyon in the Peloponnese and the most famous of several Greek figures who share his name. His story, tied to a war with Thebes and the princess Antiope, was told to explain rites for warding off misfortune in his land.

The myth

According to tradition, Epopeus was a son of Poseidon, though ancient writers disagree on his mother: some name Canace, daughter of Aeolus, others the Pleiad Alcyone. Coming from Thessaly, he seized the throne of Sicyon from Lamedon, the successor of his elder brother, King Corax.

He then also took over the regency of Thebes, carrying off Antiope, daughter of the previous regent, and making her his wife. The Thebans marched on Sicyon to free her, but they were defeated. Epopeus died shortly after his wounds, and Lamedon regained the throne. His children included Marathon, eponym of the Attic town, and Oenope.

Other traditions place him on Lesbos as the father of Nictimene, a young woman so beautiful that, after her father's unwelcome desire, she hid in the woods; out of pity, Athena turned her into an owl. Another Epopeus, a sailor, tried to harass Dionysus and was transformed into a dolphin.

Worship and religion

Pausanias saw at Titane, in Sicyonia, an altar and a mound raised to the hero Epopeus, beside the apotropaic 'Gods of Aversion', before whom rites were performed to ward off evils. According to tradition, he also founded a sanctuary of Athena on the Sicyonian acropolis to celebrate his victories, and olive oil flowed before its shrine.

Legacy

Euripides brought him to the stage in his Antiope. His name is linked to epops, the hoopoe, and Callimachus preserved a tradition that at Sicyon the bird of good omen was the hoopoe rather than the ravens.

Ancient sources: Pausanias (Description of Greece), Euripides (Antiope) and Callimachus (Aitia).

Trivia

A bird's name. His Greek name, Epopeus, is linked to epops, the hoopoe, a bird the ancients associated with watchfulness.
Sicyon's hoopoe. A fragment of Callimachus asks why, at Sicyon, the bird of good omen is the hoopoe and not the splendid ravens.
