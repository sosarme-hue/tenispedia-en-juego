Völva

The völva was a wise woman, prophetess and priestess among the Germanic and Norse peoples, said to foretell the future and practise sorcery. She is a recurring figure in Norse mythology and helped shape the European image of the witch.

The myth

According to tradition, völvas held knowledge even the gods sought. The best-known case appears in the Völuspá, where Odin himself consults a seeress about the fate of the gods. Others appear by name, such as Heidi in the Völuspá, Gróa in the Svipdagsmál, and Þorbjörg Lítilvölva in the Saga of Erik the Red.

The name völva means staff bearer, from the wand they carried. They practised seiðr, a form of magic usually linked to women, though the sources rarely separate völvas from seiðkonas, since the terms were used interchangeably. In Lokasenna, Loki accuses Odin of having travelled the world in the guise of a vitka, the feminine form of vitki, meaning sorcerer.

Worship and religion

The sources do not describe temples dedicated to them, but they do describe rituals. Accounts place them on raised platforms during their séances. They are associated with the goddess Freyja, and Olga of Kiev, a Viking princess later made a saint, is thought to have served as a priestess of Freyja among the Rus' elite before their conversion to Christianity. Some Scandinavian graves containing wands and seeds with hallucinogenic properties have been linked to such women. No living cult of them survives as such.

Legacy

Their memory survives in the English word witch, the modern form of wicce. Beliefs about their powers helped shape the European concept of the witch, although their practitioners were marginalised after Christianisation. Today they appear in popular culture and in Heathenry, a modern pagan movement that revives their role.

Ancient sources: The Völuspá, Svipdagsmál, Lokasenna and the Saga of Erik the Red, along with Jordanes' Getica and the medieval Norse texts that record their names.

Trivia

Divination bones. The term vala is linked to the knucklebones used in divination.
A name linked to Hel. A reconstructed Proto-Germanic form, *xalja-rūnō, combines the name of Hel, abode of the dead, with a word meaning mystery.
