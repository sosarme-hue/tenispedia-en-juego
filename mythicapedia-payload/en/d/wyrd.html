Anglo-Saxon concept of personal fate

Wyrd

Wyrd is the Anglo-Saxon concept of personal fate or destiny, and the ancestor of the modern English word weird. In Old Norse its cognate, urðr, is also the name of one of the Norns, which is why some scholars have seen wyrd as a goddess of fate.

The myth

According to Anglo-Saxon texts, wyrd is an inexorable force: in The Wanderer it snatches earls away from life's joys, and in Beowulf it is summed up in the idea that fate always goes as it must.

In Norse mythology, the Norns weave fate or ørlǫg; according to the Voluspa, they set up the laws and decide the lives of the children of time. Some scholars regard wyrd as a pre-Christian goddess, while others deny any pagan meaning for it in Old English literature.

Legacy

The adjective weird descends from wyrd and moved from meaning fate to meaning strange. Shakespeare brought it to the stage as the Weird Sisters in Macbeth. A mountain on Venus, Wyrd Mons, bears its name, and Frank Herbert's Dune turned it into the Weirding Way.

Ancient sources: The Voluspa and Lokasenna in the Eddas, and the Old English poems The Wanderer and Beowulf.

Trivia

From fate to strangeness. The English word weird comes from wyrd, but its modern meaning of 'odd' was first recorded in 1815.
Witches of fate. Many editions of Macbeth add a footnote linking the Weird Sisters to the Old English word wyrd.
