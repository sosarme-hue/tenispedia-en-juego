Greek culture hero, patron of herders and beekeepers

Aristaeus

Aristaeus is a culture hero of Greek mythology, son of Apollo and the nymph Cyrene, credited with teaching humanity beekeeping, cheesemaking and olive cultivation. His cult spread through Boeotia, Ceos and the Aegean islands, and his figure reflects the close bond between rural life and religion.

The myth

According to Pindar, Apollo carried off Cyrene, a young woman who was herding near the river Peneus, and took her to Libya, where she became the founder of the city that bears her name. There Aristaeus was born, and Hermes brought him to Gaia to be made immortal on nectar and ambrosia. The nymphs who raised him gave him several names: Nomius, Agreus and Aristaeus, the last meaning roughly the best, more an epithet than a true name. Bacchylides records other local traditions that make him a son of Caristus, of Chiron, of Gaia and Uranus, or of Cyrene herself.

From the nymphs he learned to curdle milk, build hives and cultivate olive trees, and he taught these skills to humankind, who honoured him with cults like those of the gods. As an adult he married Autonoe, daughter of Cadmus, in Boeotia, and became the father of Actaeon, who was ruined when Artemis caught him, and of Macris, who nursed the young Dionysus. According to Pherecydes, he also fathered Hecate.

A Delphic prophecy sent him to Ceos, where the islanders were suffering a plague under the influence of Sirius. Aristaeus offered sacrifices to Zeus and to Sirius to atone for the killing of Icarius, whose murderers had found refuge on the island. Zeus then sent the Etesian winds for forty days and the plague ended. The Ceans kept sacrificing to the Dog-Star whenever it rose, just to be safe.

In Virgil, Aristaeus loses his bees after Eurydice dies fleeing him and being bitten by a snake. Advised by his mother and by the seer Proteus, he sacrificed four bulls and four cows and left the carcasses where they lay. Returning days later, he found new swarms inside them. Finally, according to tradition, he travelled to Thrace, was initiated into the mysteries of Dionysus and, after living near Mount Haemus, vanished and received immortal honours.

Worship and religion

He was worshipped in Thessaly, Ceos, Boeotia and on islands of the Aegean, Ionian and Adriatic seas. On Ceos, sacrifices to Sirius were repeated every year even after the plague had passed, and an altar to Zeus Ikmaios was raised. The sources do not say whether his cult survives today.

Legacy

Sue Monk Kidd's 2002 novel The Secret Life of Bees gives a variation of his bee episode. As patron of shepherds, olive growers and beekeepers, his figure remains tied to rural crafts.

Ancient sources: Pindar (Pythian 9), Apollonius of Rhodes (Argonautica), Diodorus Siculus, Bacchylides, Pherecydes and Virgil (Georgics IV).

Trivia

Bees born from a dead animal. According to Virgil, Aristaeus found new swarms inside the carcasses of the animals he had sacrificed. This ancient belief is known as bugonia.
A name that is an epithet. Aristaeus means the best and worked more as an honorific title than as a proper name, which explains why several different names were attributed to him.
