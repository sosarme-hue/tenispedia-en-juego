Greek goddess of the dawn

Eos

Eos is the Greek goddess of the dawn, the personification of daybreak, who opens the gates of heaven so that her brother Helios can drive his chariot across the sky. She matters because she preserves a name and a character inherited from an ancient dawn goddess of the Indo-European peoples.

The myth

According to Hesiod, Eos is a daughter of the Titans Hyperion and Theia, and sister of Helios and Selene. Other traditions make her a daughter of the Titan Pallas, and some authors consider her a daughter of Night. Each morning she rises from her home at the edge of the river Oceanus, opens the gates of heaven with her rosy fingers and goes ahead of her brother's chariot. Her most common Homeric epithets are rhododactylos, rosy-fingered, and Erigenia, early-born.

Eos was also a notoriously passionate goddess. Her lovers included Ares, Astraeus, Cephalus, Clytus, Ganymede, Orion and Tithonus. With Astraeus she had, according to Hesiod, the winds and the stars. With the Trojan prince Tithonus she asked Zeus for immortality but forgot to ask for eternal youth, so he aged forever until she turned him into a cicada. With Tithonus she also had Memnon, who fought for the Trojans and died. Since then, the tradition says, Eos mourns her son every morning, and her tears are the dew.

In the Odyssey, Orion was carried off by Eos until Artemis struck him down on Ortygia. Cephalus, an Athenian hunter married to Procris, was also taken by the goddess; when he began to long for his wife, Eos returned him to her and cursed him. The sources do not agree on the children he had with her, and Ovid tells how a jealous Procris spied on him.

Worship and religion

Eos had little independent cult in classical Greece, despite her antiquity, and her religious presence was more poetic than ritual. In Rome, Aurora was associated with Matuta, whose temple stood in the Forum Boarium, where the Matralia was celebrated on 11 June, a festival for women in their first marriage. Among the Etruscans, the goddess of dawn was Thesan. Today no living cult of Eos survives.

Legacy

Her name survives in the Roman Aurora and in the Vedic goddess Ushas, with whom she shares an origin. The image of Eos holding the dead Memnon on her knees has been considered an inspiration for the Christian Pietà. Homer and Sappho describe her with golden arms and golden sandals, and on Attic vases she appears crowned and winged.

Ancient sources: Hesiod in the Theogony, Homer in the Iliad and the Odyssey, the Homeric Hymn to Helios, Sappho, Ovid and Apollodorus.

Trivia

A cicada as punishment. In the tradition, Eos turned Tithonus into a cicada after he kept aging without being able to die. The Greek word for cicada was tettix.
Dew as tears. Each morning Eos mourns her son Memnon, and her tears fall on the earth as dew.
