Roman naiad, mother of the Lares

Lara

Lara, also called Larunda, is a naiad of Roman mythology, daughter of the river Almo and, in the best-known story, mother of the Lares, guardians of crossroads and of the city of Rome. Her story also helps explain the cult of Tacita, the silent goddess.

The myth

In Ovid's Fasti, Jupiter fell in love with the nymph Juturna, who threw herself into the Tiber to escape him. He asked the nymphs of Latium to watch the riverbanks, and all of them obeyed except Lara. Despite her father Almo's repeated advice to keep quiet, Lara warned Juturna and then told Juno about her husband's affair. Furious, Jupiter tore out Lara's tongue and sent Mercury to take her to the underworld. On the way, Mercury raped her, and Lara, unable to speak, could only plead with her face. From this union the twins known as the Lares were born.

Other sources offer related figures. Plutarch speaks of Tacita as a Camena whom Numa taught to honour, out of respect for Pythagorean silence, and Varro considers her a Sabine deity adopted by the Romans. Lactantius identified her with Muta, "the mute one".

Worship and religion

Ovid describes an informal, secretive women's rite held during the Feralia in honour of Tacita. An old woman holding seven black beans in her mouth led the ceremony, which resembled the exorcism of wandering spirits at the Lemuria and ended with a fish-head sewn shut to bind hostile tongues to silence. Lara was sometimes associated with Acca Larentia, whose feast was the Larentalia.

Ancient sources: Ovid, Fasti (Book II), the most complete account; Plutarch and Varro also mention her, and Lactantius identifies her with Muta.

Trivia

A nymph without a tongue. According to Ovid, Jupiter tore out her tongue as punishment for her indiscretion, which explains her name as "the mute one" and her link with silence.
A name meaning chatterbox. Her name Lara is linked to her original name Lala, imitating her excessive chatter, which contrasts with her later muteness.
