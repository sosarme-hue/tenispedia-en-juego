Aetolian hero, father of Diomedes, one of the Seven against Thebes

Tydeus

Tydeus is an Aetolian hero of Greek myth, son of Oeneus, king of Calydon, and father of Diomedes, one of the leaders in the Trojan War. He is best known for his part in the attack of the Seven against Thebes, a story that inspired tragedies by Aeschylus and Euripides.

The myth

According to tradition, Tydeus was the son of Oeneus and either Periboea or Gorge, his daughter, depending on the source. After killing a relative (accounts differ on who the victim was), he was banished and reached Argos, where King Adrastus gave him his daughter Deipyle in marriage. A quarrel between Tydeus and Polynices over lodging in the palace, read by an oracle as a boar and a lion, ended in a double wedding and Adrastus's promise to help them regain their kingdoms.

On the expedition against Thebes, Tydeus was sent as envoy to demand that Eteocles give up the throne. When refused, he challenged the Theban champions and defeated every one, and after an ambush by fifty men he killed all but one. In the final battle Melanippus wounded him mortally. Athena meant to make him immortal, but Amphiaraus brought her Melanippus' severed head, and Tydeus, in a frenzy, ate its brains. The goddess, appalled, withdrew her favour, and the hero died.

Legacy

Tydeus appears in the Iliad, where Agamemnon reminds Diomedes of his father's deeds. Aeschylus and Euripides staged him in Seven Against Thebes and The Phoenician Women. Dante compares him in the Divine Comedy to Ugolino gnawing the head of Ruggieri. One of the Riace bronzes has been proposed as a likeness of him.

Ancient sources: He appears in Homer's Iliad, in the tragedies of Aeschylus and Euripides, and in Hesiod and Mimnermus, among others.

Trivia

A gruesome end. In some versions, Tydeus ate the brains of his enemy Melanippus, an act that cost him Athena's favour.
Son-in-law of a king. Adrastus, king of Argos, took him in and married him to his daughter Deipyle after a night-time quarrel with Polynices.
