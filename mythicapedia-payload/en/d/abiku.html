Yoruba spirit of children destined to die young

Abiku

In Yoruba tradition of Nigeria, the abiku is the spirit of a child who dies young and, according to belief, is reborn again and again to the same mother. The name means "one who is born to die", and belief in these spirits remains strong in family life.

The myth

According to tradition, the abiku belongs to an endless cycle of birth and death. Usually a mother gives birth to several children who die young, although some spirits wait for important life events, such as a wedding, before dying. Once the mother reaches menopause, the spirit may move to a new mother and begin again.

The stories fall into three branches. In the first, the abiku shares a newborn's body and steals part of its spiritual nourishment until the child withers. In the other two, the spirit is a thief, alone or in a gang, who takes a share of the family's possessions. Some versions say that gangs of abiku meet at night while the mother sleeps, turning into adults to attend. The cycle ends only if the object tied to their oath in the spirit world, usually buried under a tree, is found. Spirits are believed to live in trees such as the iroko, baobab and silk-cotton tree.

Worship and religion

Parents of a child thought to be abiku visit local doctors, medicine men or fetish priests (babalawo) to try to break the cycle. Rituals include scarring the body of a dead child or refusing it a proper burial, to mark the spirit and separate it from others. On living children, reported practices include putting peppers into fresh cuts, delaying circumcision, and using bells and iron bangles to drive away spirits. Names such as Kokumo or Dúrójaiyé are chosen to encourage the child to stay. The belief remains alive in parts of Nigeria.

Legacy

The abiku appears in Wole Soyinka's poem 'Abiku' and in Ben Okri's novel The Famished Road, where it drives the plot. It also inspired Debo Kotun's political satire Abiku and Gerald Brom's illustrated novel The Plucker.

Trivia

Widespread belief. A 2004 study of 1695 women from the Nigerian states of Ondo and Ekiti found that more than half believed in abiku children.
References in divination. The abiku appears in at least forty-six recorded Ifá divination verses, and possibly as many as 256.
