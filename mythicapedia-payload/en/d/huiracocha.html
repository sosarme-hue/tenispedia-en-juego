Viracocha

Viracocha is the primordial creator deity of the pre-Inca and Inca religion in the Andes, and the supreme god of the Inca pantheon. He is credited with creating the universe, the sun, the moon and humankind, and is associated with Lake Titicaca and the origin of civilization.

The myth

According to a myth recorded by Juan de Betanzos, Viracocha rose from Lake Titicaca, or sometimes from the cave of Paqariq Tampu, in a time of darkness to bring light. He made the sun, the moon and the stars, and then created humans. His first attempt was brainless giants who displeased him, so he destroyed them with a flood and made people from smaller stones.

Once created, humans spread across the world. Viracocha wandered the earth disguised as a beggar, teaching the basics of civilization and performing miracles. Many ignored his teachings and turned to warfare or crime, and the god wept at their plight. He eventually walked away across the Pacific Ocean and never returned, though it was believed he would reappear in times of trouble.

The traditions describe a divine family. In some versions he had a son, Inti the sun, and Mama Killa, while in others his sons Imaymana and Tocapo Viracocha are linked to the four directions of the empire. Another tradition gives him a wife, Mama Cocha. Pedro Sarmiento de Gamboa describes him as a man of medium height, white, dressed in a white robe, carrying a staff and a book.

Worship and religion

For the Inca, the Viracocha cult was more important than the sun cult, and his figure is central to the Gateway of the Sun at Tiwanaku. He was depicted with the sun as a crown, rays in his hands and tears falling from his eyes as rain. His worship was closely tied to the nobility.

Legacy

His name was used in colonial Spanish to refer to the Spaniards, and Christian missionaries identified him with the Christian God the Father. Thor Heyerdahl's raft Kon-Tiki took its name from one of his epithets.

Ancient sources: The colonial chronicles of Juan de Betanzos, Pedro Sarmiento de Gamboa and Pedro Cieza de León, along with the Quechua Huarochirí manuscript, record his myths and epithets.

Trivia

Origin of the name. His name has a disputed etymology. Sarmiento de Gamboa linked it to sea foam, while others connect it to the lake and the origin of all things.
A bearded god. Some Spanish chroniclers described him as a white, bearded god, an image many scholars consider a post-conquest development.
