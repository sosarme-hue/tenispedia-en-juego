Japanese god of grains and the failed envoy

Ame no Wakahiko

Ame no Wakahiko is a god of grains in Japanese mythology, son of Amatsukunitama and one of the Amatsukami, the heavenly deities. His story, which ends with a returning arrow, gave rise to a Japanese saying and to medieval tales that explain the Tanabata festival.

The myth

According to tradition, the heavenly gods sent Ame no Wakahiko down to rule the earth, waiting for Ame no Hohi to report back, but three years passed without word. In many versions he was given a bow for the mission.

In some accounts he fell in love with an earthly woman and stayed. After eight years without news, the gods sent a bird named Nakime to find out what was happening. Following a wise woman's advice, Ame no Wakahiko shot the bird with his bow. The arrow pierced it and flew up to heaven, where Takamimusubi threw it back down, striking him as he lay in bed. Other versions credit Amaterasu with the return shot. His death gave rise to a Japanese saying about the returning arrow, and scholars also read it as a possible reflection of the Yamato state's efforts to extend its authority over neighbouring lands.

His parents built a mourning hut, or moya, for him. Ajisukitakahikone came to pay respects, but his strong resemblance to the dead god led people to mistake him for a revived Ame no Wakahiko. Offended, he destroyed the hut and kicked it, and it landed in Mino province, becoming a mountain called Moyama.

In the Muromachi-period Otogi-zōshi, another tale has him take the form of a serpent who asks for the youngest daughter of a wealthy man. She cuts off the serpent's head, and a handsome dragon prince emerges from the skin. When his wife opens the chest where he has hidden the skin, he vanishes, and she must travel to heaven by climbing a gourd vine. There she completes four tasks set by her oni father-in-law, and the couple may meet only once a year, during Tanabata.

Worship and religion

Ame no Wakahiko is enshrined at Abiko Shrine in Aishō, Shiga Prefecture, and at Iwakura Shrine in Shinshiro, Aichi Prefecture.

Legacy

His story supplies the origin of a Japanese saying about the returning arrow and serves as an etiological tale for the Tanabata festival in medieval literature.

Ancient sources: His story appears in the Muromachi-period Otogi-zōshi, not in the earliest ancient chronicles.

Trivia

An arrow that returns. His death gave rise to a Japanese saying about an arrow that comes back to the one who shot it.
A Tanabata tale. In the Otogi-zōshi his story explains why the lovers meet only once a year, during the Tanabata festival.
