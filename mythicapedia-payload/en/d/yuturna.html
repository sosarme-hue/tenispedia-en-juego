Roman goddess of fountains and springs

Juturna

Juturna, also spelled Yuturna, was a Roman goddess of fountains, wells and springs, linked to healing waters in Latium. She is best known from Virgil's Aeneid, where she fights on the side of her brother Turnus against Aeneas.

The myth

According to tradition, Juturna was an ancient Latin deity connected with a sacred well at Lavinium. Arnobius considered her the daughter of Vulturnus, wife of Janus and mother of Fontus, while Ovid made her a naiad, sister of Lara, whom he described as the daughter of the river Almo. Virgil simply calls her the sister of Turnus, whom he makes the son of Daunus and Venilia.

In the Aeneid, Juturna supports Turnus against Aeneas: she gives him back his sword when he drops it in battle and draws him away from the fighting when it seems he will die. In the end she cannot save him from his fate and withdraws into her waters in mourning. Juno later grants her a catasterism that links her with the Dioscuri.

Ovid tells how Jupiter fell in love with her, but Juturna kept fleeing to the hazel groves or throwing herself into the waters. The god gathered the nymphs of the Tiber and asked for their help in holding her. Lara betrayed the secret to Juturna and Juno, and Jupiter punished her by striking her mute. Some scholars suggest that, to compensate for taking her virginity, Jupiter made her a water nymph and granted her immortality.

Worship and religion

Juturna received sacrifices from both the state and private individuals on January 11, the date of her festival, the Yuturnalia. In Rome she had a temple in the sacred area of Largo di Torre Argentina and a chapel dedicated by Lutatius Catulus in the Campus Martius. Her fountain in the Roman Forum, beside a temple of Vesta, was tended by the fontani, the men who maintained the city's fountains and aqueducts. Waters from her sanctuary at Lavinium were used in almost all sacrifices.

Legacy

Lake Juturna in Antarctica is named after her. The debut album of the American rock band Circa Survive is also titled Juturna.

Ancient sources: Virgil's Aeneid and Ovid's Metamorphoses are the main ancient sources, alongside Arnobius and Frontinus.

Trivia

A name tied to helping. Her Latin name, Iuturna, may come from a verb meaning to help, iuvaret, which fits her role as a helper of mortals.
The Dioscuri at her lake. According to tradition, Castor and Pollux watered their horses at Juturna's fountain after announcing the Roman victory at Lake Regillus.
