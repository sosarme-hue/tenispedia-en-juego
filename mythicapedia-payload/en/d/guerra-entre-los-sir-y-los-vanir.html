Æsir–Vanir War

In Norse mythology, the Æsir–Vanir War was the first war in the world, fought between two groups of deities and ending with their union into a single pantheon. It matters because it explains how two distinct divine families came to coexist and how practices such as seiðr entered Norse culture.

The myth

According to tradition, the trouble began with Gullveig, a Vanir goddess who entered the halls of the Æsir and stirred discord. The Æsir stabbed her with spears and burned her in one of Odin's halls, but she was reborn three times. Thereafter she was called Heiðr, "the Bright One", and described as a wise völva who practised seiðr, a magic linked to trance.

The Vanir demanded redress for this violence and claimed the same status and privileges as the Æsir. At the assembly of the gods, the question was whether the Æsir should pay a fine or whether all the gods should receive equal tribute. The Æsir refused, and the dispute became an all-out war, in which Odin hurled a spear over the crowd and the Vanir trampled the plain. The Völuspá version is fragmentary and its reading is debated.

The Prose Edda gives a different ending: both sides sealed a truce by spitting into a vat. From that saliva the gods made Kvasir, the wisest of beings, who was later killed, and his blood mixed with honey became the mead of poetry. In Snorri Sturluson's Heimskringla, by contrast, the war is told as a clash between real peoples, with an exchange of hostages: Njörðr and Freyr went to Asgard, and Mímir to Vanaheim. After Mímir's death, Odin kept his head, which continued to give him counsel, while Njörðr and Freyr became sacred leaders among the Æsir, and Freyja brought seiðr among them.

Legacy

The figure of Gullveig/Heiðr is often equated with Freyja, and some scholars read the episode as an allegory of fertility cults entering Norse religion. Dumézil and others have compared it with the Roman Rape of the Sabine Women and the Hindu myth of the devas and asuras, as a possible reflection of an ancient Indo-European tripartite social order.

Ancient sources: The Völuspá in the Poetic Edda, the Skáldskaparmál in the Prose Edda, and Snorri Sturluson's Heimskringla.
