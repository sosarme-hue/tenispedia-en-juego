smallpox god of Yoruba and Gbe traditions and of Candomblé

Sakpata

Sakpata is a deity linked to smallpox, the earth and healing, worshipped by the Yoruba and by Gbe-speaking peoples such as the Fon and Ewe, and also in Afro-Brazilian Candomblé. Its name changes across regions; in Yoruba tradition the same figure is better known as Sopona or Babalú-Ayé.

The myth

According to the sources, Sakpata is the son of Yemoja in Yoruba tradition. He may be seen as a vengeful aspect of the deity Obaluaye: disease was understood as a punishment sent to humans when the god felt neglected or offended.

Among the Fon and Ewe of West Africa he is regarded as the deity of the earth, wellbeing and smallpox, able both to heal and to inflict disease and madness. Oral history recalls that the epidemic decimated their ancestors during their stay at Adja Tado, until a Yoruba man named Torgbui Anyi cured them.

Worship and religion

Worship was tightly controlled by priests who looked after his shrines, and people believed angry priests could cause smallpox outbreaks. His true name is considered secret and taboo, so he is addressed through euphemisms. British colonial authorities banned his worship in 1907, but the faithful continued to honour him. In Candomblé he is dressed in straw and a straw broom is used in his rituals.

Legacy

His cult travelled with the Yoruba to the Americas during the slave trade and remains present in Afro-Brazilian and Caribbean Orisha religions, where he is also associated with contagious diseases, including HIV/AIDS today.
