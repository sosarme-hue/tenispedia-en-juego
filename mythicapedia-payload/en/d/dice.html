Greek goddess of justice

Dike

Dike (Greek Dikē) is, in Greek mythology, the personification of justice. According to Hesiod she was the daughter of Zeus and Themis, and while her mother embodied divine justice, she watched over justice among humans.

The myth

According to Hesiod's Theogony, Dike was born to Zeus and Themis and was a sister of the Horae Eunomia and Eirene. While Themis embodied the justice of the gods, Dike watched over human conduct and, when a judge perverted the law, she went before the throne of Zeus to lament. For this reason she was called his paredros, a companion or attendant. In the tragedies of Sophocles and Euripides she punishes every injustice without mercy, and in Parmenides' poem she appears beside her mother with the epithet 'she of many punishments'.

Other authors give a different origin. Roman poets, who called her Astraea, made her the daughter of Astraeus and Eos, and said she lived in the Golden Age, when men knew neither war nor disease. Aratus tells how, as greed spread, Dike first withdrew to the mountains and, when the Bronze Age came, rose to the sky and became the constellation Virgo. Her scales became the nearby Libra. Ovidio, Lucan, Statius, Juvenal and Seneca all recount this departure from the earth.

The Orphics made her born of Law (Nomos) and Piety (Eusebia). Another, more moral tale says Dike avenged Meletus, whose suitor Timagoras mocked his death; driven by a force he could not resist, Timagoras threw himself from the same rock.

Worship and religion

The sources say little about her cult. One account claims that the Persian king Darius offered a sacrifice in her name, though this is not confirmed. The Orphic texts include a hymn to her, and another to Dikaiosyne, justice understood as practice and feeling rather than as a goddess.

Legacy

Her image was fixed in art: the Chest of Cypselus, preserved at Olympia, showed her throttling Adikia, injustice, with one hand and beating her with a stick in the other. Her figure later inspired allegorical personifications of justice in rhetoric and early Christian literature. The name Astraea survives in the constellation Virgo, and Libra recalls her scales.

Ancient sources: Hesiod (Theogony), Aratus (Phaenomena), Ovid, Lucan, Statius, Juvenal and Seneca, as well as the Orphic hymns and the tragedies of Sophocles and Euripides.

Trivia

A constellation with scales. According to Aratus, the scales Dike carried became the constellation Libra, placed beside Virgo.
The snake on Malta. In the Acts of the Apostles, the islanders of Malta, seeing Paul bitten by a snake, concluded that Justice would not let him live. Most biblical scholars read this as a reference to Dike.
