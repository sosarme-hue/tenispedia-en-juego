Mesopotamian and Syrian weather god

Wer

Wer was a weather god worshipped in parts of Mesopotamia and ancient Syria, probably one of the main deities of the northern regions. He is best known as the lord of the cedar mountain and master of the monster Humbaba in an early version of the Epic of Gilgamesh.

The myth

In an Old Babylonian version of the Epic of Gilgamesh preserved on the so-called Yale tablet, Enkidu describes the cedar mountain, which Gilgamesh wants to enter, as under the control of the god Wer, a mighty being who never sleeps. According to this account, Wer appointed Humbaba as the mountain's guardian and gave him seven terrors. In other versions of the epic, Humbaba's master is Enlil.

Andrew R. George suggests that the mountain belonged to Wer, who appointed Humbaba as guardian and his second in command, though the decision still had to be approved by Enlil. His symbol was a lance, and a single Old Babylonian text even attests the deified lance as an object of worship.

Worship and religion

Worship of Wer is chiefly attested in the middle Euphrates area, northern Babylonia, the Diyala region and Assyria. He was honoured in Assur, where he appeared in the temple of Ištar-Aššurītu, and in Nineveh, in the temple of Ashur. Terqa had a house of worship dedicated to him, and his cult declined in the second half of the second millennium BCE. He is also mentioned on the Stele of Zakkur alongside Aramean deities such as Iluwer.

Legacy

His name appears in many theophoric names, such as Itūr-Mēr, Tar’am-Mēr and Ubār-Wēr. Scholars debate whether he is the same god as the tutelary deity of Mari, and the origin of his name remains unresolved.

Ancient sources: The Old Babylonian Epic of Gilgamesh (Yale tablet), the Antakya stele of Adad-nirari III, and texts from Mari, Assur and Kanesh.
