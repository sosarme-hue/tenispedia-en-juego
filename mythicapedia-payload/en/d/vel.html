sacred spear of Murugan in Hindu tradition

Vel

The vel is the divine spear associated with Murugan (Kartikeya), the Hindu god of war. For devotees it embodies the power of the goddess Parvati, and in Tamil tradition it also stands for the wisdom that dispels ignorance.

The myth

According to Shaiva tradition, Parvati gave the vel to her son Kartikeya, also called Murugan, as an embodiment of her shakti, so that he could defeat the asura Surapadman. The Skanda Purana tells how, in the war between them, Kartikeya used the vel to defeat all of Surapadman's forces.

When total defeat was near, Surapadman turned himself into a huge mango tree to hide. Murugan saw through the trick and hurled his spear, splitting the tree in two: one half became a rooster, the other a peacock. The peacock became his vahana, or mount, and the rooster the emblem on his battle flag.

Worship and religion

The vel is an object of worship in temples dedicated to Kartikeya. The annual Thaipusam festival marks the day he received the spear from his mother. During the procession to the temple, some devotees pierce their skin, tongue or cheeks with vel skewers. In Sri Lanka, Tamil Hindus observe the Adi-vel in July or August, especially in Katharagama and Colombo.

Legacy

In ancient Tamilakam the spear, or vēl, was a prominent weapon in Sangam literature, where it is linked to kings and chieftains. Jaroslav Vacek notes descriptions such as “long spear,” “sharp spear” and “victorious spear.” As a weapon of war, the vel became associated with Murugan, a deity much loved by the Tamils. A coin of the Yaudheyas, dated to around 200 BCE, already shows Murugan holding the spear.

Ancient sources: The Skanda Purana recounts the victory over Surapadman, and Sangam Tamil poetry mentions the spear as a weapon of kings and warriors.

Trivia

A spear with a crest. According to the myth, the tree split by the vel gave rise to the rooster and the peacock, two animals that accompany Murugan in his iconography.
