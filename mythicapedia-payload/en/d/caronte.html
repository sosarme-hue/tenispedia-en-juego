Charon

Charon is the ferryman of the Greek underworld, a gruff old man who carries the souls of the dead across the river that divides the world of the living from the realm of the dead. He matters because he embodies the belief that crossing into death required proper burial rites and a small payment.

The myth

According to tradition, Charon was a son of Nyx and Erebus, although no ancient source fixes his genealogy clearly. A dubious entry in the lexicon of Hesychius makes him a son of Acmon instead. His task is to ferry the dead across the rivers of the underworld, chiefly the Styx, though many accounts place the crossing on the Acheron. In the Aeneid, those who had received funeral rites and could pay the fee made the crossing, while the rest wandered the shore for one hundred years.

Charon was harsh and treated kings and commoners alike, seeing everyone naked and stripped of their possessions. According to Lucian, relatives placed a small coin, the obol, in the mouth of the dead to pay his fare. Some authors say he charged more to Athenian generals. He also ferried a few living visitors: Heracles, who overpowered him with his own pole according to Seneca, Aeneas, guided by the Sibyl of Cumae, and Orpheus, who charmed him while trying to bring back Eurydice. For letting Heracles pass without payment, some accounts say he was imprisoned for a year.

Worship and religion

No temples or festivals dedicated to Charon are recorded in the sources. His presence is felt mainly in funerary practice. In ancient Greece the dead were buried with a coin under the tongue, a custom that passed to Rome and has a parallel in Etruscan belief, where the separate figure of Charun guided the dead. Archaeology confirms low-value coins in some graves. Classical Athenian funerary vases of the 5th and 4th centuries BC show him as a rough seaman who receives the dead, and later vases portray him in a gentler, more refined manner.

Legacy

Charon has passed into art and literature. Dante places him at the entrance to hell in the Divine Comedy, where he is the first named mythological figure the poet meets, and Michelangelo shows him with an oar over his shoulder. Boccaccio read him as a symbol of time and of the passage from life to death. His name was given to Pluto's largest moon, Charon, and to a hadrosaur, Charonosaurus, found along the Amur River.

Ancient sources: He appears in the lost epic Minyas, quoted by Pausanias, and in works by Aristophanes, Euripides, Virgil, Seneca and Lucian.

Trivia

Coins in the tombs. Archaeology confirms that in some graves low-value coins were placed in or near the mouth of the dead, known as Charon's obols.
A moon named after him. Charon, the largest moon of Pluto, is named after the ferryman. Other moons of the dwarf planet carry names from the same underworld circle, such as Nix and Styx.
A dinosaur with his name. The hadrosaur Charonosaurus was named in Charon's honor because its remains were found along the banks of the Amur River in the Far East.
