Dragon King of the East Sea in Chinese mythology

Ao Guang

Ao Guang is the Dragon King of the East Sea, a figure in Chinese myth and folk religion. As the leader of the four Dragon Kings, he collected tribute from the eastern coasts in return for calm seas, tame rivers and rain for the crops.

The myth

According to the novel Fengshen Yanyi, Ao Guang spent years bringing droughts, storms and disasters until the Jade Emperor considered punishing him. One day Nezha, playing by a stream, shook the dragon's palace. The dragon sent a sentinel, who was killed by the boy, and then his third son, who also fell. Ao Guang then went to Nezha's father, Li Jing, and demanded that he sacrifice himself. Nezha confronted the dragon, beat him and forced him to submit.

In Journey to the West, the Monkey King Sun Wukong came to his undersea palace looking for a weapon. He chose the Ruyi Jingu Bang, a pillar that held back the tides and that nobody could lift. He also made the dragon hand over golden armour, a helmet and cloud boots, while Ao Guang cursed under his breath.

Worship and religion

Temples dedicated to the Dragon Kings exist in China. One was built in Beijing during the Yuan dynasty. In the Chaotian Palace in Beigang, Taiwan, which is dedicated to the goddess Mazu, the statues of the four kings take human form. The sources do not describe current rituals.

Legacy

He stars in the classic novels Fengshen Yanyi and Journey to the West. He appears as a mage in the video game SMITE, as a minor god in the second expansion of Age of Mythology, in the animated film Prince Nezha's Triumph Against Dragon King, in the Disney+ series American Born Chinese, and as the main antagonist of the Netflix film The Monkey King.

Ancient sources: The classic novels Fengshen Yanyi and Journey to the West, along with Buddhist sutras that name the Dragon King Sagara and a nāgarāja who controls the world's rain.

Trivia

A very common surname. As of 2008, Ao is the most common surname in China, shared by about 250,000 people.
A Buddhist tale. A Buddhist story in the Complete Tale of Avalokiteśvara and the Southern Seas says that Guanyin saved the dragon's third son and took his daughter Longnü as a disciple.
