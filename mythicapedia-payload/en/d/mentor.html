Odysseus's friend and Telemachus's guide in Greek myth

Mentor

Mentor is a figure of Greek mythology, the son of Alcimus and a trusted friend of Odysseus, king of Ithaca. His name gave rise to the common word "mentor", meaning a wise and experienced advisor who guides someone younger.

The myth

In Homer's Odyssey, when Odysseus left for Troy he placed Mentor in charge of his palace and of the upbringing of his son Telemachus. During his long absence, Ithaca was overrun by suitors who wanted to marry Penelope and who wrecked the household. When Telemachus called an assembly to seek help against them, Mentor tried in vain to rally the people to the prince's side.

Soon after, the goddess Athena took Mentor's form to accompany Telemachus on his journey to Pylos, to the court of Nestor, in search of news of his father. Tradition holds that she took on the same appearance again when Odysseus returned to Ithaca. Mentor appears only briefly in Book II, and he has a minor role as an independent character in the poem.

Legacy

In 1699 the French archbishop François Fénelon published The Adventures of Telemachus, in which Mentor takes on a far larger role as a teacher who instructs Odysseus's son in the virtues of a good king. The book was very popular in France at the time and was written for the education of the Duke of Burgundy, grandson of Louis XIV. In The Crime of Father Amaro (1875), Eça de Queirós refers to the "prudence of Mentor" combined with the "youth of Telemachus" to describe his protagonist. The name also entered language as a common noun, mentor, meaning an experienced counsellor or guide to a young person, and the word exists with similar meaning in Portuguese, English, German, Yiddish, Armenian and Russian.

Ancient sources: Homer's Odyssey, where he appears as Odysseus's friend and Telemachus's counsellor.
