Roman goddess of war

Bellona

Bellona is the ancient Roman goddess of war, embodying the destructive and brutal side of combat. Her figure became increasingly identified with the Greek goddess Enyo, and her temple played a key political role in Rome's decisions about war.

The myth

According to tradition, Bellona is sometimes described as the wife of Mars and at other times as his companion in battle. She is shown driving a war chariot, with a horrifying appearance that recalls the Furies.

Ancient authors describe her surrounded by thunder and blood. Horace speaks of the thunder of Bellona, who delights in bloodshed, and Ovid shows her drenching the sacred home in blood while the fighting never ends. Lucan gives her a bloody whip, and Silius Italicus has her holding the reins of Mars's chariot. Virgil mentions her in the description of Aeneas's shield, alongside the goddess Discordia.

Some scholars think Bellona may have been the original Roman god of war, before Mars was identified with Ares. She was thought to be one of the old Roman numina, possibly of Etruscan origin. Her name probably comes from an earlier Duellona, derived from duellum, the Old Latin word for war.

Worship and religion

Her priests, the Bellonarii, were known for ritual self-harm during their ceremonies. Her temple, located outside the pomerium, the sacred boundary of the city, was a common meeting place for the Senate when it discussed foreign wars. There, generals who were not allowed to enter the city could be awarded triumphs. Near the temple stood a war column representing foreign territory. To declare war, a fetial priest threw a javelin over it towards the enemy land. Augustus later moved these Senate meetings to the temple of Mars Ultor.

Legacy

Her name lies behind the English word "belligerent", formed from bellum, 'war', and gerere, 'to wage'. In post-Renaissance art she was shown with a helmet, sword, spear or shield, and often with a torch or whip, as an image of war in its crueller aspect.

Ancient sources: Virgil in the Aeneid, Ovid, Horace in the Satires, Lucan, Silius Italicus in the Punica and Plautus mention Bellona.

In art

Trivia

The cup of Bellona. The earliest known image of the goddess appears on a Campanian cup from the 4th century BCE, inscribed with the phrase 'cup of Bellona'. She is shown with a long neck and her hair tied up.
A theatre beside her temple. Augustus built the Theatre of Marcellus near the temple of Bellona, leaving only a narrow gap between the two buildings, which may have forced triumphal processions to take a different route.
