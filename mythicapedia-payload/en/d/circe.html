Circe

Circe is a witch and goddess in Greek mythology, usually described as a daughter of the sun god Helios and the Oceanid Perse, who lives on the island of Aeaea. She matters chiefly because of Homer's Odyssey, where she turns Odysseus's sailors into swine, and because later artists and writers made her the archetype of the seductive and dangerous woman.

The myth

By most accounts Circe was the daughter of Helios and Perse, although some ancient authors give her mother the names Asterope or Rhode. Hesiod makes her a sister of Aeetes, the father of Medea. A later, rationalising version, found in Dionysius Scytobrachion and Diodorus Siculus, makes her the daughter of Hecate and Aeetes, but there Hecate is a mortal woman who discovered drugs, not a goddess.

In the Odyssey, Circe lives in a stone mansion in the middle of Aeaea. Lions and wolves wander around her house; they are men she has transformed, and they are tame and friendly toward strangers. When Odysseus arrives, she invites his sailors to a feast, drugs them and turns them into pigs with her wand. Only Eurylochus escapes, having suspected treachery. Odysseus goes to rescue them, protected by the herb moly that Hermes gave him, and forces Circe to restore their human shape. He then stays with her for a year, and she advises him on the route home to Ithaca, past Scylla and Charybdis or through the wandering rocks.

In later stories, Circe turns the Latian king Picus into a woodpecker for rejecting her love, and she poisons the waters where Scylla bathes, because Scylla was loved by Glaucus, turning her into a monster with dogs around her waist. According to Hesiod she bore Agrius and Latinus to Odysseus, and Telegonus to Aphrodite's favour, and these sons ruled over the Tyrrhenians. Other writers added more children and linked them to Italian cities.

Legacy

Circe has inspired many works. Chapter 15 of James Joyce's Ulysses is titled 'Circe', and the story by Julio Cortázar that gives its name to the collection Bestiary (1951) also draws on her. C. S. Lewis reimagined her in the White Witch of The Chronicles of Narnia, and Madeline Miller's novel Circe (2018) tells the story from her point of view. In DC Comics she is a recurring enemy of Wonder Woman. In enzymology, a chemical phenomenon is named the Circe effect after her.

Ancient sources: Homer's Odyssey, Hesiod's Theogony and Apollonius of Rhodes's Argonautica, as well as Ovid's Metamorphoses.

Trivia

A woodpecker. Circe turned Picus, a Latian king, into a woodpecker for rejecting her love. In this way an Italian episode became part of her legend.
An effect named after her. In enzymology, a phenomenon is called the Circe effect, showing that her figure remains present even outside literature.
