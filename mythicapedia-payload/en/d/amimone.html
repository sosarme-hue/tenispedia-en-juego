Danaid, daughter of Danaus, beloved of Poseidon

Amymone

Amymone is one of the fifty Danaids, daughters of Danaus, and in some tellings the only one who did not kill her husband on their wedding night. She is best known for her encounter with Poseidon and for the myth behind the springs of Lerna.

The myth

According to the tradition, Danaus married his daughters to the fifty sons of his brother Aegyptus in a marriage of convenience. Amymone was matched with Enceladus. Some accounts say she killed him on the wedding night, as most of her sisters did; others insist she spared him, like Hypermnestra. Some sources instead make her the wife of Lynceus.

Apollodorus tells how Poseidon dried up the springs of Argos after Inachus declared the land belonged to Hera. Danaus sent his daughters to fetch water. Amymone shot an arrow at a deer but struck a sleeping satyr by mistake, and he tried to seize her. Poseidon appeared, drove the satyr off, lay with her, and revealed the springs of Lerna. In another version, the god hurled his trident into a rock, and a spring gushed forth.

By Poseidon she bore Nauplius, a seafarer who lured ships with torches to wreck them. His sons were Palamedes, Oeax and Nausimedon.

Worship and religion

No source describes a cult devoted to Amymone herself. Her name is tied to the springs of Argos and to the ancient sanctuary at Lerna, a site linked to Poseidon, whose worship the tradition places before that of Hera in the region.

Legacy

Aeschylus wrote a lost satyr play titled Amymone about her seduction by Poseidon. Her son Nauplius gave his name to the port city of Nafplio (Nauplia). In art she is often shown with a water pitcher, a symbol of the springs of Argos.

Ancient sources: Apollodorus and Hyginus mention her, and Aeschylus wrote a satyr play about her that is now lost.

Trivia

A city named after her son. Nauplius, son of Amymone and Poseidon, gave his name to the port city of Nafplio (Nauplia) in the Argolid.
A water pitcher as her emblem. She is shown with a water pitcher, a reminder of the springs of Argos and a contrast with her sisters, who were condemned to fill a bottomless tub.
Two possible Amymones. A naiad daughter of Inachus shares her name, and the two figures may have been confused at some point.
