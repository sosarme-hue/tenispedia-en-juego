sea monster of European tradition

Bishop-fish

The sea bishop, or bishop-fish, is a legendary sea creature from European tradition, first recorded in the 16th century. Described as half man and half fish, its fame comes from tales in which it asks to be freed and vanishes into the sea after making the sign of the cross.

The myth

According to the legend, the sea bishop was taken before the King of Poland, who wished to keep it captive. It was then shown to a group of Catholic bishops, to whom the creature gestured, pleading to be released. They granted its wish, but as soon as it was free it made the sign of the cross and disappeared into the sea. Its appearance was half human and half fish: its torso was covered in scales, and a scaly, hood-like covering framed its head.

Another, so the story goes, was captured in the ocean near Germany in 1531. It refused to eat and died after three days. The naturalist Conrad Gesner described and pictured it in the fourth volume of his Historiae animalium, published between 1551 and 1558. For cryptozoologist Bernard Heuvelmans, the report may have been based on the discovery of a large, mutilated Grimaldi scaled squid.

Legacy

Today the Spanish name is given to the species Pteromylaeus bovinus, also called chucho vaca or raya toro, because its shape recalls the fish of the legend. The bishop-fish also appears in the Monster in My Pocket collection, as monster number 058.

Ancient sources: Conrad Gesner, Historiae animalium, fourth volume (1551-1558), which describes and pictures it.

Trivia

A squid behind the legend. Cryptozoologist Bernard Heuvelmans thought the story may have arisen from the discovery of a mutilated Grimaldi scaled squid.
A fish named after a bishop. The species Pteromylaeus bovinus is today called Pez Obispo in Spanish, although its shape only loosely recalls the legendary fish.
