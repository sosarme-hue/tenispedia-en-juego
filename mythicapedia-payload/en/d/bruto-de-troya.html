legendary founder of Britain

Brutus of Troy

Brutus of Troy is a legendary hero of British tradition, a descendant of the Trojan Aeneas and regarded as the founder and first king of Britain. His story matters because it ties the origin of the island to the lineage of the heroes of Troy.

The myth

According to the legend, Brutus was born in Italy, in the line of Aeneas and Ascanius, founders of Alba Longa. His mother died giving birth, and a seer foretold that he would be the bravest and most beloved man in the land. Yet while hunting he accidentally killed his father Silvius with an arrow, and he was banished.

In exile he freed a group of enslaved Trojans in Greece, who chose him as their leader. After a vision at a temple of Diana, he set out for a western island inhabited only by giants. Following battles in Gaul, where he founded Tours, and with the help of his Trojan companion Corineus, he landed at Totnes, defeated the giants and gave the island his name. He married Innogen, daughter of the Greek king Pandrasus.

He founded Trinovantum on the Thames, which Geoffrey explained as "New Troy", a name said to have become London. He gave laws to his people and, at his death, divided the realm among his three sons: Locrinus (England), Albanactus (Scotland) and Kamber (Wales).

Legacy

His story gave its name to the word brut, which came to mean a chronicle of British history, as in Wace's Norman French Roman de Brut and Layamon's Brut. In Welsh, the Brut y Brenhinedd adapted Geoffrey's work. For centuries, chroniclers such as Holinshed in 1577 treated him as real history. On stage, Nahum Tate wrote Brutus of Alba in 1678, and in 1801 John Ogilvie published the epic poem Britannia about his arrival in the island.

Ancient sources: The ninth-century Historia Brittonum, attributed to Nennius, and Geoffrey of Monmouth's twelfth-century Historia Regum Britanniae, the best-known version.

Trivia

A stone in Totnes. On Fore Street in Totnes, Devon, a stone known as the Brutus Stone marks the spot where, according to Geoffrey, Brutus and his companions landed.
A doubtful etymology. Isidore of Seville linked Britain to bruti, meaning brutes or savages, and the name Brutus may have grown out of that unfounded etymology.
