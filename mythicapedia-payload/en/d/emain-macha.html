Emain Macha

Emain Macha, today Navan Fort, is an ancient ceremonial enclosure near Armagh in Northern Ireland. According to tradition it was the capital of the Ulaid and one of the great royal sites of pre-Christian Gaelic Ireland, and it plays a key role in the Ulster Cycle.

The myth

In Irish tales, the goddess Macha founded the site. Its name is explained in more than one way: "Macha's brooch", after she marked its boundaries with her pin, or "Macha's twins", after she gave birth to children following a forced chariot race.

In the Ulster Cycle it is the seat of Conchobar mac Nessa, who supposedly kept three houses there: the Cróeb Ruad, where the king sat; the Cróeb Derg, for battle trophies; and the Téte Brecc, for warriors' weapons. After Fergus Foga was defeated at the battle of Achadh Leithdheirg, the Three Collas burned the site, and it was abandoned.

Worship and religion

Excavations point to a pagan ritual space rather than a fortress. Around 95 BC a huge timber structure with a central oak pillar was erected here; it was then filled with stones, deliberately burned and covered with earth. Many researchers read it as a sacrificial offering, and its western entrance, facing the setting sun, suggests it was not a home. One theory holds that the mound served as a platform where druids performed ceremonies and kings were crowned. No living cult survives.

Legacy

Navan Fort is the heart of the Navan complex, alongside Haughey's Fort, the King's Stables and Loughnashade lake. It was on the UK's Tentative List between 1988 and 1989, and its visitor centre now opens only seasonally.

Ancient sources: The Annals of the Four Masters record its abandonment after the Three Collas burned it in 331.

Trivia

A ditch in the wrong place. The ditch lies inside the bank rather than outside it, suggesting the earthwork was symbolic rather than defensive.
A monkey at the site. Among the finds is the skull of a Barbary monkey, likely a pet that was imported or given as a gift.
A dated oak pillar. The central oak pillar was dated by dendrochronology to 95 BC and could have stood about 13 metres tall.
