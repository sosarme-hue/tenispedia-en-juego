bodhisattva of wisdom in Mahayana Buddhism

Manjushri

Manjushri is a bodhisattva in Mahayana Buddhism who embodies the transcendent wisdom (prajñā) of the Buddhas. He is regarded as one of the oldest and most important bodhisattvas in that tradition, and in Vajrayana Buddhism he is considered a fully enlightened Buddha.

The myth

His name can be rendered as Beautiful One with Glory. According to the Mahayana sutras, Manjushri is in fact a Buddha who has always reached complete awakening, though in our world he appears as a bodhisattva. The Śūraṅgama Samādhi Sūtra tells that countless eons ago he had already attained Buddhahood under the name Dragon Superior Tathagata. Another text places him as a Buddha in the north, in a world called Constant Joy, and states that he will become a Buddha again in this world under the name Universally Seen.

Tradition presents him as a guide for all beings. One Mahayana sutra calls him the mother of all Buddhas of past, present and future, since the wish to awaken arises from his teaching. According to the Avatamsaka Sutra, his pure land, Vimala, lies in the East. In the Lotus Sutra he leads the daughter of a naga king toward enlightenment, and in the Vimalakirti Sutra he debates the layman Vimalakirti on non-duality, the only bodhisattva who dares to do so.

Worship and religion

In Vajrayana Buddhism he is a meditational deity, and in Shingon he is one of the Thirteen Buddhas venerated by practitioners. He appears in many esoteric texts, such as the Mañjuśrīmūlakalpa, and in some traditions his consort is Saraswati. In China he is one of the Four Great Bodhisattvas, often paired with Samantabhadra, and is venerated in his thousand-armed, thousand-bowl form. In Tibetan Buddhism he sometimes appears in a trinity with Avalokiteshvara and Vajrapani. Je Tsongkhapa, founder of the Gelug school, is said to have received his teachings in visions. He is typically shown as a youth seated on a lotus, with a flaming sword in his right hand cutting through ignorance and a Prajñāpāramitā sutra on the lotus in his left; he is often seated on or riding a blue lion, symbolizing the taming of the mind.

Legacy

His image has been widely painted in China and Japan, where the ruyi scepter sometimes replaces the sword. According to Berthold Laufer, the first Chinese depiction of a ruyi appears in an 8th-century painting of Manjushri by Wu Daozi. The arapacana syllabary, named after its first five letters, is associated with his mantra and was used with the Kharoshthi script for the Gandhari language.

Ancient sources: Mahayana sutras such as the Lotus Sutra, the Vimalakirti Sutra, the Śūraṅgama Samādhi Sūtra and the Saptaśatikā Prajñāpāramitā Sūtra mention him, alongside esoteric texts such as the Mañjuśrīmūlakalpa.

Trivia

A Buddha who appears as a disciple. According to some sutras, although he is a Buddha, he appears as a disciple of Shakyamuni so that the same world does not have two main Buddhas.
A thousand arms, a thousand bowls. In his Chinese form with a thousand arms, each hand holds a bowl containing a figure of Shakyamuni.
