helmsman of Menelaus in Greek mythology

Canopus

Canopus was, according to Greek tradition, the helmsman of the Spartan king Menelaus, and his name later spread to an Egyptian city and to one of the brightest stars in the southern sky. His story links the Trojan War cycle with Egyptian legends about his death and transformation.

The myth

According to the tale, Canopus was a handsome young Spartan from Amyclae who served as pilot of Menelaus's ship. After the fall of Troy, Menelaus travelled to Egypt with Helen. There Theonoe, an Egyptian prophetess, fell in love with him, but her feelings were never returned.

One day, while visiting the Egyptian coast, Canopus was bitten by a serpent and died. Menelaus and Helen carried his body to a place that Menelaus named Canopus in his honour, and he was buried there. Helen mourned him, and from her tears a plant called helenium is said to have sprung.

Another version makes him the pilot of the Egyptian god Osiris. In this account, Canopus steered the ship Argo, and both he and the vessel were transformed into constellations.

Legacy

The Egyptian city of Canopus, near Alexandria, took its name from the place where he was said to have died. Canopus, the brightest star in the southern constellation Carina, the keel of the ship Argo, also bears his name and is the second-brightest star in the night sky after Sirius. The last de Havilland Comet jet airliner to fly was also named Canopus.

Trivia

A star bearing his name. Canopus is the second-brightest star in the night sky, surpassed only by Sirius. It lies in Carina, the keel, which in tradition is linked to the ship Argo.
