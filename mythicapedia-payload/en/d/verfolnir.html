hawk of Norse mythology

Veðrfölnir

Veðrfölnir is a hawk in Norse mythology that sits between the eyes of an unnamed eagle perched at the top of Yggdrasil, the world tree. It appears mainly in the Prose Edda, and its name, which suggests a "storm pale" or "wind bleached" bird, makes it a rare but recognizable creature within the Norse cosmos.

The myth

In Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda, in chapter 16 of Gylfaginning, the hawk Veðrfölnir sits between the eyes of an eagle that keeps watch from the crown of Yggdrasil, the ash tree that holds up the world. The eagle has no name in the texts and is described as knowing many things, so the hawk sits right beside that wisdom.

The story does not stop with the hawk but places the tree within a web of messages. The squirrel Ratatoskr runs up and down the trunk carrying gossip and slander between the eagle and Nidhöggr, the dragon that gnaws at the roots below, and so provokes them both. In the Poetic Edda poem Grímnismál, Odin, disguised as Grimnir, also speaks of the eagle, which shows that the image was part of the tradition before Snorri set it down in writing.

Beyond this scene, the sources say nothing more about Veðrfölnir: there are no deeds, no genealogy and no ending. Its role is reduced to a detail of the cosmic landscape. In the Prose Edda it appears within High's answer, given from the enthroned seat, to the questions of King Gylfi in disguise as Gangleri.

Worship and religion

The sources do not document any cult devoted to Veðrfölnir. It is known, in general terms, that Germanic peoples worshipped their gods in forest clearings and that a sky god was especially linked to the oak. For the scholar Hilda Ellis Davidson, the central tree was a natural symbol for them, which places Yggdrasil within that symbolic world, although no specific rites for the hawk are recorded.

Ancient sources: The Poetic Edda (Grímnismál), compiled in the 13th century from earlier traditional sources, and Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda (Gylfaginning), also written in the 13th century.

Trivia

A name with wind in it. Its Old Norse name has been translated as "storm pale" or "bleached by the weather", a reference that scholars interpret in several ways.
The gossip of the ash. The squirrel Ratatoskr runs along the tree spreading gossip and slander, provoking the eagle and Nidhöggr.
