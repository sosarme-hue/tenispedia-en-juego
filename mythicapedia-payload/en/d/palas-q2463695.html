giant of Greek mythology linked to Athena

Pallas

Pallas was one of the Giants of Greek mythology, associated with Athena (Minerva in Roman tradition). He matters because, according to one tradition, he gave his name to an epithet of the goddess and helps explain why Athena wears a flayed hide as her aegis.

The myth

According to the mythographer Apollodorus, Pallas was one of the Giants, offspring of Gaia, and in other versions he was born from the blood of the castrated Uranus. During the Gigantomachy, the cosmic battle between the Giants and the Olympian gods, Athena killed him, flayed him and used his skin as a shield.

A fragment of Epicharmus says the goddess took her name from the Giant after wrapping herself in his skin as a cloak, though the origin of the epithet "Pallas" remains obscure. Euripides describes a similar episode in which Athena Pallas killed the Gorgon and has since worn the aegis's hide on her back. Latin poets also speak of another Pallas, the father of Minerva, who supposedly tried to rape her. Claudian places the Giant among those turned to stone by the goddess's shield, while his brother Damastor uses the petrified body as a weapon. Some traditions confuse the Giant with a Titan of the same name.

Worship and religion

Pallas is mainly associated with Attic myth. According to Pausanias, local traditions linked him to the region of Pallene, showing that his story became part of the religious and geographic memory of Attica. Some scholars suggest his figure was connected with ritual dances or martial displays, such as the female pyrrhic dance performed in honour of Athena. The sources give no evidence that his cult survives today.

Legacy

His clearest legacy is the epithet "Pallas" that accompanies Athena, although its origin is still uncertain. The scene of Giants petrified by the aegis was recorded by Claudian in his Gigantomachia, and the figure illustrates how late authors imagined beings who predated the Olympian gods.

Ancient sources: Apollodorus, Epicharmus, Euripides and Claudian, who mention him in the Library, in a fragment of drama and in the Gigantomachia.

Trivia

A shared name. Pallas was also the name of a Titan, and authors sometimes confused or identified the two figures.
Turned to stone. In Claudian's Gigantomachia, Pallas is turned to stone and his brother Damastor hurls his body at the gods as a weapon.
