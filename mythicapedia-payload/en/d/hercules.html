Roman hero of superhuman strength, the Roman counterpart of Heracles

Hercules

Hercules is the semidivine hero of Roman mythology, son of Jupiter and the mortal Alcmena. The Romans took over the Greek hero Heracles and gave him a character of their own, casting him as protector of humankind and a symbol of strength and virtue. Few figures from antiquity have survived so strongly in Western art, language and popular culture.

The myth

In the Roman tradition, Hercules's life began with trouble. Juno, wife of Jupiter and the child's enemy, sent two witches to stop the birth and then two serpents to kill him in his cradle, but the baby strangled both. In another version, Alcmena abandoned him in the woods to shield him from the goddess's wrath, and it was Minerva who found him and brought him to Juno, presenting him as an orphan. Juno nursed him, but the boy bit her nipple and she pushed him away so roughly that her spilled milk formed the Milky Way. Minerva then raised him, and the divine milk gave him extra strength.

As an adult, Hercules became the champion of the weak and a protector of humanity. He carried out the twelve great labours and was deified after his death. Among his distinctly Roman exploits was his defeat of Cacus, a monster who terrorised the countryside around Rome; the hero was also linked to the Aventine Hill through his son Aventinus. Herodotus assigned him an Egyptian origin, earlier than the Greek myths, in a process of cultural assimilation like the one that affected almost every deity of the Greek pantheon.

Worship and religion

Hercules received worship across much of Italy, especially in the centre and south, and the Romans venerated him at the Forum Boarium. Bronze statuettes were dedicated to him in sanctuaries throughout Samnium and the Apennines, and more than a hundred were found near Corfinium. He was invoked as a protector of children and childbirth, and Roman brides wore a special belt tied with the Knot of Hercules, which was meant to be hard to untie. His cult spread through the provinces, from Hispania to Gaul.

Legacy

The Spanish adjective hercúleo, meaning very strong and muscular, has its English echo in herculean, and the Pillars of Hercules at the Strait of Gibraltar or the Tower of Hercules in A Coruña keep his name on real places. In the Renaissance and Baroque, Hercules became a symbol of the virtuous prince who masters his passions, as in the theme of Hercules at the Crossroads, and in the Middle Ages he was read as a moral model. His name also entered the Latin language in oaths such as Mehercle!

Ancient sources: Plautus brought his conception to comedy in Amphitryon, Seneca wrote the tragedy Hercules Furens about his madness, Dionysius of Halicarnassus stressed his popularity in Italy, and Tacitus linked him with the Germanic peoples in his Germania.

Trivia

The Milky Way. According to the myth, the milk Juno spilled when she pushed away the infant Hercules formed the band of stars we now call the Milky Way.
A Latin oath. Mehercle! or Hercule! was a very common interjection in Classical Latin, used as a mild oath.
