legendary heroine of Carcassonne (France)

Lady Carcas

Lady Carcas is the legendary heroine to whom tradition credits the saving of Carcassonne, in southern France, through a clever trick during Charlemagne's siege. Her legend explains the city's name and is one of the few that presents a heroine in control of her own fate.

The myth

According to the story, the city was ruled by the Saracen king Balaak, who died after refusing to surrender it to Charlemagne. His widow, Lady Carcas, organised the defence: she placed dummy soldiers armed with crossbows on the walls and shot arrows from the ramparts to make it look as if the men were still there.

When food ran short, she had the city's only pig fattened on wheat and thrown from the highest tower. The Franks, believing the town still had plenty, lifted the siege. Lady Carcas then had the horn or the bells sounded, and one of Charlemagne's men cried out "Carcas sonne!", the origin of the city's name. Depending on the version, she converted to Christianity and married a Frankish noble.

Legacy

A replica of her bust greets visitors at the entrance of the castle's drawbridge, and the city has an inn that bears her name. Shortbreads called "Friandises de Dame Carcas" are sold at a local grocery store.

Ancient sources: The first written mention is Jean Dupré's poem (1534), dedicated to Margaret of Angoulême; Guillaume Catel and Guillaume Besse later recorded the story in the 17th century. Earlier texts such as the Song of the Albigensian Crusade and the Song of Roland allude to the siege of the city.

Trivia

A name from a sound. The phrase "Carcas sonne" plays on the sound of the city's name, and according to the legend Carcassonne takes its name from it.
A coat of arms with bells. The wordplay appears on the coat of arms of the Carcassonne family, painted in a late 16th-century mansion in Montpellier.
