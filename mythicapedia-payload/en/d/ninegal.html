Mesopotamian goddess of royal palaces

Ninegal

Ninegal (also Belat Ekalli, "lady of the palace") was a Mesopotamian goddess who protected royal palaces and the sovereignty of kings and governors. Her worship spread as far as Syria, Elam and the Hittite Empire.

The myth

Ninegal survives less through narratives than through god lists and administrative texts. For a long time she was thought to be a form of Inanna, but today she is seen as a distinct minor goddess who, in literature, came to be used as an epithet of Inanna.

She could act as an intercessor before her husband Urash, much like other divine wives.

Worship and religion

She had temples in Lagash, Ur (the Egalmah), Assur (the Ekinam) and Dilbat. In Mari and Qatna she was the tutelary deity of the ruling house and of the city. Ur III kings such as Ur-Namma and Shulgi took part in her cult. There is no evidence that her worship survives today.

Legacy

Her name appears in Mesopotamian theophoric personal names and, as Pentikalli, in Hurrian and Ugaritic texts.

Ancient sources: God lists, dedicatory inscriptions, Ur III administrative texts, and letters from Mari and Babylon.

In art

Trivia

Exalted palace. Her main temple in Ur was called Egalmah, "exalted palace", a name that reflects her role as protector of palaces.
Honored with sacrifices. In Mari she received, during some festivals, the same number of sacrifices as eight other highly honored gods.
