Chinese god of wealth and prosperity

Caishen

Caishen (Cai Shen) is the Chinese god of prosperity, worshipped in Chinese folk religion and Taoism. His name is often invoked during Chinese New Year celebrations, and he is linked both to honest, salaried wealth and to commercial fortune.

The myth

Caishen has no single origin: the figure seems to merge several legends, and the oldest is that of Bi Gan, a Shang dynasty prince and minister put to death by his nephew Di Xin. According to the story, Bi Gan had a wife of the Chen clan and a son named Quan. After the execution, his wife and son fled into the woods, and Quan was later honoured by King Wu of Zhou as the founding ancestor of the Lin clan. Bi Gan's deification as a wealth god is one of the oldest layers of the tradition.

A separate strand centres on Caibo Xingjun, the Star Lord of Wealth and Silk, commonly identified with Li Guizu, a magistrate from Zichuan District in Shandong. Tradition holds that he was honoured posthumously as a wealth deity in the early Tang dynasty for his administrative service. Over time, Zhao Gongming became the central figure of the Five Roads Wealth Gods, flanked by four assistants representing the cardinal directions. He is often shown riding a black tiger and holding a golden rod, or with an iron tool that can turn stone and iron into gold.

Worship and religion

Caishen is among the most prominent deities greeted during Chinese New Year. Families in northern China welcome the God of Wealth on the second day of the first lunar month, while in southern and overseas Chinese communities the main observance falls on the fifth day. Offerings include fish, fruit and ingot-shaped dumplings, and many businesses time their first trading day after the holiday to match. Worship remains alive, and Pure Land Buddhists and Tibetan Buddhists also venerate him, the latter as Jambhala.

Legacy

He appears in the Ming novel The Investiture of the Gods, which dramatises Bi Gan's story at length. He is sometimes confused with Fu Xing, one of the Fushoulu deities of fortune, prosperity and longevity.

Ancient sources: The Ming novel The Investiture of the Gods and the religious compendium Sanjiao Soushen Daquan contain his most elaborate versions.

Trivia

Two kinds of wealth gods. Tradition separates civil wealth gods, such as Bi Gan and Fan Li, shown in court robes, from military ones, such as Zhao Gongming and Guan Yu, shown in armour.
A god with many identities. Caishen has been identified with many historical and legendary figures, and it is unclear whether any of them really existed.
