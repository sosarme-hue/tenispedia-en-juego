Thracian king and seer in Greek myth

Phineus

Phineus is a king of Salmydessus in Thrace and a blind seer in Greek myth, best known for his role in the voyage of the Argonauts. Cursed by the gods and tormented by the Harpies, he helped Jason and his crew cross the dangerous waters toward Colchis.

The myth

Ancient sources disagree about his parentage. Apollonius of Rhodes makes him a son of Agenor, the Bibliotheca notes that other authors named Poseidon as the ultimate father, and the Hesiodic Catalogue of Women gives him as the son of Phoenix and Cassiopeia. With his first wife, Cleopatra, daughter of Boreas and Oreithyia, he had two sons, usually called Plexippus and Pandion, though other sources give different names. He later married Idaea, daughter of Dardanus, who bore him Mariandynus and Thynus. In the best-known version, Idaea persuaded him to blind the sons of Cleopatra, and for this the gods blinded Phineus in turn.

The reasons for his blindness vary. Hesiod says he showed Phrixus the way to Colchis, or that he preferred a long life to sight. In the Argonautica, the most famous account, he was punished for revealing the future to mortals. Helios, or sometimes Zeus, sent the Harpies to snatch or foul his food whenever he tried to eat.

When the Argo reached his Thracian home, Phineus told the crew of his suffering. The winged sons of Boreas, Calais and Zetes, chased the Harpies as far as the Floating Islands, where Iris stopped them with an oath sworn by the Styx, and the Boreads returned to the Argonauts. The islands were therefore called the Strophades, the Turning Islands. In gratitude, Phineus described the course of the voyage and told Jason how to pass the Symplegades, advising him to send a dove through the clashing rocks first.

Legacy

Aeschylus wrote a now-lost tragedy titled Phineus, the first play of a trilogy that included The Persians, staged in 472 B.C. The story of Phineus and Cleopatra is also briefly mentioned in Sophocles' Antigone.

Ancient sources: Hesiod (Catalogue of Women), Apollonius of Rhodes (Argonautica), the Bibliotheca, the scholia on the Odyssey, Aeschylus and Sophocles.

Trivia

The Strophades. The Floating Islands where Iris halted the sons of Boreas were later called the Strophades, the Turning Islands, after the place where the Boreads turned back.
A final punishment. According to one account, Helios eventually turned Phineus into a mole, a blind creature, for an unspecified offence.
