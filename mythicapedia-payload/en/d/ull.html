Norse god of archery, hunting and skiing

Ullr

Ullr is a Norse god linked to archery, hunting and skiing. His name, rooted in the idea of glory, suggests he may have been an important deity in prehistoric Scandinavia.

The myth

In Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda, Ullr is the son of Sif and therefore stepson of Thor, although his father is not recorded in surviving sources. Snorri describes him briefly in the Gylfaginning but relates no myths about him. In the Skáldskaparmál he simply lists his epithets, such as ski-god, bow-god, hunting-god and shield-god, and notes that a shield may be called Ullr's ship.

Skaldic poetry confirms the link with Thor. Three poems, including Haustlöng and Þórsdrápa, call Thor Ullr's stepfather. Kennings such as "Ullr's ship" for a shield also survive. Their origin is unclear, though they may connect with early skis, which could have resembled shields.

In the Poetic Edda, Ullr appears in the Grímnismál, where his dwelling is called Ýdalir, "yew dales", and yew was used to make bows. An obscure stanza in the same poem seems to refer to a religious ceremony. In the Atlakviða he is again linked to a rite, this time the swearing of oaths on a ring. Both poems are among the oldest of the Eddic collection, and they are the only ones that mention him.

Worship and religion

The sources say little about his worship. Place-names in Norway and Sweden that contain his name, and the Lilla Ullevi site in Sweden, with a rock arrangement from the Vendel period, suggest he held greater religious importance in Scandinavia than the texts reveal. His name often appears beside those of Njörðr and Freyr. No details of his rituals survive, and nothing indicates that his worship continues today.

Legacy

His name survives mainly in Norwegian and Swedish place-names, such as Ullensvang and Ulvik. Ullevaal Stadium in Oslo takes its name from an old farm called Ullarhváll, meaning "Ullr's hill". Philologists link his name to the Germanic word for glory, and to Latin vultus and Old Irish filed. Jan de Vries proposed that Ullr stood in opposition to Óðinn, a contrast he compared to the Vedic pair Varuna and Mitra.

Ancient sources: Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda, the Poetic Edda (Grímnismál and Atlakviða), skaldic poetry, and the Thorsberg chape, where his name may appear in an inscription from the 3rd century AD.

Trivia

Ullr's ship. In skaldic poetry, a shield could be called "Ullr's ship". Snorri records the expression but does not explain why it was used.
An oath on a ring. In the Atlakviða, Ullr is linked to swearing oaths on a ring. The ring was later associated with Thor in a reference to Norse settlers in Dublin.
