god of Maharashtra, a form of Shiva

Khandoba

Khandoba is a form of Shiva worshipped mainly in Maharashtra and Karnataka, in the Deccan region of India. He is the most popular family deity (kuladevata) in Maharashtra and the patron of warriors, shepherds, farmers and hunter-gatherer communities.

The myth

According to the Malhari Mahatmya, the demons Malla and his younger brother Mani, invincible thanks to a boon from Brahma, spread chaos and tormented the sages. They sought Shiva's help, and he appeared as Martanda Bhairava, golden like the sun, with three eyes, riding the bull Nandi at the head of a divine army.

Khandoba killed both demons. Dying, Mani offered him his white horse and asked to be present in his shrines and receive offerings of goat meat. Malla, granted a boon, asked for the destruction of the world, and Khandoba beheaded him. His wives Mhalsa, jealous but an excellent cook, and Banai, a shepherd woman who came to him through love and capture, star in folk songs full of their quarrels.

Worship and religion

His main sanctuary is the temple of Jejuri in Maharashtra, where he is honoured as a kingly figure who rules from his fortress and distributes gold at his court. He is worshipped as a linga, the symbol of Shiva, or as a warrior on horseback accompanied by one or more dogs, and many temples display both forms. His devotees, the muralis, girls "married" to him, are part of his cult. The worship remains alive, and his sect welcomes people of all castes, including Muslims.

Ancient sources: The Malhari Mahatmya, a Sanskrit text composed around 1460–1510 that claims to belong to the Brahmanda Purana, though it is absent from standard editions of that Purana, and a Marathi version of 1585 by Siddhapal Kesasri.

Trivia

Two sacred forms. Many temples house both the linga, the symbol of Shiva, and the human-like image of Khandoba on horseback.
A cult open to all. His sect attracts followers from different castes and also Muslims, which is unusual for local cults.
