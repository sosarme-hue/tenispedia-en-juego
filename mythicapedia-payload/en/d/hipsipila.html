queen of Lemnos in Greek myth

Hypsipyle

Hypsipyle is the queen of Lemnos in Greek myth, daughter of King Thoas and mother of twin sons by Jason, Euneus and Nebrophonus. Her story, tied to the massacre of the island's men and the voyage of the Argonauts, gave the language the phrase "Lemnian crime".

The myth

According to the tradition, Aphrodite punished the women of Lemnos for neglecting her worship by making their husbands reject them in favour of captive Thracian women. In revenge, the women killed every man on the island. Only Hypsipyle saved her father Thoas, hiding him. According to Valerius Flaccus, she disguised him as the cult statue of Dionysus and led him out of the city on the ritual chariot.

When the Argonauts arrived, Hypsipyle ruled the island. In Apollonius of Rhodes, the women feared they were Thracian attackers and prepared gifts to send the men away, but the old nurse Polyxo urged them to take the Argonauts as mates and protectors. Hypsipyle had two sons by Jason, Euneus and Nebrophonus according to Apollodorus, or Deipylus according to Hyginus. Jason sailed on and forgot her. The Lemnian women, furious that she had saved her father, forced her to flee. Captured by pirates, she was sold to Lycurgus, king of Nemea, and cared for his infant son Opheltes, who was killed by a serpent while in her charge.

Legacy

The phrase "Lemnian crime", recorded by Herodotus in the 5th century BC, came to mean any cruel deed. Aeschylus staged the story in tragedies now lost, and Euripides wrote a play about it of which fragments survive.

Ancient sources: The Iliad mentions Euneus as the son of Jason and Hypsipyle. The story is also told by Aeschylus, Euripides, Apollonius of Rhodes, Valerius Flaccus, Apollodorus and Hyginus.

Trivia

A proverb born from a myth. Herodotus records the phrase "Lemnian crime" as a synonym for any cruel deed, showing how famous the story had become.
A father disguised as a god. According to Valerius Flaccus, Hypsipyle hid her father as the cult statue of Dionysus and carried him out of the city on the ritual chariot.
