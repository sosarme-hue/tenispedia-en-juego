Wendigo

The wendigo is a malevolent creature or spirit from the folklore of Algonquian-speaking peoples such as the Ojibwe, Cree and Innu, in northern North America. It matters because it embodies gluttony and cannibalism, and it has given its name to a controversial psychiatric syndrome.

The myth

According to tradition, the wendigo was originally a human who, after eating another person to survive famine, became possessed by a spirit and was transformed. Accounts vary: in some it is a giant that grows with every victim, in others a large, white-haired forest spirit that feeds on moss. Its hunger is never satisfied, so it constantly hunts new victims.

Some stories say it calls its prey by name until they are lost in the forest. Others portray it as a mere companion of the traveller, vanishing whenever the traveller looks back, so that the real danger lies in the fear it provokes.

Worship and religion

The Algonquian peoples associated it with winter, the north, cold and famine. The myth served as a warning against cannibalism and a call to moderation. Among the Assiniboine, Cree and Ojibwe, a satirical dance called wiindigookaanzhimowin was performed in times of famine, with masks and dancing backwards to the beat of a drum. It is still performed on the last day of the Sun Dance.

Legacy

The term wendigo psychosis describes a culture-bound syndrome marked by an intense craving for human flesh, although its validity is debated among researchers. In literature, Algernon Blackwood popularised the name with his 1910 novella The Wendigo. Stephen King mentions it in Pet Sematary, and the film Ravenous centres on the creature.

Ancient sources: There is no canonical ancient text: the tradition is oral, and the earliest references come from accounts recorded by missionaries and ethnographers, such as the Jesuit Relations of 1661.

Trivia

A reported case. Swift Runner, a Plains Cree trapper from Alberta, killed and ate his wife and five surviving children in 1878, even though food supplies were nearby. He confessed and was executed.
A name with several origins. The English word partly comes from Cree and partly from Ojibwe, and similar forms appear in English from 1714.
