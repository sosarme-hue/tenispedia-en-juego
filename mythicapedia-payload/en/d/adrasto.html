Phrygian prince in Greek tradition

Adrastus

Adrastus is a Phrygian prince whose story is told by Herodotus in his Histories, in connection with the Lydian king Croesus. His tale shows how an unintended guilt can weigh heavier than any pardon.

The myth

According to the tradition, Adrastus was a son of Gordias, king of Phrygia, and a grandson of Midas. Without meaning to, he killed his brother, and his father drove him out of the kingdom. He fled to Sardis, to the court of Croesus, who purified him and welcomed him generously.

At that time a huge boar was ravaging the lands of the Mysians. Croesus, warned in a dream that his son Atys would die from an iron spearpoint, was reluctant to send him, but Atys persuaded his father, and the king charged Adrastus with guarding the young man during the hunt. Throwing his spears at the beast, Adrastus missed and struck Atys dead. He then begged to be sacrificed over the prince's body, but Croesus refused, blaming the gods' vengeance. Unable to live with another death on his conscience, Adrastus took his own life beside Atys's tomb.

Ancient sources: Herodotus, in Book I of his Histories, tells his story within the account of Croesus.

Trivia

A prophetic dream. Croesus dreamed that his son would die from an iron spearpoint, so he removed all the weapons from his palace.
A son he did not count. Croesus had another son, who was deaf, and whom he did not regard as his own.
