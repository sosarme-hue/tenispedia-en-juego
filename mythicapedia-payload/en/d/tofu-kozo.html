Japanese yōkai resembling a boy carrying a tray of tofu

Tōfu-kozō

The tōfu-kozō is a yōkai from Japanese folklore that takes the form of a child holding a tray of tofu. It emerged in popular Edo-period literature and matters because it shows how urban folklore blended with everyday life and food culture.

The myth

According to popular stories, the tōfu-kozō usually appears as a child carrying a round tray with a tofu pressed into the shape of a maple leaf (momiji-dōfu). It wears bamboo and a kasa hat, and its clothing bears charms against smallpox, such as harukoma, daruma dolls and red fish.

In Edo-period books it has no special powers. It appears as a servant who brings tofu and sake around town. Sometimes it follows people on rainy nights, but it does them no harm. It is shy, friendly and often comic, and even other yōkai tease it for being weak. Later literature says it offers tofu to passersby, which grows mold halfway through eating.

Legacy

It appears in kusazōshi, kibyōshi and kaidan books, and on toys such as kites, sugoroku and karuta cards. A statue of it stands on the Mizuki Shigeru Road in Sakaiminato, the hometown of manga artist Shigeru Mizuki.

Ancient sources: Its earliest known appearances are in Edo-period works from the An'ei era (1772–1781), such as the Bakemono Shiuchi Hyōbanki.

Trivia

Possible origin. Some scholars believe it was created by the tofu trade in the Edo period, perhaps as an advertising character.
Large head. Many works emphasize its large head, a trait it shares with the kozō, the boy monks of the period's literature.
