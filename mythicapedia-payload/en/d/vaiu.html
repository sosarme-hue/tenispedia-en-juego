Hindu god of the wind and the breath of life

Vayu

Vayu (vāyu, ‘the blower’) is the Hindu god of the wind and a divine messenger of the gods. His figure appears from the Vedas to the Upanishads, and he is also linked to the vital breath, prāna, which sustains life.

The myth

In the Rig Veda, Vayu is closely associated with Indra, and the two share the first drink of soma. The hymns describe him as a fierce and heroic figure who rides a shining chariot pulled by white and purple horses, and the Maruts, the storm deities, are said to have been born from his belly.

In the Upanishads, especially the Brihadaranyaka, the gods who control the bodily functions compete to see which of them is the greatest. When each deity leaves a man's body, he goes on living, though impaired, but when Mukhya Prana, the chief vital breath, begins to depart, the others are pulled from their posts like a strong horse tearing up the pegs it is tied to. They realise that they can act only through the power of Vayu.

In the epics he is the father of Hanuman, the loyal monkey helper of Rama, and of Bhima, the mighty Pandava of the Mahabharata, born of his union with Queen Kunti. In later tradition he is one of the dikpalas, the guardians of the directions, and watches over the northwest. Followers of the thirteenth-century teacher Madhva regard Madhva as an incarnation of Vayu, though this identification belongs to his sectarian tradition, and some of the texts cited for it have been questioned by modern scholars.

Worship and religion

The sources do not describe temples dedicated to Vayu. His worship is understood mainly through the Vedic hymns and his place in ritual. He belongs to the five great elements of Hinduism, the pancha maha bhuta, alongside earth, water, fire and space. In East Asian Buddhism he is venerated as a guardian of the directions: in China as Fengtian, among the Twenty-Four Protective Deities, and in Japan as Fūten, among the Twelve Devas of the esoteric Mikkyō traditions, presiding over the northwest.

Legacy

The name Pavan, from Pavana, remains a fairly common personal name in India. The word vātāvaraṇam, meaning 'atmosphere' in Sanskrit and Hindi, comes from Vata, one of his alternative names. In China his image appears in statues of protective devas, such as those at the Shanhua and Tiefo temples in Shanxi.

Ancient sources: The Rig Veda names him as god of the winds and companion of Indra; the Upanishads present him as Prana, and the epics tell of his fatherhood of Hanuman and Bhima.

Trivia

A god who sustains the others. In the Brihadaranyaka story, the other deities can function only while Vayu empowers them, which is why he is seen as the breath that keeps life going.
Son of the wind. Hanuman is also called Pavanaputra and Vayuputra, both meaning son of the wind.
