queen of Iolcus in Greek myth

Astydamia

Astydamia is the queen of Iolcus, in Thessaly, and the wife of King Acastus. Her story, preserved by Pindar and Apollodorus, combines a rejected seduction, a false accusation and a war that ends with the destruction of her city.

The myth

According to the tradition, Acastus purified Peleus after the death of Eurytion, but Astydamia fell in love with her guest and, rejected, wrote a forged letter to Antigone, Peleus's wife, claiming he was about to marry Sterope. Antigone hanged herself. Astydamia then accused Peleus of trying to assault her. Acastus, unwilling to kill him himself, took him hunting, hid his sword and left him among centaurs. Chiron returned the weapon and Peleus escaped.

Years later, Peleus came back to Iolcus with Jason and the Dioscuri, sacked the city and killed Astydamia, cutting her body into pieces before marching his army between the remains. According to Pindar, however, it was Hephaestus who gave Peleus a knife to defend himself.

Ancient sources: Pindar, in his Nemean Odes IV and V (c. 483-473 BC), and Apollodorus, who gives the most detailed version; a scholiast on Aristophanes also records the story.

Trivia

A widespread folk pattern. The story follows a pattern found widely in folklore: a seductress who, after being rejected, falsely accuses the man she desired, as in the biblical story of Joseph and Potiphar's wife.
A possible purification rite. The army marching between Astydamia's remains may reflect a rite of sacrifice or purification. In Boeotia and Macedonia there were rites in which a dog was cut in two and people walked between the halves.
