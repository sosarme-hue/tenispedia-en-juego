Greek prophetess linked to Phrygia in medieval and Renaissance tradition

Phrygian Sibyl

The Phrygian Sibyl is a prophetess of Greek tradition who, in the Gothic and Renaissance imagination, was counted among the sibyls who foretold the end of time. Her figure was tied to an Apollonian oracle in Phrygia, a region in the west central Anatolian highlands.

The myth

Popular tradition identified her with Cassandra, the prophetess daughter of Priam in Homer's Iliad, whose warnings went unheeded. In this version, she presided over an oracle dedicated to Apollo in Phrygia. Modern scholars note that the only oracular site documented in the area was at Gergitis, and that the figure may be a doublet of the Hellespontine Sibyl, with whom she is often grouped in a trio alongside the Erythraean Sibyl.

Worship and religion

The only oracular site documented in Phrygia was at Gergitis, so her cult seems more literary than religious.

Legacy

In Christian Europe she was one of the sibyls who prefigured the Last Judgment. Lactantius, in the 4th century, raised their number to ten, quoting a lost work by Varro. In 1575, the engraver Philip Galle depicted her in a series of ten copperplates, with verses by Aria Montano based on the Tuba mirum.

Ancient sources: Homer's Iliad, where Cassandra appears, the figure she was identified with; the list of sibyls is preserved by Varro, through Lactantius.
