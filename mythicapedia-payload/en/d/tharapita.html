god of war in Estonian mythology

Tharapita

Tharapita, also known as Taara or Taarapita, is a god of Estonian mythology whom the Chronicle of Henry of Livonia describes as the chief deity of the Oeselians of Saaremaa. Often compared to the Germanic Thor, he was reinterpreted with real force in modern times.

The myth

According to the Chronicle of Henry of Livonia, the people of Vironia believed Tharapita had been born on a beautiful wooded hill and had flown from there to Saaremaa. When the crusaders arrived in 1220, missionaries felled the images of the gods on that hill, and locals were amazed that the statues did not bleed.

The chronicle also tells how, during the siege of the Muhu hill fort, the defenders called on Tharapita while the Christians called on Jesus. After the victory, priests baptised the people of Saaremaa and drove Tharapita out of the island.

Worship and religion

The chronicle notes that the Tavastians of Finland worshipped Taara at an old cult site now called Laurin Lähde, until church authorities shut it down. Among Estonians of the western coast, the so-called Tooru nights were held, when people gathered in the sacred grove on Thursdays.

Legacy

In the nineteenth century the Estonian national movement made Taara the supreme god of a romantic mythology. Tartu, the country's second city, was poetically called Taaralinn. A modern theory links his flight to the impact that formed Lake Kaali, and today a neopagan movement venerates him.

Ancient sources: The Chronicle of Henry of Livonia is the main medieval source that mentions him.
