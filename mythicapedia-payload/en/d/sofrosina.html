Greek personification of moderation and self-control

Sophrosyne

Sophrosyne (Greek Σωφροσύνη, Sōphrosýnē) is the personification of moderation, temperance and self-control in Greek mythology. It was also a central virtue in Greek ethics, and its influence reached deep into Western philosophy.

The myth

According to the poet Theognis of Megara, Sophrosyne was one of the minor powers that escaped from Pandora's jar. In his lament, he says that among the gods who remained with mankind only Hope is left, while Trust, Restraint and the Graces have departed the earth.

Roman tradition identified her with Continentia, and some sources describe her as a daughter of Erebus and Night, although Greek authors never fixed a single genealogy. In The Golden Ass, Apuleius casts her as an enemy of Aphrodite, goddess of passion and lust. In Euripides' Hippolytus, sophrosyne is embodied both by the goddess Artemis and by the young hero himself.

Legacy

Plato made sophrosyne the subject of his dialogue Charmides and linked it to the Pythagorean idea of harmony. Aristotle studied it as a mean between excess and deficiency in pleasures, and the Stoics counted it among their chief virtues. Thomas Aquinas later named it the fourth and final cardinal virtue, shaping the Western idea of temperance.

Ancient sources: Theognis of Megara mentions her among the daimones released from Pandora's jar, and Euripides explores her in his Hippolytus.

Trivia

A Homeric remedy. In the Odyssey, Odysseus resists Circe's enchantments thanks to the herb moly, which some interpretations link to sophrosyne.
A virtue with four Latin names. Cicero used four Latin terms to translate sophrosyne: temperantia, moderatio, verecundia and frugalitas.
