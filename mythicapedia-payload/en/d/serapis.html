Graeco-Egyptian god of the underworld and fertility

Serapis

Serapis is a syncretic god of the Graeco-Egyptian world, born from the merging of the cults of Osiris and the sacred bull Apis and dressed in Greek form. Ptolemy I promoted his worship to unite Greek and Egyptian subjects, and it spread across the Mediterranean.

The myth

According to the traditions, the god's origin is linked to the bull Apis. His Egyptian name, Userhapi ("Osiris-Apis"), gave rise to the Greek form Sarapis. He was regarded as Osiris in full, not just his ka or life force. The name's earliest mention is tied to a disputed account of Alexander the Great's death.

The best-known legend tells that Ptolemy I stole a statue from Sinope, on the Black Sea, after receiving a dream instruction to bring it to Alexandria. There two experts, the Egyptian priest Manetho and Timotheus of the Eumolpidae, identified it as Serapis. Plutarch transmits this story, although some scholars think the Sinope tale is a distortion of Sinopeion, the hill of the Memphis Serapeum.

The statue showed the god enthroned, wearing the modius, a grain basket and symbol of the underworld, and holding a sceptre, with Cerberus and a serpent at his feet. With Isis and Harpocrates he formed a triad. Some sources also credit him with traits of Dionysus, Hades and Demeter.

Worship and religion

The most famous sanctuary was the Serapeum of Alexandria; there were also serapea at Corinth and in Boeotia, and in Rome he was worshipped in the Iseum Campense. His cult grew popular in the West, and some emperors, such as Vespasian, linked it to miraculous events. It declined under the religious laws of Theodosius I, and the Alexandrian Serapeum was destroyed around 391 CE by a Christian mob. No living cult survives.

Legacy

Serapis appears in many sculptures, imperial coins and oil lamps. Jewish and Christian writers of antiquity debated his origins.

Ancient sources: Plutarch and Arrian mention a temple of Serapis in the 4th century BCE; Tacitus and Pausanias also name him.

In art

Trivia

A god on coins. From the Flavian dynasty on, Serapis appeared on imperial coinage alongside the reigning emperor.
An Egyptian Cerberus. Anubis was sometimes identified with Cerberus, the Greek guardian of the underworld who rests at the feet of the Serapis statue.
