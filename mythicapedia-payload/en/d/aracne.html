mortal weaver turned into a spider in Greek and Roman myth

Arachne

Arachne is a mortal weaver in Greco-Roman mythology, daughter of the dyer Idmon, who challenged the goddess Athena (Minerva in Rome) to a weaving contest. Her story, told mainly by Ovid, explains the origin of spiders and warns against hubris.

The myth

According to the myth, Arachne was a Lydian maiden, daughter of Idmon of Colophon, a famous dyer of Tyrian purple. She was credited with inventing linen and nets, and her son Closter introduced the spindle into wool-making. In Ovid's Metamorphoses she is a shepherd's daughter who began weaving as a child and came to boast that she surpassed Minerva, refusing to admit that part of her skill came from the goddess.

Disguised as an old woman, Minerva advised her to beg the goddess for forgiveness, but Arachne insulted her and dared her to show her power in person. When the goddess revealed herself, the contest began. Minerva's tapestry showed mortals punished for presuming to equal the gods; Arachne's showed Jupiter and other gods deceiving and abusing women. Seeing her rival's flawless work, Minerva tore the tapestry apart and struck Arachne with her shuttle. Ashamed, Arachne hanged herself. Moved to pity, the goddess turned her into a spider, condemned to spin forever.

According to Ovid's telling, the tale explained why spiders can weave webs, and it served as a warning about pride. Some modern scholars have read it as an allegory of art and power: in Augustus's time, when Ovid was writing and was exiled, weaving was a common metaphor for poetry.

Legacy

Edmund Spenser adapted the episode in his mock-heroic poem Muiopotmos, where Arachne's descendant Aragnoll hates the butterfly Clarion. Dante mentions her in Canto XVII of the Inferno to describe the painted skin of the monster Geryon, whose patterns were richer than any Arachne ever wove.

Ancient sources: Ovid's Metamorphoses (Book VI) is the fullest version; Virgil, Pliny the Elder, Lucian and the Vatican Mythographers also mention her.

Trivia

A son named «spindle». Pliny the Elder records that Arachne had a son, Closter, whose name means «spindle» in Greek, and that he introduced the spindle into wool-making.
A version with a brother. In a rarer variant, Arachne is a girl from Attica taught weaving by Athena, while her brother Phalanx learns martial arts; after they commit incest, the goddess turns both into spiders.
