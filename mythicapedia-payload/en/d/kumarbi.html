chief god of the Hurrians, father of the gods

Kumarbi

Kumarbi was the chief god of the Hurrian pantheon, whom the texts call the father of the gods. He is the central figure of the so-called Kumarbi Cycle, in which he schemes to overthrow his son, the storm god Teshub.

The myth

According to the Hittite tradition preserved in the Kingship in Heaven, Anu overthrew the first celestial king, Alalu. Kumarbi served Anu for nine years before defeating him. As Anu tried to flee into the sky, Kumarbi caught him and bit off his genitals; part of Anu's virility entered Kumarbi's body, and the gods born from it would later cause him trouble, Teshub among them, who eventually plotted against his own father.

In the Song of Ullikummi, Kumarbi fathers a diorite giant from a rock in order to defeat Teshub. The monster grows until it touches the sky, but Ea cuts off its feet with a copper knife and it is brought down.

Worship and religion

Worship of him is attested across all the areas inhabited by the Hurrians, from Anatolia to the Zagros Mountains, although his ritual importance seems to have been modest. He was identified with the god Dagan and, from the sixteenth century BCE, with Enlil. He appears in texts from Mari, Ugarit and Alalakh, was absorbed into the Hittite pantheon, and is depicted among the gods at the Yazılıkaya sanctuary. A link with agriculture has been proposed, but the evidence is limited.

Legacy

The stories of his cycle have been compared with the tale of Uranus, Kronos and Zeus, and are commonly thought to have influenced Hesiod's Theogony, especially the character of Kronos. Influences have also been suggested on the Phoenician History of Philo of Byblos and on Orphic theogonies such as that of the Derveni papyrus.

Ancient sources: Hittite tablets such as the Kingship in Heaven and the Song of Ullikummi are his main sources, alongside Hurrian and Ugaritic texts.

Trivia

A name with meaning. His Hurrian name is usually translated as he of Kumar, possibly an early settlement that is poorly documented in the texts.
Written with the grain sign. In some texts his name is written with the logogram of the grain goddess Nisaba, perhaps as a scribal wordplay.
