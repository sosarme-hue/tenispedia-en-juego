Clytemnestra

Clytemnestra is, in Greek mythology, the wife of Agamemnon, king of Mycenae, and one of the most famous female figures of tragedy. Daughter of Tyndareus and Leda, she goes from queen and mother to the murderer of her husband after the sacrifice of their daughter Iphigenia.

The myth

According to tradition, Clytemnestra was a sister of Helen, Castor and Pollux, though ancient sources do not agree on her origin. Homer and the tragedians present her as a daughter of Tyndareus and Leda, while other versions link her to Zeus through the same Leda. Before marrying Agamemnon, she had been married to Tantalus, whom the king of Mycenae killed so he could take her as his wife.

When the Greek fleet was stuck at the port of Aulis with no favourable winds, the seer Calchas announced that Artemis demanded the sacrifice of Iphigenia. Agamemnon summoned his daughter on the pretext of marrying her to Achilles. In the best-known version, Artemis replaced the girl with a deer and carried her off to Tauris; in Sophocles' account, the sacrifice did take place.

During the long Trojan War, Clytemnestra began an affair with Aegisthus, Agamemnon's cousin. When the king returned with the Trojan princess Cassandra, the pair killed him. In Aeschylus' Agamemnon, she traps him in a net and stabs him in his bath, and Cassandra also dies. Aegisthus then ruled Mycenae alongside the queen, and they had children.

Years later, Orestes came back to avenge his father, aided by his sister Electra, and killed his mother and Aegisthus. As she died, Clytemnestra cursed him, and the young man was pursued by the Erinyes until he was freed from their torment.

Legacy

Clytemnestra is above all a tragic figure. Aeschylus gave her her largest role in the Oresteia, Sophocles showed her through the eyes of her daughter Electra in his play of that name, and Euripides portrayed her as a desperate mother in Iphigenia at Aulis. The writer Madeline Miller has called her, after Medea, one of the most notorious women in Greek mythology. Her name, read as famed for her suitors or as a schemer, reflects the ambiguity of her character.

Ancient sources: She appears in the Odyssey, where Agamemnon's ghost accuses her of his murder, and more discreetly in the Iliad; also in Aeschylus' Agamemnon, Libation Bearers and Eumenides, in Sophocles' Electra, in Euripides' Iphigenia at Aulis, and in the Catalogue of Women attributed to Hesiod.

Trivia

A misread name. Her name is often glossed as famed for her suitors, but this is a late reading based on a false link to the verb meaning to woo. Aeschylus played with another sense, that of a schemer.
Helen's twin?. She is often presented today as Helen's twin, but ancient sources do not support this. The first to describe all four children born from two eggs is the First Vatican Mythographer.
Letters that stirred betrayal. According to some versions, Nauplius travelled across Greece telling several queens that their husbands were taking concubines during the war, which supposedly made them prone to adultery.
