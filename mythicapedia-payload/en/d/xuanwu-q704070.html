Chinese god of the north, water and winter

Xuanwu

Xuanwu is one of the most venerated deities in Chinese traditional religion and one of the highest-ranking gods in Taoism. Linked to the north, water and winter, he is seen as a guardian against evil and disaster, and he is especially honoured by martial artists.

The myth

According to one tradition, Xuanwu was originally a prince of the Jingle Kingdom in northern Hebei, in the time of the Yellow Emperor. As he grew up he felt the sorrow of ordinary people and chose to withdraw to a remote mountain to cultivate the Tao, spending forty-two years there until he attained immortality.

A later version, recorded during the Qing dynasty, makes him a butcher who had killed many animals without mercy. Repenting, he gave up the trade and retreated to the mountains. After helping a woman in childbirth, he washed his blood-stained clothes in a river, and the title Dark Heavenly Highest Deity appeared before him. The woman was a manifestation of Guanyin, who was testing his resolve. To atone for his sins he took out his stomach and intestines and washed them in the river, which turned dark and murky before running clear again. The Jade Emperor made him an immortal. His discarded viscera became a demonic turtle and snake that harmed people, until Xuanwu returned to subdue them and made them his servants.

He is sometimes shown with two generals, Wan Gong and Wan Ma. One legend says he holds a sword he borrowed from Lü Dongbin to defeat a demon and then refused to return; the sword would fly back to its owner if released, so he grips it tightly.

Worship and religion

His main cult centre is the Wudang Mountains in Hubei, where according to tradition he attained immortality. The Ming emperors, especially Yongle, sponsored a vast complex of Taoist temples there after the Jingnan Campaign. His birthday is celebrated on the third day of the third lunar month. Migration carried his worship south, and it survives today in Taiwan, Southeast Asia and the overseas Chinese diaspora; in Thailand, the shrine in Bangkok is much visited during Chinese New Year.

Legacy

He appears in the classic novel Journey to the West as a northern king with two generals, a General Tortoise and a General Snake. In Wuhan, a Tortoise Mountain and a Snake Mountain face each other across a river, linked to his figure.

Ancient sources: The earliest references present him as a cosmological symbol of the northern constellation in Han dynasty texts, before he became a personified deity during the Song dynasty.
