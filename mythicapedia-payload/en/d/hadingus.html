legendary king of Denmark

Hadingus

Hadingus, also Hading, is one of the earliest legendary kings of Denmark in Scandinavian tradition. His story, told in detail by Saxo Grammaticus, blends the figure of a warrior hero with elements of Norse mythology.

The myth

According to Saxo Grammaticus in the Gesta Danorum, Hadingus was the son of King Gram and Signe, daughter of the Finnish king Sumble. Gram seized Signe from her wedding to the king of the Saxons, whom he killed. After Gram was slain by the Norwegian king Svipdag, the boy was raised in Sweden by the giant Wagnofthus and his daughter Harthgrepa, who tried to steer him away from war toward a quasi-incestuous love affair.

After Harthgrepa was killed by supernatural beings, Odin became his patron, foretelling his future and giving him counsel. Hadingus became a great warrior in the Baltic, defeated Suibdagerus, his father's killer, and ruled Denmark. He offended the gods by killing a sacred animal, but atoned with a sacrifice to Freyr. He rescued the princess Regnilda from giants and married her, visited the underworld, and died by hanging himself before his subjects.

Legacy

The story of Hadingus is retold in fictionalized form in Poul Anderson's novel War of the Gods. Georges Dumézil and others have argued that the character was partly modelled on the god Njörðr.

Ancient sources: Saxo Grammaticus narrates his story in the Gesta Danorum, and his name appears briefly in the Codex Runicus.

Trivia

Runic mention. Hading appears in the Runekrønike section of the Codex Runicus as the father of King Froþe, although the fragment is incomplete.
