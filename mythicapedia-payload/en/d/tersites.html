Achaean soldier in the Trojan War

Thersites

Thersites is a soldier of the Greek army in the Trojan War, remembered as the ugliest man at Troy and as the one who dares to voice what others think about the leaders. He matters because Homer uses him as a critical counterpoint within the Iliad.

The myth

Homer describes him in Book II of the Iliad as the ugliest of the Greeks: bow-legged, lame, with hunched shoulders and a pointed head covered in tufts of hair. His father is not named there, which suggests he was no aristocratic hero. The lost epic Aethiopis, and later the Bibliotheca, make him a son of Agrius.

In the assembly, Thersites attacks Agamemnon as greedy and urges the Greeks to abandon the war and sail home. Odysseus beats him with Agamemnon's sceptre, and the army, far from pitying him, laughs. He does not appear again in the Iliad.

According to the Aethiopis, after Achilles killed Penthesilea, queen of the Amazons, Thersites tore out her eyes. Achilles killed him for mocking his grief at her beauty. Quintus Smyrnaeus adds that the death enraged Diomedes, Thersites' cousin, and that Achilles had to be purified after Odysseus cleared him of the murder.

Legacy

Shakespeare casts him in Troilus and Cressida as a sharp-tongued servant, first of Ajax and then of Achilles, and Goethe has him appear briefly in Faust. Stefan Zweig wrote a play about him in 1907. Hegel, Nietzsche and Edward Said cited him as a figure of social criticism, and left-wing literature has treated him as a forerunner. Plato mentions him in The Republic, where he chooses to be reborn as an ape.

Ancient sources: Homer describes him in the Iliad (Book II); the lost Aethiopis and the Bibliotheca give him a father, and Quintus Smyrnaeus recounts his death at Achilles' hands.

Trivia

A medical eponym. His deformity has given its name to a medical term, according to the sources.
Ajax's slave. In Shakespeare he begins as a mangy slave of Ajax and soon moves into the service of Achilles, who values his caustic humour.
