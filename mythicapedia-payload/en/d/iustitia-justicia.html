Roman goddess of justice

Iustitia (Lady Justice)

Iustitia, or Lady Justice, is the Roman goddess of justice, equivalent to the Greek Dike and heir to earlier images such as Maat in Egypt. Her figure holding scales, a sword and sometimes a blindfold is one of the most recognisable symbols of modern judicial systems.

The myth

In Greek tradition, Dike was a daughter of Themis, the personification of divine order, law and good custom. A fragment of Bacchylides shows her holding the scales, and the image passed to Rome, where Justitia became a central figure of public virtue. Roman authors offer no independent myth for Iustitia; she is mainly a personified virtue.

The scales have an even older origin. In the Egyptian Book of the Dead, the god Anubis weighed the heart of the deceased against the feather of truth of the goddess Maat. A light heart allowed entry to the afterlife; otherwise the deceased was devoured by Ammit, a crocodile-headed monster.

The sword stood for authority and for a justice that could be swift and final. The blindfold, by contrast, is not ancient: it appeared in the sixteenth century, first as a satire showing Justice blind to injustice, and was later reinterpreted as a symbol of impartiality.

Worship and religion

Augustus included justice among the virtues he celebrated, and Tiberius established a temple of Justitia in Rome. Emperors used her image to proclaim themselves protectors of justice; Vespasian minted coins showing the goddess seated under the name Iustitia Augusta. Although she had a temple, she seems to have been viewed more as a symbolic personification than as a deity with real religious worship.

Legacy

Her image remains on courthouses across the world, from London and Bern to Tehran and Brasilia. She appears in paintings by Raphael, Luca Giordano, Lucas Cranach and Carl Spitzweg, and in coats of arms of legal institutions. The scales symbol also has a place in Unicode, registered as U+2696. In 2024 the Supreme Court of India unveiled Nyay Devi, a new statue without a blindfold and with a book in place of the sword.

Trivia

A statue without a blindfold. Atop London's Old Bailey, the statue of Lady Justice has no blindfold, since her maidenly form is considered enough to guarantee impartiality.
The newer blindfold. The earliest Roman coins show her with uncovered eyes. The first known blindfolded Justice is Hans Gieng's 1543 statue in Bern.
