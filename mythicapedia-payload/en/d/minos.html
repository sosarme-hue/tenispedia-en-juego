king of Crete in Greek mythology

Minos

Minos was a king of Crete and son of Zeus and Europa, remembered in Greek tradition as a lawgiver and the founder of Crete's naval power, while Athenian stories portray him as a ruthless tyrant. His name gave rise to the term "Minoan civilization", coined by archaeologist Arthur Evans for the Bronze Age culture of Crete.

The myth

According to the most common version, Zeus, smitten with Europa, daughter of Phoenix, took the form of a gentle bull and carried her across the sea from Phoenicia to Crete. There she bore Minos, Rhadamanthus and Sarpedon. Asterion, prince of the Cretans, married Europa and raised her sons. Other accounts differ, and Herodotus says the rivalry between Minos and Sarpedon was over the throne rather than a young man.

Minos ruled Crete, gave it laws and was the first Greek to command the sea with a substantial fleet. The tradition says he lived at Knossos and every nine years withdrew to a sacred cave to receive from Zeus the rules he would govern by. He married Pasiphaë, and their children included Ariadne, Phaedra, Androgeus and Glaucus.

In the Athenian story, his son Androgeus died after competing in Athens, and Minos demanded that seven youths and seven maidens be sent every nine years as tribute to feed the Minotaur, born of Pasiphaë and a bull Poseidon had sent. Daedalus built the labyrinth where the creature was confined, and Theseus killed it with Ariadne's help. In another episode, Minos ordered Polyidus to revive his son Glaucus, who had drowned in a cask of honey, and the story ends with Polyidus teaching Glaucus and then losing what he had taught him.

Legacy

The name of Minos gave rise to the term "Minoan" for the Bronze Age civilization of Crete. In later tradition Minos appears as a judge of the dead alongside Rhadamanthus and Aeacus.

Ancient sources: Homer mentions him as king of Knossos in the Iliad and the Odyssey, and Thucydides records his role in building one of the earliest navies.
