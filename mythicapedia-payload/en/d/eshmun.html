Phoenician god of healing and patron of Sidon

Eshmun

Eshmun was one of the main gods of the Phoenician pantheon, linked to healing and health, and the tutelary god of the city of Sidon, in present-day Lebanon. Greeks and Romans identified him with Asclepius and Aesculapius.

The myth

According to the Phoenician tradition transmitted by Sanchuniathon, Eshmun was the son of the god Sydyk, whose name means 'Righteousness'. Sydyk first fathered seven sons and then an eighth, Eshmun, by one of the Titanides. His name appears to mean 'the Eighth', which explains his place in the family.

The Neoplatonist Damascius records a different version in which Asclepius of Beirut was a young hunter pursued relentlessly by the goddess Astronoe, often identified with Astarte. In despair the youth castrated himself and died. The goddess restored him to life with the warmth of her body, named him Paeon, 'Healer', and made him a god.

Pausanias reports that the Phoenicians regarded Apollo as the father of Asclepius, though not the son of a mortal mother, a idea that seems to be a late elaboration.

Worship and religion

The main temple of Eshmun stood about a kilometre from Sidon, on the Bostrenus river (now the Awali). Construction began at the end of the sixth century BCE and additions continued into the Roman period. Many votive offerings were found there, mainly statues of people healed by the god, especially babies and young children. Rituals included ablutions and dances, and games were held in his honour in which the winner received a purple cloth. He also had temples at Tyre and Carthage, the latter destroyed by the Romans in the Third Punic War.

Legacy

In Cyprus he was merged with Melqart, and an dedication in Ibiza is addressed to 'Eshmun-Melqart'. The name astresmunim, 'herb of Eshmun', was given by Dioscorides to a medicinal solanum plant. A village near Beirut, Qabr Shmoun, still keeps a name meaning 'Eshmun's grave'.

Ancient sources: Sanchuniathon, transmitted by Eusebius of Caesarea; Damascius, Pausanias and Photius, as well as a trilingual Sardinian inscription of the 2nd century BCE.

Trivia

A statue for every cure. Many votive statues of people healed by the god were found near the temple, especially babies and young children.
A village with his name. Qabr Shmoun, near Beirut, means 'Eshmun's grave' and still exists.
