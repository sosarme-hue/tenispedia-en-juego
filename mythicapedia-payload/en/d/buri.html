primordial god of Norse mythology

Búri

Búri is, according to Norse tradition, the first deity to emerge at the beginning of the world, freed from the ice by a primeval cow. He is considered the father of Borr and, depending on how the genealogy is read, the grandfather or father of Odin.

The myth

According to the tale, Búri emerged in the primordial void of Ginnungagap, between ice and fire. Over three days, the primeval cow Auðumbla licked salty rime stones until Búri was freed from them. The sources say nothing about how he existed before that moment.

His descendants are the most debated point. Some sources make him the father of Borr, but they never explain how that son was begotten, whether by himself or through sexual reproduction. Scholars debate whether Borr and Búri are really the same generational figure. If they are not, Búri was Odin's grandfather through Borr; if they are, he was Odin's father.

His name offers a clue. Búri and Borr both derive from Proto-Germanic *buriz, 'son, born', so the two names basically mean the same thing. Research often reads Búri as 'begotten, father' and Borr as 'begotten, son', a difference probably tied to the generational sequence.

The main source is the Prose Edda, written in the 13th century by Snorri Sturluson. He appears in the Gylfaginning account of origins, and in the Skáldskaparmál Snorri quotes a verse by the 12th-century skald Þórvaldr Blönduskáld. The Poetic Edda never mentions him, so almost everything known about him comes from Snorri.

Ancient sources: The Prose Edda of Snorri Sturluson (Gylfaginning and Skáldskaparmál), which includes a verse by Þórvaldr Blönduskáld; he does not appear in the Poetic Edda.

Trivia

Shared name. Búri and his supposed son Borr have names derived from the same Proto-Germanic root, meaning roughly 'son' or 'born'.
A single mention in poetry. His name appears only once in the skaldic corpus, quoted by Snorri in the Skáldskaparmál.
