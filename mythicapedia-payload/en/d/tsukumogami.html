household objects that come alive in Japanese folklore

Tsukumogami

Tsukumogami are everyday tools that, in Japanese tradition, gain a spirit (kami) after a hundred years, or ninety-nine in another reading of the name. They form a class of yōkai and obake that grew largely through Edo-period imagery.

The myth

The word appears in The Tales of Ise, in a 9th-century poem where it describes an old woman's white hair. The idea of a spirit after a hundred years, and the spelling with the characters for ninety-nine, come from later sources such as the Tsukumogami Emaki, which shows objects transforming into monsters that take revenge on those who discard them carelessly.

According to these sources, old objects could take human, oni or animal forms, and in some tales they banded together to punish owners who threw them away. To prevent this, households cleaned their homes at year's end and got rid of old items before they reached that age. Accounts are scarce, and many of the named examples come from the imagination of artists such as Toriyama Sekien.

Worship and religion

Some texts note that in the tenth century the tsukumogami idea was used to spread Shingon Esoteric Buddhism to a wide range of audiences. Today some Shinto shrines hold ceremonies to console and send off broken or unusable objects.

Legacy

Tsukumogami have inspired woodblock prints and paintings, and have entered Japanese popular culture as mischievous but mostly harmless beings. One example is the biwa-bokuboku, a lute turned spirit that has appeared in many artworks.

Ancient sources: The Tales of Ise (9th century) mentions the word in a poem, and the Edo-period Tsukumogami Emaki records the idea of objects coming alive.

Trivia

A tea with a soul. According to Elison and Smith, Tsukumogami was the name of an animated tea caddy that Matsunaga Hisahide used to bargain for peace with Oda Nobunaga.
A symbolic number. The number one hundred is not meant literally: it stands for the idea that very old things acquire a spiritual nature.
