popular Hindu goddess and protector of children

Shashthi

Shashthi is a folk goddess of Hinduism, venerated especially in eastern India and Nepal as the protector of children and patron of fertility and vegetation. Her name means "sixth", referring to the sixth day of the lunar month and the sixth day after a child's birth.

The myth

According to tradition, Shashthi grew out of ancient folk beliefs, which over the centuries merged with Vedic figures such as Sinivali-Kuhu, goddess of the new moon, and Shri-Lakshmi. Her relationship with Skanda, the war god, is the core of her stories.

In early texts she appears as a foster mother of Skanda. The Mahabharata presents her as an attendant of Skanda who causes disease in mothers and children, and in that text she is given the epithet Revati. Later she was identified with Devasena, Skanda's consort, daughter of Prajapati, whom the king of the gods, Indra, betrothed to him. A fifth-century text calls her the sixth form of Skanda. Over time she shifted from a threat to a benefactor of infancy.

She is depicted as a young woman with golden skin, often holding as many as eight infants, and riding a cat. In Kushan coins and sculptures of the early centuries CE she appears with six heads, like Skanda.

Worship and religion

She is invoked on the sixth day of each lunar month and on the sixth day after a child is born. Women who wish to conceive and mothers seeking protection for their children ask her for blessings. She is represented by a red stone beneath a banyan tree, an earthen pitcher, or a branch planted in the family garden, and outdoor spaces are consecrated to her. Her worship is especially strong in eastern India. Her major festival is Chhath, lasting four days, with holy bathing, fasting, standing in water, and offerings of arghya to the setting and rising sun. This worship continues today.

Ancient sources: The Mahabharata, the Yajnavalkya Smriti, the Kashyapa Samhita, the Manava Grhya Sutra, the Vayu Purana and the Padma Purana, among other Hindu texts.

Trivia

Six-headed coins. Numerous Kushan and Yaudheya coins, sculptures and inscriptions, produced from 500 BCE to 1200 CE, show Shashthi with six heads, often on the reverse, with six-headed Skanda on the obverse.
A four-day festival. Chhath is celebrated six days after Deepavali, on the sixth day of the lunar month of Kartika, and its rituals span four days.
