Roman god of boundary markers

Terminus

Terminus was the Roman god of boundary markers, protecting the stones that divided private estates and state territory. His sacred stone was believed to guard the order and stability of the community.

The myth

According to tradition, the worship of Terminus reached Rome during the reign of the early kings. Plutarch and other writers credit Numa Pompilius, who wanted to prevent violent disputes over land and ordered the markers consecrated. Other authors link the cult to Titus Tatius, the Sabine colleague of Romulus, and ancient writers generally regarded the god as Sabine in origin.

In the stories, Terminus has little narrative of his own. He is essentially a stone or pillar, later shown with a human head. Dionysius of Halicarnassus and Livy recount that when the altars on the Capitoline were cleared for the great temple of Jupiter, Terminus refused to be moved, so his stone was enclosed within the building. His immovability was taken as a good omen for the permanence of Rome's boundaries.

Worship and religion

Each year on 23 February the Terminalia was held where the Via Ostiensis and Via Laurentina met, an old limit of early Rome. Landowners garlanded their boundary stones, offered crops, honeycombs and wine, and sacrificed a lamb or pig, its blood poured over the marker, followed by a shared feast and hymns. Public rites took place as well. In the Capitoline temple, a small hole in the roof left the stone open to the sky. The cult has no living continuity today.

Legacy

The god's name is the Latin word for a boundary stone, the root of the English word terminus. Georges Dumézil placed him in a proto-Indo-European triad with Jupiter and Juventas, and in 303 AD Diocletian began his persecution of Christians on 23 February, seen as an attempt to set a limit on Christianity's progress.

Ancient sources: Plutarch, Dionysius of Halicarnassus, Varro, Ovid and Livy mention him, and Festus and Siculus Flaccus describe his rites.

Trivia

A hole in the roof. In the Temple of Jupiter Optimus Maximus, Terminus's stone sat beneath a small opening that let it face the sky.
A stone that would not move. When augurs tried to move the altars on the Capitoline, Terminus refused, and the temple was built around his stone.
Diocletian and 23 February. In 303 AD, Diocletian chose this date to begin his persecution of Christians, read as an attempt to halt their progress.
