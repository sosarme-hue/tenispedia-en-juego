magic ring from Greek philosophy that grants invisibility

Ring of Gyges

The Ring of Gyges is a magic ring that, according to Plato, gave its wearer the power to become invisible at will. It appears in the Republic as an example for asking whether a person would still act justly if no one could see or punish them.

The myth

The best-known version comes from Plato's Republic, where Glaucon tells it as the story of an ancestor of Gyges, a shepherd in the service of the king of Lydia. After an earthquake opened a chasm on the hillside where he was grazing his flock, he climbed down and found a bronze horse holding a corpse larger than a man's, wearing a golden ring. He took the ring and discovered that turning its setting towards his palm made him invisible.

With this advantage he arranged to become one of the king's messengers, seduced the queen and, with her help, murdered the king and seized the throne. Herodotus, by contrast, describes a historical Gyges, a subordinate of King Candaules, who killed him and married the queen, with no mention of any ring. Glaucon uses the tale to argue that no one would be just if they could act unseen. Plato replies that the man who used the ring became a slave to his appetites, while the one who refused it kept control of himself and was happy.

Legacy

The idea of an object that grants invisibility and corrupts its owner has inspired many writers. Cicero retells the story in De Officiis as a thought experiment about immunity from punishment. Rousseau mentions it in his Reveries of the Solitary Walker and wonders what he would do with such a ring. H. G. Wells drew on it for The Invisible Man. In Wagner's opera The Ring of the Nibelung, Alberich's ring likewise corrupts whoever possesses it. Tolkien, though no documentary evidence confirms it, is sometimes linked to this tradition through the One Ring, which grants invisibility and corrupts its bearer.

Ancient sources: Plato recounts it in Book II of the Republic, and Herodotus, in his Histories, describes the historical Gyges, king of Lydia.

Trivia

A real king. Gyges was a real king of Lydia and founder of the Mermnad dynasty. The ring story, however, comes from Plato's imagination.
A link to Tolkien. Tradition suggests Tolkien may have read part of Plato's original Greek text, though no documentary evidence confirms that he drew on it.
