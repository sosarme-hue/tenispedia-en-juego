phantom island of the North Atlantic

Buss Island

Buss Island was a phantom island that appeared for centuries on maps of the North Atlantic, placed between Iceland and the equally legendary Frisland. It matters because it shows how a navigational error could turn into charted land for more than two centuries.

The myth

According to the records, in September 1578, during the third expedition of Martin Frobisher, sailors aboard the Emmanuel, under the command of Richard Newton, reported sighting an island around 57° north. It was named after the type of vessel its discoverers used, the buss.

It is believed that Frobisher mistook Greenland for Frisland and Baffin Island for Greenland, and that optical effects near Greenland, at about 62° north, may have given rise to the supposed discovery. On the ship's return to England, errors in dead reckoning led to its being drawn on the charts.

In 1671, a Thomas Shepard claimed to have explored and mapped it, and it continued to appear on maps. As Atlantic traffic grew, its supposed size shrank and, by 1745, it was clear there was no island at the site; it was renamed the Sunken Land of Buss. Its existence was finally disproved by John Ross in 1818, during his first Arctic expedition.

Trivia

Named after a boat. The island took its name from the buss, a type of small vessel used by its supposed discoverers.
Lingering on charts. The island, or 'site of sunken island', remained on nautical charts into the 19th century.
