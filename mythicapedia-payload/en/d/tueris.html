Egyptian goddess of childbirth and fertility

Taweret

Taweret, also known as Tueris in Greek sources, is the protective goddess of childbirth and fertility in ancient Egyptian religion. Her name means "the great one", a conciliatory form of address used for dangerous deities.

The myth

She is typically shown as a bipedal female hippopotamus with feline features, pendulous human breasts, the limbs and paws of a lion, and the back and tail of a crocodile, a blend of fearsome animals that mirrors her dual role as protector and formidable power.

In the myth of the Eye of Ra, the solar goddess grows angry with her father and retreats to Nubia in the form of a lioness. When she returns to Egypt she takes the shape of a hippopotamus, presumably Taweret, and her arrival brings the Nile flood. This gives her the epithet "Mistress of Pure Water", since bringing life to the living through birth and flood is linked to purifying the dead so they can pass safely into the afterlife.

In the New Kingdom she also appears as a northern constellation in astronomical funerary paintings, almost always beside the foreleg of a bull linked to Seth, god of chaos. Some scholars connect these images with the Great Bear.

Worship and religion

Hippopotamus goddesses, including Ipet, Reret and Hedjet, appear already in the Pyramid Texts of the Old Kingdom, and Taweret's cult grew in importance during the Middle Kingdom. Her amulets and ritual objects, such as ivory wands and children's feeding cups, show her role in child care and household protection, a domestic cult that remained strong into the Roman period. Hippopotamus figures placed in tombs and temples linked her to rebirth after death. At Karnak, the temple dedicated to Ipet was thought to witness the daily birth of the sun god from the hippopotamus goddesses.

Ancient sources: She appears in the Pyramid Texts of the Old Kingdom, on Middle Kingdom ritual objects, and in New Kingdom astronomical funerary paintings, such as those in the tombs of Senenmut and Seti I.
