old woman guarding the Sanzu River in Japanese Buddhist hell

Datsue-ba

Datsue-ba is a fearsome old woman of Japanese Buddhism who guards the Sanzu River, the boundary between life and death. She matters because she embodies the passage of souls to the afterlife in Japanese tradition.

The myth

According to Japanese Buddhist folklore, souls must cross the Sanzu River, but children, who have not gathered enough experience, cannot do so. Datsue-ba strips them of their clothes and orders them to pile stones to climb toward paradise, though demons knock the tower down again and again. Only the bodhisattva Jizō can hide them under his robe and carry them across.

Adult sinners are stripped as well, and her companion Keneō hangs their clothes on a tree so that their weight reveals the gravity of their faults. Each fault then receives its punishment; thieves, for instance, have their fingers broken.

Worship and religion

There is no evidence of a separate cult, but at Zenkoji temple in Nagano she appears on a pilgrimage mandala beside the main gate. During the Kaei era (1848-1854), a statue of her at Shōjuin temple in Edo gained fame for granting wishes.

Legacy

The printmaker Utagawa Kuniyoshi portrayed her in ukiyo-e woodblock prints as a blue-skinned woman in a yellow robe, far from the frightening hag of the texts.

Ancient sources: She appears in the Hokke genki (1040-1044), the Konjaku monogatari shū and the Jizō jūō kyō.

Trivia

Nameless at first. In the earliest eleventh-century tales she appears only as an oni or an old female ogre, with no proper name.
Three crossings. According to the Jūō santanshō, the river has three crossings: shallows, a bridge and strong deep currents, assigned according to the gravity of one's sins.
