La Llorona

La Llorona is a ghost of Hispanic American folklore: the restless soul of a woman who drowned her children and now searches for them at night along rivers, and whose wail warns those who hear it of misfortune. Her figure blends Mesoamerican roots with colonial history, so countless versions of her story exist.

The myth

Oral tradition tells that a woman drowned her children in a river. Repentant and cursed, she has wandered riverbanks, villages and cities ever since, searching for them, and her piercing wail warns anyone who hears it at night of misfortune, death or bad luck.

Her roots reach back to pre-Hispanic cultures. Researchers link her to beings such as Cihuacóatl among the Nahua, the Lacandon goddess Xtabay or Auicanime among the Purépecha. Some also connect her with Tenpecutli or with Chocacíhuatl, a cihuateteo, the spirit of a woman who died in childbirth, who wailed for her child and preyed on travelers at crossroads. The legend took its general shape during the Viceregal period. Three explanations have been proposed: that a real crime gave rise to the tale, that a myth was superimposed on a concrete story, as with Cihuacóatl and La Malinche, or that it is a symbolic story giving voice to Indigenous peoples silenced during the Conquest.

Legacy

Today the legend remains very popular from Mexico to Argentina, and also in southern US states with large Hispanic populations, such as Texas, Arizona and New Mexico. In Mexico she is a symbol of national identity and forms part of Mexico City's Intangible Cultural Heritage.

Trivia

A name with meaning. Chocacíhuatl means 'weeping woman': the Nahuatl choka means 'to cry' and cihuatl means 'woman'.
Confused with La Malinche. La Llorona is sometimes conflated with La Malinche, the Nahua interpreter of Cortés who bore his son.
Weeping in Talamanca. Among the Bribri and Cabécar, the word itsö means both 'weeping woman' and 'tulevieja', linking legends from Costa Rica and Panama.
