Calydonian maiden of Greek mythology

Callirhoë

Callirhoë is a young woman from Calydon whose rejection of the priest Coresus unleashes the wrath of Dionysus on the entire city. Her story, preserved by Pausanias, is an example of the collective madness the god inflicted on those who offended him.

The myth

According to the tradition, Coresus, priest of Dionysus Calydonius, fell in love with Callirhoë, but she rejected all his prayers and promises. Coresus then begged Dionysus for revenge, and the god sent a plague that drove the people of Calydon to raving madness and death.

The Calydonians consulted the oracle of Zeus and Dione at Dodona, which declared that the plague would end only if Callirhoë, or someone willing to take her place, was sacrificed by Coresus himself. No one stepped forward, not even her parents. When the moment came, Coresus could not bring himself to kill her and stabbed himself instead. Moved by pity and remorse, Callirhoë cut her own throat by a spring near Calydon, which was named after her.

Legacy

The myth inspired Giovanni Battista Guarini's tragicomedy Il pastor fido, where it appears in the characters Aminta and Lucrina. André Destouches composed the opera Callirhoé in 1712, and Jean-Honoré Fragonard painted Coresus Sacrificing Himself to Save Callirhoe, first shown in 1765 and now in the Louvre.

Ancient sources: Pausanias, a Greek traveller of the second century AD, records the story in his writings.

Trivia

Name of the spring. According to the myth, the spring where Callirhoë took her life was named after her, and the story may explain the name of the spring near Calydon.
A parallel with Admetus. As with King Admetus, who could escape death if someone agreed to die in his place, only his wife was willing to do so.
