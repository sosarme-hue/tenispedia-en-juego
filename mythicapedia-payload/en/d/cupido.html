Roman god of desire and erotic love

Cupid

Cupid is the Roman god of desire and erotic love, the counterpart of the Greek Eros. His image as a winged boy with a bow and arrows became one of the most recognizable symbols of love in Western culture.

The myth

According to tradition, Cupid is the son of Venus, though accounts disagree about his father. Seneca makes Vulcan his father, Virgil calls Jupiter his father and also his grandfather through Venus, and Cicero distinguishes three Cupids born from different divine pairs. In other traditions he descends from Chaos or from Night and Erebus, and poets moved freely between his playful, capricious youthful side and his older role as a gentle primordial force.

He was said to be born in Cyprus, and Jupiter wanted to strike him down for the harm he would cause the universe, but fate kept him safe. He was raised in the woods by wild beasts, and there he made a bow of ash and arrows of cypress. Venus later gave him a bow and arrows of gold, and his power reached gods and mortals alike, even his own mother. His arrows came in two kinds: golden ones caused uncontrollable desire, and leaden ones caused aversion. Ovid tells how, with a leaden arrow, he made Apollo flee from the nymph Daphne, who was turned into a laurel. His best-known myth is that of Cupid and Psyche, in which he is wounded by his own weapons. Another story tells how Venus turned the nymph Peristera into a dove after a flower-gathering contest that Cupid had lost.

Legacy

His image of a winged boy with a blindfold became proverbial in Western art, and the cherubs and putti of painting and sculpture descend from him. Renaissance poets such as Spenser used him as a motif, and Botticelli painted him in Primavera. Today he is the usual emblem of Valentine's Day. His name gave rise to words such as cupidity, from the Latin cupiditas, and the Latin cupidus survives in the vocabulary of passion.

Ancient sources: Ovid recounts him in the first book of the Metamorphoses, and Virgil gave him his name in the Aeneid.

Trivia

Two kinds of arrows. Cupid carries golden arrows, which inspire desire, and leaden ones, which cause aversion. They explain why love can be returned or can flee.
Cicero's three Cupids. Cicero claimed there were up to three Cupids, each born from a different divine couple, and that the third was identified with Anteros, requited love.
