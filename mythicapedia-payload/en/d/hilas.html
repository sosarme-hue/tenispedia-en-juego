youth companion of Heracles in Greek myth

Hylas

Hylas is a youth in Greek myth, companion and squire of Heracles. His disappearance at a spring in Mysia became one of the best-known episodes of the Argonaut cycle.

The myth

According to tradition, Hylas was the son of King Theiodamas of the Dryopians and the nymph Menodice, daughter of Orion. Other accounts change his parentage and name Heracles, Euphemus or King Ceyx of Trachis as his father. When Heracles killed Theiodamas in a dispute over a plough ox, he took the boy under his care, trained him as a warrior and kept him as his attendant.

Hylas later sailed on the Argo and became one of the Argonauts. During a stop in Mysia, while he went to fetch water from the Pegae spring or the river Ascanius, water nymphs fell in love with his beauty and pulled him under. In one version the nymph Eudaces, or Efidacia in some tellings, drew him down; in another, Hera was behind the abduction. Theocritus says the nymphs stifled his cries. Heracles, helped by Polyphemus, searched for him for a long time without success, and the Argo sailed on without them.

Valerius Flaccus says Hylas stayed with the nymphs and shared their power and their love. Antoninus Liberalis offers another ending: the nymphs turned him into an echo that repeated Heracles' calls again and again.

Worship and religion

According to Strabo, in Roman times the people of Prusias, the city later founded on that site, held a procession in which they called out for Hylas, as if setting out into the woods to look for him. Ancient sources also indicate that sacrifices were offered to him in the area in historical times.

Legacy

His story inspired Theocritus and many artists in antiquity and later. Edmund Spenser retold it in The Faerie Queene, Christopher Marlowe alluded to him in Edward II, and Oscar Wilde mentioned him in several works, including The Picture of Dorian Gray. George Berkeley gave the name Hylas to the materialist spokesman of his Three Dialogues, and Stanisław Lem reused those characters. On screen he appears in Jason and the Argonauts (1963).

Ancient sources: Theocritus (Idyll 13), Apollonius of Rhodes, Valerius Flaccus, Antoninus Liberalis and Strabo mention him.

Trivia

A philosophical name. Berkeley's Hylas takes his name from the Greek word hyle, meaning matter. He represents the materialist view that the author argues against.
An echo in Hardy's novel. In Far from the Madding Crowd, Thomas Hardy compares a shepherd's call echoing through the valleys to sailors invoking Hylas on the Mysian shore.
