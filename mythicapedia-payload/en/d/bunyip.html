amphibious creature of Australian Aboriginal folklore

Bunyip

The bunyip is a creature from the Aboriginal folklore of southeastern Australia, said to lurk in swamps, billabongs and rivers and to devour anyone who ventures too close. Its shape, a blend of seal, dog and long-necked beast, made it one of the best-known figures in Australian folklore.

The myth

Accounts describe the bunyip as an amphibious, nocturnal and elusive being that lives in lakes, rivers, swamps and creeks. According to tradition, it swims swiftly with fins or flippers, gives a loud roar and feeds on crayfish, although some legends portray it as a predator of people, especially women and children. For that reason, many communities avoided unfamiliar waters.

Descriptions vary greatly from region to region. The most common fall into two types: one resembling a seal or a swimming dog, with shaggy dark fur and a round head, and another with a long neck, a head like a horse or emu, and a horse-like tail. In Ngarrindjeri tradition it appears as a water spirit called the Mulyawonk, which punishes anyone who takes more than their share of fish or lets children wander too close to the water. Its stories taught people how to keep the river's resources for the future. Some tales add that it lays its eggs in platypus nests.

Legacy

The name has entered everyday speech: the Australasian bittern, whose low booming call recalls a roar, is nicknamed the ‘bunyip bird’. Modern theories link the creature to seals that swam up the Murray River, to extinct marsupials such as Diprotodon, or to the cassowary. The word first appeared in print in 1812, in the Sydney Gazette.

Trivia

The ‘bunyip bird’. The Australasian bittern, a marsh bird, produces a low booming call during the breeding season, which has earned it the nickname.
Many versions. In 2001, Robert Holden identified at least nine regional variants of the creature across Aboriginal Australia.
