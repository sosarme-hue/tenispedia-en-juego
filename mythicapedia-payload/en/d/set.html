Egyptian god of deserts, storms and foreigners

Set

Set is the Egyptian god of deserts, storms and foreigners, and one of the most ambiguous figures in the pantheon. He was seen both as protector of Ra's solar barque and as the murderer of Osiris, which makes him a central figure in the Osiris myth.

The myth

According to tradition, Set was a son of Geb and Nut and a brother of Osiris, Isis and Nephthys. Jealous of Osiris, who ruled the fertile Nile valley while Set received the desert as his inheritance, he killed his brother, cut the body into pieces and scattered them.

Isis gathered the remains and, with the help of Nephthys, conceived Horus, who later claimed the throne and fought Set in long contests. The accounts disagree: in some texts Horus is Osiris's son and Set's nephew, in others his brother. The Contendings of Horus and Set describes boat races, battles in the form of hippopotamuses and grotesque episodes, such as Set's sexual assault on Horus or the loss of Horus's eye, linked to the moon. The dispute is said to have lasted eighty years, partly because the creator god favoured Set.

Set also had a useful role: standing at the prow of Ra's barque, he fought the serpent Apep every night, the enemy of cosmic order.

Worship and religion

Set was worshipped mainly at Nubt (Ombos, today Kom Ombo) in Upper Egypt, and had shrines at Avaris, Abydos and Pi-Ramesses. He received official worship in the early dynastic period, and the Hyksos made him a national god, with the ruler Apophis I building a temple beside his palace at Avaris. The Ramesside kings, who came from his region, restored his prestige. Under foreign rule in the Late Period his image darkened. He was never widely popular among ordinary Egyptians, and his cult did not survive.

Legacy

The so-called Set animal, whose identity remains uncertain, has been linked to the aardvark, the donkey, the jackal and the greyhound, among other animals, and still fascinates Egyptologists. The Greeks associated him with Typhon, a monster of raging nature. Several pharaohs bore his name, including Seti I, father of Ramesses II. The Coptic form Sēt is the basis for the English pronunciation of the name.

Ancient sources: The Pyramid Texts, the earliest written source for the myth, and the Contendings of Horus and Set, from the Ramesside period.

Trivia

An animal without a name. Egyptologists have not been able to identify with certainty the animal that represents Set, with its curved snout and squared-off ears. Proposals include the aardvark, the donkey and the greyhound.
Lettuce and semen. In the Contendings, Isis has Set eat lettuce leaves smeared with Horus's semen. The semen later appears on Set's forehead as a golden disk, proof of his defeat.
