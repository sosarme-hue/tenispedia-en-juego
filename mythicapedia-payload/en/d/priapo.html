Greek god of fertility, gardens and the phallus

Priapus

Priapus is a minor god of fertility, protector of gardens, vineyards and livestock, recognizable by his enormous permanently erect phallus. Born in the Greek world of Asia Minor, his figure spread to Greece and Italy, where the Romans turned him into a popular character in art and literature.

The myth

Tradition does not agree on his parents. He is most often presented as the son of Dionysus and Aphrodite, though some sources make him the son of Dionysus and a naiad or of Chione, and others name Hermes, Zeus or Pan. In one version, Hera cursed him while he was still in his mother's womb, in revenge for the judgement of Paris, giving him ugliness, foul-mindedness and an impotence that kept him from achieving an erection at the decisive moment. In another, Hera's anger came from the child being fathered by Zeus. The gods threw him off Olympus, and a shepherd found him and raised him.

As an adult he joined Pan and the satyrs as a spirit of fertility, but his sexual clumsiness made him a constant figure of mockery. In Ovid's Fasti he tries to assault the nymph Lotis while she sleeps drunk at a banquet, but a donkey belonging to Silenus brays and wakes her. Lotis flees and is turned into a lotus. Furious, Priapus kills the donkey, and this story explained the donkey sacrifices at Lampsacus, his main city. Other accounts replace the victim with Hestia, or tell of a donkey given speech by Dionysus that challenged Priapus over the size of their members; Priapus won, killed it, and the animal was placed among the stars.

Orphic writers identified him with gods such as Dionysus, Hermes and Helios. Lucian presents him as a warlike god in Bithynia and a rustic tutor of Ares, while Strabo says he was declared a god by later generations.

Worship and religion

His cult began at Lampsacus on the Hellespont and spread to Greece and Italy by the 3rd century BC. He seems to have had no organized priesthood. He was mostly worshipped in gardens and homes, though there were also temples. Offerings included fruit, flowers, cakes, milk and honey, and sacrifices of donkeys, rams and fish. As a protector of sailors and fishermen, he was believed to ward off the evil eye. His cult faded with the ancient world, yet he continued to be invoked as a symbol of health and fertility long after the fall of Rome.

Legacy

His name gave rise to the medical term priapism, for a prolonged erection. He lent his name to the Priapeia, a collection of often obscene and humorous Latin verses. In Pompeii, a fresco in the House of the Vettii shows him weighing his phallus against a bag of coins. In the 1980s, D. F. Cassidy founded the St. Priapus Church, centred on worship of the phallus.

Ancient sources: Ovid (Fasti), Strabo, Pausanias and Lucian mention him; Homer and Hesiod do not.

Trivia

A rival donkey. According to one legend, a donkey that Dionysus had given the power of speech challenged Priapus to a contest over who had the bigger member. Priapus won, but then killed the animal.
A modern church. In the 1980s, D. F. Cassidy founded the St. Priapus Church, a modern congregation centred on worship of the phallus.
