hero of Eleusis, teacher of agriculture

Triptolemus

Triptolemus is a hero of Eleusis in Greek mythology. According to tradition, Demeter taught him the art of growing wheat and he spread it through Greece and beyond, which makes him a central figure of the Eleusinian Mysteries.

The myth

According to the myth, Demeter arrived at Eleusis disguised as an old woman while searching for her daughter Persephone, who had been abducted by Hades. She was welcomed by Metaneira and Celeus, and took charge of raising their son Demophon. Intending to make him immortal, she anointed him with ambrosia and placed him in the embers at night, until Metaneira caught her and the child was consumed by the flames. Demeter then revealed herself as a goddess and taught her rites to the people of Eleusis.

As a reward, she gave Triptolemus grain and a winged chariot drawn by dragons, and he flew across the land sowing seed. He carried agriculture to Scythia, where King Lyncus plotted to kill him, and Demeter turned Lyncus into a lynx. He also travelled to other peoples and founded cities. Triptolemus was, moreover, one of the first priests of the mysteries.

His genealogy is not consistent. The Homeric Hymn to Demeter barely mentions him as a priest, while other authors make him a son of Celeus and Metaneira, of Oceanus and Gaia, or of Eleusis. After his death, some accounts say he judged initiates in the underworld.

Worship and religion

He had a cult of his own, separate from the Mysteries, with temples at Eleusis and in Athens. A fifth-century relief in the National Museum in Athens shows him between Demeter and Kore, receiving from the goddess a golden ear of grain, now lost. He was credited with three commandments: honour your parents, honour the gods with fruits, and spare the animals.

Ancient sources: The Homeric Hymn to Demeter names him as one of the first priests of the Mysteries, while Pausanias and Apollodorus record variants about his parents and deeds.

Trivia

A chariot with one wheel. His chariot had wings and a single wheel, which some scholars read as the circle in which oxen turn when threshing grain.
Three commandments. The philosopher Porphyry attributes to him three commandments for a simple and pious life, among them sparing animals.
