Roman god of the sea and waters

Neptune

Neptune is the Roman god of the sea and, more broadly, of all waters, identified early on with the Greek god Poseidon. He matters because he shows how Roman religion absorbed a Greek divinity while keeping traces of an older god of freshwater.

The myth

According to tradition, Neptune was a son of Saturn and Ops, and a brother of Jupiter, Juno, Ceres, Vesta and Pluto. Soon after his birth, Saturn threw him down into the depths of the sea, fearing that a male son would overthrow him, and the child was protected so that he would survive. When Jupiter drove his father from power, the three male brothers divided the world by lot: Jupiter received the sky, Neptune the sea and Pluto the underworld. Each has a sign of power: Jupiter's three-part thunderbolt, Neptune's trident and Pluto's three-headed hound.

Accounts differ on his wife. Hyginus gives him Amphitrite, mother of Triton, while other Roman mythographers name Salacia or Venilia instead. With Medusa he fathered Pegasus and Chrysaor, and with Theophane he became the father of the golden ram that carried Phrixus to Colchis. His offspring were many: Polyphemus, the Cyclops whom Odysseus met, was his son, and several Argonauts, such as Eufemus, Nauplius and Amycus, counted him as their father.

Neptune also appears in tales of punishment and rivalry. He competed with Minerva for the right to found a city in Attica; Jupiter judged between them and awarded the victory to the goddess, who had planted the olive tree. Enraged, Neptune tried to flood the land, but Mercury, acting on Jupiter's orders, stopped him. In another story he sent a sea monster against Troy, because King Laomedon had broken his promise to sacrifice his livestock to the god, and he demanded that Andromeda be given to a sea monster after her mother boasted that her daughter was more beautiful than the Nereids.

Worship and religion

Neptune had only one temple in Rome, near the Circus Flaminius, the racetrack in the southern part of the Campus Martius. It already existed by 206 BC and was restored by Gnaeus Domitius Ahenobarbus. Later, Agrippa dedicated a basilica on the Campus Martius in honour of the naval victory at Actium, which replaced the older temple.

His festival, the Neptunalia, was held on July 23, at the height of summer heat and drought. People sheltered under huts made of branches, drinking spring water and wine, and it was a time of merrymaking when men and women could mix without the usual social constraints. Worship included the sacrifice of a bull, and Neptune was one of only four Roman gods to whom such a sacrifice was considered appropriate, alongside Apollo, Mars and Jupiter. He was also worshipped as Neptunus equestris, patron of horse racing, a link inherited from the figure of Poseidon.

Legacy

His image appears in Roman art. Mosaics, especially those from North Africa, show him riding a chariot pulled by hippocamps or albino dolphins, surrounded by tritons and nereids, in a style shaped by Hellenistic conventions. In literature, Boccaccio used him allegorically to represent the prosperity that the sea and waters bring to peoples, through navigation, fishing and rivers. In politics, Sextus Pompeius called himself the son of Neptune, showing how association with the sea god could serve as propaganda.

Ancient sources: Varro, Cicero, Hyginus in his Fabulae and Servius in his commentary on the Aeneid all mention him. Greek poets such as Homer assigned dominion over the waters to Poseidon, the god with whom Neptune came to be identified.

Trivia

Festival of the heat. The Neptunalia fell during the peak of summer, when freshwater sources were at their lowest. Participants drank spring water and wine under branch huts.
A single temple in Rome. Neptune had only one temple in Rome, near the Circus Flaminius. In Britain, an inscription from Chichester dedicates a temple to him together with Minerva.
