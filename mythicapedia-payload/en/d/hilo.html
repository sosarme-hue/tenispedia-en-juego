Greek hero, son of Heracles and ancestor of the Heracleidae

Hyllus

Hyllus was a son of Heracles and Deianira in Greek myth, and the husband of Iole. His descendants, the Heracleidae, claimed the Peloponnese, and his story, known as the Return of the Heracleidae or Dorian invasion, explains the origin of the Dorian ruling families.

The myth

According to the most common account, Heracles and Deianira had four sons, Hyllus, Glenus, Ctesippus and Onites, born at Calydon in the early years of their marriage; another version adds a daughter, Macaria. A late and altered tradition, however, says Hyllus was born to the nymph Melite, with whom Heracles united after being exiled for killing his children by Megara. Before he died, Heracles told Hyllus to marry Iole, whom Heracles himself had loved, once he came of age.

After their father's death, the Heracleidae fled from Eurystheus and sought refuge in Athens. In the battle that followed, Eurystheus was defeated and, according to Sophocles and Apollodorus, Hyllus pursued him and killed him. His head was given to Alcmene, who tore out his eyes with a weaving shuttle. The Heracleidae then invaded the Peloponnese, but a plague forced them to withdraw.

The Delphic oracle advised them to wait for the "third fruit". Hyllus took this to mean the third year and returned too early. At the Isthmus of Corinth, a single combat was agreed: if he won, the Heracleidae would enter the Peloponnese; if he lost, they would withdraw and not try again for a hundred years. According to Herodotus and Pausanias, the Peloponnesian champion Echemus of Tegea ended up killing Hyllus.

Ancient sources: Sophocles' Trachiniae, Apollodorus' Bibliotheca, Herodotus, Pausanias and Hyginus.

Trivia

A shuttle against Eurystheus. According to tradition, Alcmene, Hyllus's paternal grandmother, tore out Eurystheus's eyes with a weaving shuttle after receiving his head.
A misread oracle. The Delphic oracle told the Heracleidae to wait for the "third harvest". Hyllus took it to mean the third year and returned too early, with fatal results.
