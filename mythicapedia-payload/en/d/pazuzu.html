king of the demons of the wind in Mesopotamian mythology

Pazuzu

Pazuzu is the king of the wind demons in Mesopotamian mythology, son of the god Hanbi. Although regarded as evil, he was invoked as a guardian of the home, especially of pregnant women and infants, against the demoness Lamashtu.

The myth

According to Mesopotamian texts, Pazuzu was the son of the god Hanbi and ruled over the wind demons, the lilû. He was associated with the southwest wind, which brought famine in the dry seasons and locusts in the rainy ones, and also with plague, fever and delirium.

In one account he meets other lilû on his travels and breaks their wings so that they cannot do harm. In other texts he appears as a more fearsome figure, called the "Agony of Mankind" or the "Sickness of Mankind", and chants were recited to keep him out of homes.

His great rival was Lamashtu, his consort and enemy, who according to tradition attacked mothers and children during childbirth. Pazuzu could force her back to the underworld, which gave him a protective role. He was depicted with a human body, lion or dog head, goat horns, bird talons, two pairs of eagle wings, a scorpion tail and a serpent-shaped penis.

Worship and religion

Pazuzu had no known major temples, but he was invoked in rituals and exorcisms. His images were placed in houses, often on the wall facing the entrance, and worn as amulets, especially to protect pregnant women. Ritual texts from Uruk state that a woman could wear a bronze necklace bearing his image to protect her from miscarriage caused by Lamashtu.

Legacy

His image became famous in modern popular culture through the 1971 novel The Exorcist and the film based on it, in which a statuette of him in the Louvre served as the model.

Ancient sources: Mesopotamian ritual texts, such as the Uruk incantations and the exorcistic texts found at Nimrud, are the main ancient references to him.

In art

Trivia

Heads on necklaces. Large numbers of Pazuzu heads, mostly in terracotta, have been found with holes or loops so they could be hung from the neck.
Possible origin in a king. One hypothesis links his name to King Bazi, named in a version of the Sumerian King List, though this is not proven.
