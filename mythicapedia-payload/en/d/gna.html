Norse goddess, messenger and errand-runner of Frigg

Gná

Gná is a goddess in Norse mythology who serves Frigg as a messenger, running her errands in other worlds. She appears in Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda, which makes her one of the best-documented goddesses in the written sources of medieval Iceland.

The myth

According to the Prose Edda, Frigg sends Gná to different worlds with messages, and she rides the horse Hófvarpnir, which can travel through the air and run over the sea.

Snorri tells how some Vanir saw her crossing the sky and asked her what was flying there. Gná answered in verse that she was not flying but moving through the air on Hófvarpnir, the offspring of Hamskerpir and the mare Garðrofa. These verses appear nowhere else, and Snorri does not say where he got them.

He also links her name to the verb gnæfa, so that saying something gnæfir means it rises up high.

Legacy

Scholars have proposed that Gná means something like "goddess of fullness", although Rudolf Simek considers Snorri's etymology doubtful. In the nineteenth century, Jacob Grimm compared her with Fama, the Roman personification of rumour, but noted that Gná is not described as winged, unlike Hófvarpnir, which may have been, much like the winged horse Pegasus. Hófvarpnir and the eight-legged horse Sleipnir are often cited as the leading examples of horses in Norse mythology that link earth and sky.

Ancient sources: Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda, in the Gylfaginning and Skáldskaparmál books, names her among the goddesses.

Trivia

A horse that treads the sea. Hófvarpnir can move through the air and over the sea. His name is usually translated as "he who throws his hoofs about" or "hoof-thrower".
A possible link with Fama. In the nineteenth century, Jacob Grimm proposed that Gná and Fama, the Roman personification of rumour, might share a common origin.
