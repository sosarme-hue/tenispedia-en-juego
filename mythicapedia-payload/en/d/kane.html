Hawaiian god of procreation and creation

Kāne

Kāne is the highest of the three major Hawaiian deities, alongside Kū and Lono. He is regarded as the god of procreation, the creator of life and the ancestor of both chiefs and commoners, linked to dawn, the sun and the sky.

The myth

According to Hawaiian tradition, Kāne arrived from Kahiki together with Kanaloa and travelled through the islands with him. One account says the two lived for a time in Waipio valley with Maliu, Kaekae and Ouli, where they grew bananas and lived peacefully. He is also credited with bringing edible plants to Hawaiʻi.

In the creation account recorded in the 1907 book Legends of Hawaii, there was at first nothing but Po, an endless black chaos. Kāne pulled himself free of it by sheer will, and Lono and Kū followed. Kāne created light, Lono brought sound and Kū brought substance, and together they made the lesser gods and the Menehune. They then shaped a man from red clay mixed with their spittle and breathed life into him; in some versions the first man was made in Kāne's image. Kanaloa tried to copy the feat, failed, and claimed the dead as his own, though other versions present him as Kāne's dark alter ego.

The Kumulipo tells a different story in which Kāne does not appear: from the stillborn son of Wākea and Hoʻohokukalani sprouts the first taro plant, Hāloa, and humanity descends from the pair of brothers.

Worship and religion

Worship of Kāne required no human sacrifice or laborious ritual. Offerings included fish, coconut and taro, and sacred objects associated with him included pigs, coconuts, breadfruit, awa and the wauke plant. Worshippers faced their front doors east and turned toward the sun in morning prayers, and he was invoked when canoes were built.
