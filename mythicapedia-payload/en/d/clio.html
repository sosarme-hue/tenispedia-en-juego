Greek muse of history

Clio

Clio is, in Greek mythology, the muse of history and, in a few accounts, of lyre playing. Daughter of Zeus and the Titaness Mnemosyne, her name comes from a root meaning "to make famous", which explains her role as keeper of the memory of great deeds.

The myth

According to tradition, Clio was one of the nine Muses, daughters of Zeus and Mnemosyne, the goddess of memory. Together with her sisters she dwelt on Mount Helicon or Parnassus, although they were also placed in Pieria, near Olympus. Among her attributes were the laurel crown and the scroll or book, and in some traditions she was credited with inventing rhetoric.

Accounts of her children vary from author to author. A scholium on Euripides makes her the mother of Hymenaeus and Rhesus, while other sources give her a son, Hyacinth, by Pierus. According to Apollodorus, Aphrodite made her fall in love with Pierus as punishment for mocking the goddess's affair with Adonis. As for Linus, some versions make him the son of Clio and Magnes, while others name different mothers, among them Urania or Calliope.

Legacy

Her name appears in many modern brands and awards, including the Clio Awards for advertising. Academic societies such as the Cambridge University History Society are informally known as Clio, and Clio Bay in Antarctica is named after the muse. In academic usage the prefix survives in coined words such as cliometrics and cliodynamics. In art, Clio has been painted by artists such as Artemisia Gentileschi and Pierre Mignard, usually recognised by her trumpet and book.

Ancient sources: Apollodorus and a scholium on Euripides record her offspring and loves; Statius invokes her at the start of his poems as guardian of the annals of the past.
