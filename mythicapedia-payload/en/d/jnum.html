Egyptian ram-headed god of creation and the waters

Khnum

Khnum is an Egyptian god with a ram's head, originally linked to the First Cataract of the Nile. He was regarded as the creator of human bodies and their ka, the life force, which places him at the heart of fertility and birth.

The myth

According to tradition, Khnum was the son of Nun, the primordial waters, and Nunet. Stories credit him with shaping people from Nile clay on a potter's wheel, giving them form before birth and placing them in their mothers' wombs.

Another version tells that, tired of turning the wheel, he broke it and placed a part of it in each woman, so that from then on people could reproduce without his direct involvement. He was also credited with the primordial egg from which sunlight emerged, and with the creation of gods, animals and plants, according to hymns inscribed at the temple of Esna.

As guardian of the waters, he was believed to regulate the Nile's yearly flood and to watch over its sources at Elephantine, as well as the waters of the Duat, the underworld. His partners vary by place: at Elephantine he is the husband of Satis and father of Anuket; at Esna he appears with Menhit, Neith and Heka, and at Antinoe with Heket. Heka is named as his eldest son and successor, and Ra is said to be their child, sometimes called Khnum-Ra.

Worship and religion

His main centre was Elephantine, where he formed a triad with Satis and Anuket, and Esna, where he was worshipped alongside other deities. His cult is also attested at File, Hypselis and Antinoopolis. Mummified sacred rams adorned with gold headgear have been found in stone coffins.

The temples at Elephantine and Esna hosted fertility rites reserved for women trying to conceive, with male priests excluded. The "Installation of the Potter's Wheel" ceremony ended with a feast on the first day of the month of Paremhat. His worship lasted from the First Dynasty into the Greco-Roman period, and he was associated with Ra, Ptah, Amun and Jepri.

Legacy

His image, with a ram's or crocodile's head and the atef crown, survives in statuettes, amulets and inscribed ostraca held in museums such as the Museo Egizio in Turin. The sources do not record a later presence in the culture of other peoples.

Ancient sources: No independent ancient texts describe him; his attributes are known mainly from the hymns and reliefs of the temples at Esna and Elephantine and from Greco-Roman inscriptions.

In art

Trivia

A royal name. The god's name appears in the titulary of the pharaoh Khufu in the form "Khnum protects me", showing his importance in the age of the pyramids.
A ritual for women only. At the temple of Esna, women presented a potter's wheel and chanted before a hidden statue of the god in a fertility ceremony without male priests.
