the labyrinth of the Minotaur in Greek myth

Labyrinth of Crete

The Labyrinth of Crete is, in Greek myth, the maze that Daedalus built to imprison the Minotaur on the island of Crete. Many scholars believe the legend drew on the palace complex at Knossos, whose many rooms and corridors may have seemed like a true maze to visitors.

The myth

According to the story, King Minos had the Minotaur locked in the labyrinth, a creature fed with human sacrifices. After Athens lost a war against Minos, the city was obliged to send seven young women and seven young men every so often to be devoured.

When the third tribute was due, the Athenian prince Theseus volunteered as one of the youths, intending to kill the monster. Ariadne, daughter of Minos, gave him a ball of thread to mark his way back, and after defeating the Minotaur, Theseus left the labyrinth by following it. He forgot to raise the white sails, and his father Aegeus, believing him dead, threw himself from Cape Sounion. The Aegean Sea is said to take its name from him.

In another episode, Daedalus, an Athenian architect exiled on Crete, was locked with his son Icarus in his own labyrinth. He made wax wings to escape, but Icarus flew too close to the sun, the wax melted, and he fell into the sea.

Worship and religion

According to tradition, the Athenians preserved Theseus's ship for centuries, repairing it constantly, and used it as a sacred vessel to carry an embassy every year to the festivals of Apollo at Delphi. The Minotaur is linked to the bull worship practised on Crete.

Legacy

The Labyrinth of Crete gave its name to the intricate pattern we now call a labyrinth. Turf mazes survive in Britain, and the Etruscan Tragliatella jar shows a labyrinth labelled with the word Troy. In 2024 a circular structure with a labyrinthine layout was announced at Papoura, near Kastelli, which may have served as a Minoan ritual centre.
