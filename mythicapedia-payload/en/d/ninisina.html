Sumerian goddess of medicine and patron of the city of Isin

Ninisina

Ninisina, whose name means "Mistress of Isin", was the protective goddess of the Mesopotamian city of Isin and a deity linked to healing. She was regarded as a divine physician and midwife who could also drive out demons, and her character came to overlap with that of other healing goddesses in the region.

The myth

According to Mesopotamian texts, Ninisina was first portrayed as a goddess without a husband or children. Over time, the god Pabilsaĝ came to be seen as her husband, and her children were Damu, Gunura and Šumaḫ, all of them associated with healing. Her court included other deities, such as Ninarali, linked to the underworld, and Šumaḫ served as her sukkal, a divine attendant.

Stories depict her with surgical tools such as the scalpel and the lancet, and wearing a great robe associated with physicians. Her hands were described as soothing, and she was called "cold-handed" or "soft-handed mother". She was also said to know medicinal plants and the mythical "plant of life" from the Epic of Gilgamesh. As a midwife she was invoked in texts and hymns, and she was believed to confront demons such as Namtar. Some hymns and laments also express her grief over the loss of her city and of her son Damu.

Worship and religion

Her worship is first attested in Isin in the Early Dynastic period and is also documented in Larak, Nippur and Lagash. The kings of the Isin dynasty made her their royal goddess, built temples in her honour, and derived their authority from her, as shown in art where she hands them the rod-and-ring symbol. She continued to be venerated into the Neo-Babylonian period.

Ancient sources: She appears in many Mesopotamian texts, including the hymn "Ninisina's Journey to Nippur", preserved in monolingual and Sumerian-Akkadian bilingual versions, as well as in hymns, laments and god lists.
