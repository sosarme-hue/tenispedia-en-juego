father of Erigone and devotee of Dionysus in Greek mythology

Icarius

Icarius is a figure of Athenian Greek mythology, remembered as the first man to receive wine from Dionysus. His tragic death, and that of his daughter Erigone, gave rise to a legend about a plague and to the placing of the constellation Boötes among the stars.

The myth

According to the tradition, Icarius welcomed Dionysus warmly, and in gratitude the god taught him to cultivate the vine and make wine. Icarius wished to share the gift with his shepherds, but they drank too much, became intoxicated and, believing the wine had poisoned them, killed him.

His daughter Erigone and her dog Maera found the body. Erigone hanged herself over her father's grave. Dionysus, angered, punished Athens with a plague that drove the unmarried women to madness, and they too hanged themselves. The epidemic only ended when the Athenians established rites in honour of Icarius and Erigone.

Dionysus, or in other accounts Zeus, moved by their misfortune, placed Icarius among the stars as the constellation Boötes.

Worship and religion

The legend linked Icarius and Erigone to Athenian rites instituted to end the plague, which the tradition connects with the cult of Dionysus. A mosaic in a mid-2nd century AD Roman villa in Nea Paphos, Cyprus, shows the god giving wine to Icarius, indicating that the story still circulated in Roman times.

Legacy

The figure of Erigone gave its name to one of Jupiter's moons, and the constellation Boötes preserves the memory of the character in the night sky.

Ancient sources: Clement of Alexandria refers to him as the husband of Phanothea, a woman believed to have invented the hexameter.

Trivia

Paphos mosaic. A mid-2nd century AD Roman villa in Nea Paphos, Cyprus, known as the House of Dionysus, holds a mosaic showing the god giving wine to Icarius as a reward for his hospitality.
A dog with a name. Erigone's dog was called Maera and was the one who found Icarius's body together with its mistress.
