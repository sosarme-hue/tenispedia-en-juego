archaic Roman deity

Helernus

Helernus, also known as Alernus, was an archaic Roman deity about whom very little is known, and even his name is not fully settled. Scholars have linked him to agriculture or to a minor role in the underworld, depending on how the evidence is read.

The myth

The surviving stories about Helernus are extremely sparse. According to the tradition that has come down to us, he was the father of Carna, a goddess who protected children's intestines from striges, creatures in Roman lore often compared to vampires.

His sacred grove, the lucus, lay near the mouth of the Tiber river. The sources describe no deeds or episodes involving him, so his figure is known mainly through the worship he received.

Worship and religion

Every year on 1 February, the Roman pontiffs offered him a sacrifice of a black ox. That choice suggests he may have been a minor chthonic deity, since black animals were given to gods of the underworld. Some scholars link him to the sowing of vegetables and grain, though that reading rests on no direct testimony.

Ancient sources: Some manuscripts of Ovid record his name, in forms that later scholars emended. The grammarian Festus describes the sacrifice of a black ox to a god who is probably Helernus.

Trivia

Protector of children. His daughter Carna protected children's intestines from striges, beings that Roman tradition associated with vampires.
A parallel in the Iguvine Tablets. Some researchers compare Helernus with Hule, a figure who appears a couple of times in the Iguvine Tablets, ritual texts in the Umbrian language.
