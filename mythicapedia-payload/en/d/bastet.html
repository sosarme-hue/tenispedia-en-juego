Egyptian goddess of protection, love and dance, with a cat's head

Bastet

Bastet is the Egyptian goddess of protection, love, dance and harmony, shown as a domestic cat or as a woman with feline features. She guarded homes, temples, pregnancies and births, and her cult left behind one of the largest animal burial sites in Egypt.

The myth

According to tradition, Bastet began as a lioness goddess linked to the warm rays of the sun. She embodied the sun's gentle side, while Sekhmet represented its destructive face. As the Eye of Atum she was also associated with the Moon, and as an Eye of Ra she took part in the struggle against Apep, the serpent enemy of the sun god. Accounts differ on her parentage: some make her a daughter of Ra and Isis, others of Ra and Hathor. With Ptah she was said to have a son, Maahes.

Her character was unpredictable. She could be tender and protective, but when angered she became a fierce lioness, even more violent than Sekhmet. One myth tells how a fiery lioness was cooled by the water of her temple's sacred lake and became a gentle cat who stayed there. She protected pregnant women and newborns from disease and evil spirits, and as a good mother she was sometimes shown surrounded by kittens.

Worship and religion

Her worship dates to the earliest days of Egyptian civilization and she is mentioned in the Pyramid Texts and the Book of the Dead. Her main centre was Bubastis in the Nile Delta, where the temple was surrounded by water on three sides. Cats were raised in her sanctuaries and, when they died, were mummified and buried in dedicated cemeteries. Herodotus describes the town's annual festival as the most popular in Egypt: crowds arrived by boat, with music, singing, dancing and plenty of wine, to keep the goddess content and prevent her from turning into a raging lioness.

Legacy

Her name gave rise to the town of Per-Bastet, which appears in Hebrew as Pibeseth in the Book of Ezekiel. One suggestion is that the word alabaster may derive from her name through Greek, though this would be a much later usage than her time as a protective goddess.

Ancient sources: The Pyramid Texts and the Book of the Dead.

In art

Trivia

A cat cemetery. More than 300,000 mummified cats were found when the goddess's temple at Bubastis was excavated. Many were buried next to their owners.
Crowds at the festival. Herodotus relates that some 700,000 visitors, men and women but not children, came to Bubastis each year for the goddess's festival.
Cats on shields. According to a legend, Egypt surrendered to the Persians, who carried cats on their shields, because Egyptians would not harm a cat. They thus lost the city of Pelusium.
