Greek warrior of the Trojan War

Sinon

Sinon is a Greek warrior, cousin of Odysseus, who during the Trojan War persuaded the Trojans to bring the wooden horse into their city. His role as a traitor makes him a key figure in the war's ending.

The myth

According to tradition, Sinon was the son of Aesimus, grandson of Autolycus, and a cousin of Odysseus through his mother. Homer does not mention him, and his story appears in later works that differ in the details.

In Virgil's Aeneid, shepherds find him near Troy after the Greeks have sailed away. He presents himself as a deserter, claims the horse is an offering to ensure the Greeks' safe voyage home, and says Odysseus had left him to die. He convinces Priam and the Trojans to bring him inside the city, despite Cassandra's warnings. At night, Sinon opens the belly of the horse so the hidden warriors can come out, and he lights a torch as a signal to the fleet returning from Tenedos.

Other versions change the details: in some, the signal is lit beside Achilles' tomb; in Tryphiodorus, Sinon feigns mistreatment by the Greeks, and Helen also signals with a golden torch.

Legacy

Sinon appears in art, often shown being dragged toward Troy as a captive with the horse behind him. Virgil used him to reinforce the Roman image of the Greeks as cunning and deceitful. A tragedy by Sophocles that featured him has been lost, though Virgil is thought to have drawn inspiration from it.

Ancient sources: Virgil's Aeneid, the Little Iliad and the Iliupersis (both lost), Sophocles' tragedy (lost), Apollodorus' Bibliotheca, Hyginus' Fabulae, Quintus Smyrnaeus' Posthomerica and Tryphiodorus' Alosis Iliou.

Trivia

A rationalist version. Palaephatus, who sought to explain Greek myths rationally, rejected the hollow horse and described a horse too large to pass through the city gates.
A lost tragedy. Sophocles wrote a tragedy titled Sinon, of which only four quotations of one word or less survive.
