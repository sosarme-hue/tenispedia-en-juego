never-ending battle in Norse mythology

Hjaðningavíg

It is the endless battle between the followers of Heðinn and Högni, which Norse legend places in a combat repeated every day until Ragnarök. It survives in Scandinavian sources and is believed to have continental Germanic origins.

The myth

According to the tale, Heðinn, son of Hjarrandi, abducts Hildr, daughter of Högni, while her father is away. When Högni returns he finds her on an island where Heðinn waits with his army; Snorri Sturluson places it on Hoy in Orkney. Hildr offers peace and a necklace on Heðinn's behalf, but Högni has already drawn Dáinsleif, a sword whose wounds never heal and which always kills a man once unsheathed. They fight all day; in the evening both return to their camps, but Hildr revives the fallen with incantations and the battle begins again.

Saxo Grammaticus tells a different version, with Hithinus and Hilda, and a holmgang in which Högni spares his wounded son-in-law. Seven years later they fight again and both die, yet Hilda raises them each night. In the Eddas, Skíði asks for Hildr's hand to halt a war that threatened Valhalla. The tradition ends when Christianity arrives and breaks the curse.

Legacy

It appears on the Stora Hammar image stone on Gotland and in Old English poetry such as Deor, which mocks the Heodenings with irony, and in the Middle High German poem Kudrun.

Ancient sources: Skáldskaparmál and Ragnarsdrápa in the Eddas, Sörla þáttr in Flateyjarbók, Saxo Grammaticus's Gesta Danorum, Skíðaríma, Háttalykill inn forni and the Old English poem Deor.

Trivia

Cursed sword. Dáinsleif, Högni's sword, caused wounds that never healed and had to kill a man each time it was drawn.
Mercy in combat. According to Saxo, Högni spared Heðinn because the ancient Scandinavians considered it cowardly to kill a weaker opponent.
