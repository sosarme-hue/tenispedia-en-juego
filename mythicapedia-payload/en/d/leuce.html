Oceanid nymph of the white poplar in Greek mythology

Leuce

Leuce is an Oceanid nymph of Greek mythology, a daughter of Oceanus according to the late commentator Servius. Her story, which involves abduction by Hades and transformation into a white poplar, explains the tree's link with the underworld and with Heracles.

The myth

According to the tale, Hades fell in love with Leuce and, using his strength, carried her off to the underworld. Although she was the daughter of a Titan, Leuce was not immortal: she spent her life there and, when she died, the god turned her into a white poplar and placed it in the Elysian Fields.

Tradition adds that Heracles, returning from the underworld after his twelfth and final labour, crowned his head with a branch of this tree. Pausanias says the hero discovered the white poplar on the banks of the Acheron in Thesprotia and brought it to Greece, which is why Homer called it Acheroid. Pausanias discusses the poplar and Heracles but does not mention the nymph.

Worship and religion

The white poplar was sacred to Persephone, for whom Leuce may be an epithet or a double. The Eleans burned only its wood in sacrifices to Zeus, in memory of Heracles. The sources do not document a cult dedicated to Leuce herself.

Legacy

Robert Graves revived the myth in The White Goddess, where he counts the white poplar among the "three trees of resurrection," alongside alder and cypress. His idea that the leaf's underside turned white from the sweat of Heracles is a poetic theory, not an ancient account. Leuce was also the name the Greeks gave to Snake Island in the Black Sea.

Ancient sources: Servius presents her as a daughter of Oceanus and recounts her abduction; Pausanias and Homer link the white poplar to Heracles.

Trivia

A two-faced tree. White poplar leaves are dark on top and white underneath, which Servius read as a symbol of Heracles' labours in the upper and the underworld.
Crowns for heroes and athletes. The white poplar could be worn as a crown at athletic contests in honour of Heracles, patron of the Olympic games.
