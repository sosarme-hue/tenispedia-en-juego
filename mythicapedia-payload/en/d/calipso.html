nymph in Greek mythology who detained Odysseus

Calypso

Calypso is a nymph in Greek mythology who ruled the island of Ogygia and kept Odysseus there for years, offering him immortality if he stayed. She matters chiefly for her role in Homer's Odyssey, where she stands for the temptation of pleasure set against the hero's longing for home.

The myth

In the Odyssey, Calypso welcomed the shipwrecked Odysseus to her cave on Ogygia, feeding him, giving him wine and sharing her bed. She kept him by force for seven years, though other accounts give five or only one. She promised him immortality and eternal youth, but the hero grew weary, wept on the shore and longed for Penelope.

Athena asked Zeus to free him, and Zeus sent Hermes to deliver the order. Calypso, though resentful, obeyed: she gave him tools, provisions and materials to build a raft, and a favourable wind. Odysseus set off, half-suspecting a trap.

Accounts of her parentage differ. Homer makes her a daughter of Atlas, Hesiod places her among the Oceanids, daughters of Oceanus and Tethys, and later traditions name Helios and Perse, or Atlas and Pleione, as her parents. According to Hesiod, she bore Odysseus two sons, Nausithous and Nausinous, and later legends add Telegonus, Ausonus or Latinus, though Latinus is usually given to Circe. Her ending is sorrowful: some versions say she waited endlessly for Odysseus' return, while Hyginus says she died of grief by her own hand.

Legacy

The episode has been compared with Gilgamesh and Siduri in the Epic of Gilgamesh, through the motif of the lone woman who counsels the wandering hero and the building of a raft. Letitia Landon wrote the poem Calypso Watching the Ocean, which portrays her yearning endlessly for Ulysses' return. Arthur Quiller-Couch's comic novel The Astonishing History of Troy Town gives the name to the younger daughter of Admiral Buzza. Philosophers Theodor Adorno and Max Horkheimer discussed her as a figure of power over fate, and Ryan Patrick Hanley read her in Fénelon's Les Aventures de Télémaque as a link between eros and pride. She has appeared in films and television, and the Pirates of the Caribbean character Tia Dalma is later revealed to be her.

Ancient sources: Homer's Odyssey, books V and VII; Hesiod and the Catalogue of Women, known through fragments; and the Library of Apollodorus and Hyginus' Fabulae.

Trivia

Telling name. Her Greek name is linked to the verb meaning to conceal, and the Etymologicum Magnum reads it as "she who conceals knowledge".
Sons with seafaring names. The names of her sons, Nausithous and Nausinous, are linked to seafaring, according to the sources.
