fire serpent of Tupi-Guarani folklore

Boitatá

A fire serpent from Tupi-Guarani folklore in the Brazilian Amazon, also known in Paraguay, Uruguay and Argentina. It punishes anyone who harms forests and animals; its name means fire serpent in Guarani.

The myth

According to a legend recorded in Rio Grande do Sul, a time came when the forest was plunged into darkness and endless floods. A serpent living in a dark cave, able to see in the gloom, decided to feed only on the eyes of other animals. After devouring so many, its body became a mass of flames and it died. Since then it appears as a beam of fire over fields and rivers, and whoever crosses its path may be blinded, driven mad or killed. To avoid this, tradition advises staying perfectly still, holding one's breath and keeping one's eyes shut until it passes.

Legacy

Folklorist Franklin Cascaes produced some thirty artworks on the creature, and writer Simões Lopes Neto documented its lore in literature. It stars in modern children's books such as Who's Afraid of the Boitatá? (2007), and in Brazil its name is also used as a bogeyman to frighten children.

Ancient sources: The earliest known written mention is a letter by the Jesuit José de Anchieta, dated 1560.

Trivia

A 16th-century description. In 1560 the Jesuit José de Anchieta described a shining beam moving toward its victims, attributed to this being and likened to a serpent's undulating motion.
A fiery bull. In Santa Catarina it is described as a bull-sized creature with a glowing eye in its forehead. The name was confused with the Portuguese word boi, meaning ox.
