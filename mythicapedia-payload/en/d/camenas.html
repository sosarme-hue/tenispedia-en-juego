Italic spring nymphs in Roman religion

Camenae

The Camenae were Italic nymphs of springs and fountains, worshipped in Rome and later identified with the Muses. They were associated with the fountains near the Porta Capena, by the Caelian Hill.

The myth

According to tradition, the Camenae were nymphs who lived in the wells, springs and fountains near the Porta Capena. Ancient texts say little about them, and their names appear alongside those of Egeria, Carmenta, Juturna and Tacita, though it is unclear whether these figures belonged to the group or were only closely connected to it.

Livy tells that King Numa dedicated a grove to the Muses because he met his wife Egeria there. Plutarch describes these Muses as mountain nymphs who gave oracles, and says Numa taught his people how to honour them. Later, Latin poetry identified them with the Greek Muses.

Worship and religion

There is no evidence of a widespread public cult. Local oracular worship seems to have taken place in a chapel beside the fountain of the Porta Capena, with a nearby grove dedicated at least to Egeria. Libations were offered to them. Milk was the usual offering to fountain deities, though Martial sacrificed a virgin sow and Horace a young goat at different fountains.

Legacy

Livius Andronicus rendered the Greek word Mousa as Camena, and Horace spoke of the "soft breath of the Greek Camena" in his odes. He also mentions the "nine Camenae" in the Carmen saeculare. Their use as a synonym for the Muses later declined, then revived around Horace's time.

Ancient sources: Ovid, Livy, Horace, Plutarch and Frontinus mention them, and the expanded Servius records the libations offered to them.

Trivia

A translation with consequences. Livius Andronicus translated Homer's Odyssey into Latin and used the word Camena to render Μοῦσα. This created the link between the Camenae and the Muses.
A sacrifice at a fountain. The poet Martial sacrificed a virgin sow at the fountain of his friend Estela, an offering to a spring that differed from the usual milk.
