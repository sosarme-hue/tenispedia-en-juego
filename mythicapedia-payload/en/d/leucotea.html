Greek sea goddess who rescues sailors

Leucothea

Leucothea is a sea goddess of Greek mythology, linked to sea foam and calm waters. She is usually identified with Ino, a Theban princess who threw herself into the sea, although in other versions she originates from the nymph Halia; her main role is to rescue sailors from storms.

The myth

In the most common version, Ino was the sister of Semele and wife of King Athamas. After Semele's death, Ino helped raise the infant Dionysus, which angered Hera, who struck her with madness. Ino leapt into the sea with her son Melicertes in her arms, and out of pity the gods turned them into sea deities: the boy became Palaemon and she became Leucothea.

In Homer's Odyssey, Leucothea appears as the goddess who saves Odysseus. After Poseidon destroys his raft, the hero sinks but reaches the surface thanks to a magic veil the goddess gives him, in the form of a seabird. Swimming for two days, he reaches the island of the Phaeacians.

On Rhodes, by contrast, the tradition speaks of the nymph Halia, with whom Poseidon had children. When these sons went mad at Aphrodite's doing and forced their mother, Halia threw herself into the sea and received the name Leucothea. A Miletus tradition tells that the goddess, in the form of a swan, revealed her identity to some boys and ordered athletic games held in her honour.

Worship and religion

The cult of Leucothea, identified with Ino, spread across the Greek world. The lyric poet Alcman mentions her sanctuary, and at Megara sacrifices were offered to her every year. At Corinth she was worshipped together with Poseidon and Palaemon, and on the road to the port of Lechaeum both were depicted with a dolphin. Strabo also records a temple in Colchis.

Legacy

Her name comes from the Greek leukós, 'white', alluding to sea foam. Her figure appears in ancient literature and in Robert Graves's proposed cult of the White Goddess.

Ancient sources: Homer mentions her in the Odyssey as the goddess who saved Odysseus; Pindar and Cornutus place her among the Nereids or as a daughter of Nereus.

Trivia

Goddess of the foam. Her name means 'white goddess', and it relates to the colour of sea foam.
Ino and Mater Matuta. Cicero states that Ino was worshipped as Leucothea in Greece and as Mater Matuta in Rome.
