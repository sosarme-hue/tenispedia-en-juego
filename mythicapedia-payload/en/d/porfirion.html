Porphyrion

Porphyrion is one of the giants of Greek mythology and, according to some accounts, the strongest of them and their king. His story matters because it captures the rebellion of the giants against the Olympian gods, known as the Gigantomachy, and the decisive roles of Zeus and Heracles in their defeat.

The myth

According to Apollodorus, Porphyrion surpassed the other giants and was born of Uranus and Gaia, the sky and the earth. Other traditions place his origin earlier: Hyginus makes him a son of Erebus and Night, and lists him among the personified abstractions. Some accounts even say he was among the first beings to sprout from the ground. His Greek name, Porphyríōn, is linked to verbs meaning "to push," "to rise up" or "to spring forth."

When the giants rose against the gods, Porphyrion was one of the most fearsome. The tradition holds that he tried to hurl the island of Delos at them. To weaken him, Zeus stirred in him a sudden desire for Hera in the middle of the battle. The giant's response was so violent that, as he tried to force himself on her, he tore the goddess's robe. Zeus seized the moment to strike him down with a thunderbolt, and Heracles finished him off with arrows.

According to Nonnus, his mother Gaia had promised him the hand of Hebe, goddess of youth, a detail that ties him to the world of the gods he sought to challenge.

Ancient sources: Apollodorus, Hyginus and Nonnus record the different versions of his origin, his role in the Gigantomachy and his links to Hera and Hebe.

Trivia

An attempt against Delos. Porphyrion tried to hurl the island of Delos at the gods, a gesture that shows the scale of the giants' rebellion.
A name of movement. His Greek name can be rendered as "to push," "to rise up" or "to spring forth," verbs that hint at an origin tied to the earth.
