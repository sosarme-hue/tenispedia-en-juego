Roman rustic goddess, wife or sister of Faunus

Fauna

Fauna is a rustic goddess of Roman mythology who, according to ancient sources, was the wife, sister or daughter of Faunus, the Roman counterpart of Pan. She is linked to prophecy and to the nurturing of all living things.

The myth

The ancient sources do not agree on her family. Varro presents her as the female counterpart of Faunus and explains that the fauni, gods of the Latins, had prophetic powers: they foretold future events in the woods using verses called Saturnians, and their name supposedly came from fari, "to speak." Servius identifies Faunus with Fatuclus and says his wife was Fatua or Fauna, names he also links to fari because they can foretell the future.

The early Christian writer Lactantius calls her Fenta Fauna and says she was both sister and wife of Faunus. Just as he sang the fata, "fates," to men, she sang them to women. Justin describes Fatua, wife of Faunus, filled with divine spirit and predicting the future as if in a frenzy, which he takes as the origin of the verb fatuari, to speak under divine inspiration. Macrobius, by contrast, derives her name from faveo, "to favor," because she nurtures everything useful to living beings.

Worship and religion

According to Macrobius, the Books of the Pontiffs treated Bona Dea, Fauna, Ops and Fatua as names of one and the same goddess, whom they called Maia. The sources do not describe temples or festivals specific to Fauna, so her worship is poorly known.

Ancient sources: Varro, Servius, Lactantius, Justin and Macrobius discuss her in their works.

Trivia

An Oscan dedication. At Aeclanum, in Irpinia, an inscription in the Oscan language was found naming Fatuveís, the genitive of Fatui, which shows that the concept was Italic.
The Favourable. Georges Dumézil translated her name as "the Favourable," linking it to the root of Faunus, which goes back to Proto-Indo-European.
