restless spirits of the dead in Roman religion

Lemures

In Roman religion, the lemures were the spirits of the restless or malignant dead, who could return from the grave to torment the living. Their cult, including the festival of the Lemuria, reveals Roman fear of the dead who lacked proper burial and honours.

The myth

According to ancient writers, the lemures were the souls of those who had not received a proper burial or the affectionate remembrance of their families. They wandered without rest and could take revenge on the living.

Ovid calls them di manes or di parentes, ancestral gods or spirits of the underworld, vagrant, unsatisfied and potentially vengeful. They had no fixed form and moved in a liminal space between the world of the living and that of the dead, associated with darkness and the fear it brings.

St Augustine grouped them with larvae as malignant spirits, in contrast to the lares, the benevolent ancestors who protected the home. Horace used the term larvae, while Ovid used lemures in his Fasti.

Worship and religion

On May 9, 11 and 13 the Lemuria was celebrated. At midnight the paterfamilias rose, threw black beans behind him without looking back, and believed the lemures would feast on them. Black was the proper colour for offerings to chthonic deities. William Warde Fowler read the offering as a gift of life, and priests were barred from the rite as too impure. If the spirits remained dissatisfied, they were driven off by banging bronze pots.

Legacy

The naturalist Carl Linnaeus took the name lemur for a family of nocturnal primates with slow movements, inspired by his reading of Virgil and Ovid. In Goethe's Faust, a chorus of lemures serving Mephistopheles digs Faust's grave.

Ancient sources: Horace (Epistles) uses the term larvae, and Ovid (Fasti) describes the lemures and their rites.

Trivia

The beans of the Lemuria. The rite involved throwing black beans without looking back, an offering that, according to Fowler, symbolised a gift of life.
A primate's name. The term lemur that Linnaeus applied to nocturnal primates comes from these Roman mythological spirits.
