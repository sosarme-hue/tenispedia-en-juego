Chinese god of love between men, known as the Rabbit God

Hu Tianbao

Hu Tianbao, also called the Rabbit God (Tu'er Shen), is a Chinese deity who watches over love and sex between men. His cult grew in Fuzhou, in Fujian province, and remains one of the best-documented examples of devotion to a same-sex patron in traditional China.

The myth

According to the tradition, Hu Tianbao was a young man from Fuzhou who fell in love with a handsome, clever magistrate and followed him to every public event. Blinded by his passion, he spied on the magistrate while he was using the toilet. Caught, he was beaten until he confessed his attraction, and the furious magistrate ordered the beating to continue until he died.

The legend adds that, because his crime had been born of love, underworld officials decided to right the injustice by making him protector of affection between men. Soon after his death he appeared to his beloved and later to a man from his hometown in a dream, asking for a temple. The temple was built, and its cult survived discreetly for almost a century before drawing the attention of the authorities.

Worship and religion

Men who wanted to win another man's love came to the temple, known as the Lesser Official Temple. In 1765 the official Zhu Gui described its statue as two men embracing, one older and one young and pale. If a prayer was granted, believers returned to smear the god's mouth with pig intestines mixed with sugar. Zhu Gui destroyed the statue and the wooden tablets. No temple survives in Fujian, but a shrine in Yonghe, Taiwan, founded in 2006, venerates him today and draws thousands of pilgrims each year, many of them gay men seeking a partner.

Legacy

His story appears in the 2010 Taiwanese drama The Rabbit God's Matchmaking, the short film Kiss of the Rabbit God, and the American Gods episode "The Rapture of Burning". He is also a playable character in the game Tokyo Afterschool Summoners.

Ancient sources: Yuan Mei recorded his story in What the Master Would Not Discuss (Zi buyu) in the 18th century, and the official Zhu Gui condemned the cult in his report on Fujian's prohibited cults.

Trivia

A nickname with a double meaning. In late imperial China, "rabbits" was a slang term for homosexual men. That is why he is called the Rabbit God, although he has nothing to do with rabbits.
A shrine in Taiwan. The shrine in Yonghe, Taiwan, is the only known one dedicated to this deity, since the original temple in Fujian has not been rebuilt.
