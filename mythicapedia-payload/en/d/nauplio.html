Greek hero, son of Poseidon and eponym of Nauplia

Nauplius

Nauplius is, in Greek myth, a son of Poseidon and Amymone and the eponymous hero of Nauplia, in Argolis. He is remembered above all for his revenge on the Greeks returning from Troy, whom he lured onto the rocks with false lights on the coast.

The myth

According to Apollodorus, Nauplius was a seafarer who for many years sailed the sea luring the ships he met with torches so that he could kill their crews, until he too perished in the same way. According to the tragedians and the author of the Nostoi, he married Clymene, daughter of Catreus, though other versions give his wife as Philyra or, according to Cercops, Hesione. Their children were Palamedes, Oeax and Nausimedon.

Palamedes was stoned to death through the schemes of Odysseus. Nauplius went to the Greeks to demand justice for his son, but returned unsatisfied, since Agamemnon, Odysseus's accomplice, sided with the accusers. He then plotted revenge: he stirred adultery among the wives of the Greek leaders, Clytemnestra with Aegisthus among them. When the fleet was sailing home, he lit torches on Mount Caphereus, and the ships, mistaking them for a harbour, were wrecked on the rocks. According to Hyginus, the gods had already punished the Greeks for sacking Ilion and for Ajax's outrage against Cassandra, and Nauplius exploited the storm for his revenge.

In the lost tragedies of Aeschylus, Sophocles and Euripides, all titled Palamedes, the hero appeared defending his son. According to Sophocles, Nauplius delivered a speech listing Palamedes' inventions.

Legacy

His name gave rise to the city of Nauplia, in Argolis. The Caphereus rocks, where the wrecks occurred, were called the Rocks of Ajax in Hyginus's account.

Ancient sources: Apollodorus (Library), Hyginus (Fabulae), the Greek tragedians (Aeschylus, Sophocles and Euripides, in lost plays) and the Nostoi.

Trivia

Rocks of Ajax. According to Hyginus, the rocks where the Greeks were wrecked were named the Rocks of Ajax, after the hero whose impiety had provoked divine anger.
