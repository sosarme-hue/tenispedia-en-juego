Mesopotamian goddess of love

Nanaya

Nanaya is the Mesopotamian goddess of love, voluptuousness and sensuality, venerated by Sumerians and Akkadians. Closely tied to Inanna, she was especially honoured in Uruk.

The myth

According to the texts, she was the daughter of the god An and sometimes of Inanna, who is occasionally presented as her mother. She also had a daughter, Kanisurra. Her origin is debated: some scholars see her as a minor Akkadian goddess, others as a manifestation of Inanna, but the evidence is inconclusive.

Her main role was as lady of love, and the texts speak about sexuality with notable frankness. She protected lovers, including the rejected and the betrayed, as shown in a text from Sippar titled 'The Faithful Lover'. Over time she lost her individuality and came to be seen as an aspect of Inanna/Ishtar, who absorbed her. Later she was syncretised with the Babylonian goddess Tasmetu.

Worship and religion

Her main cult centre was Uruk, alongside Inanna, though she also had a sanctuary at Kish. Her veneration spread as far as Syria and Iran. Neo-Babylonian archives from Uruk list extensive offerings and regalia dedicated to her: feathered tiaras, crowns, jewellery, mirrors and garments embroidered with golden sequins. As part of an extinct religion, her cult is no longer practised today.

Legacy

For a long time Assyriologists identified her with Venus, as they did Inanna. Since the 1990s Joan Goodnick Westenholz has challenged that view, and most later studies accept her position; the texts link her more with the moon.

Ancient sources: Hymns and cult songs, royal inscriptions (one by Esarhaddon), the Sippar text 'The Faithful Lover', incantations from Isin and Neo-Babylonian archives from Uruk.

Trivia

Sacred fees. A cult song describes her in erotic terms and states that she was believed to charge fees for sexual services.
An invented name. Late Babylonian texts proposed an artificial Sumerian etymology from NA, 'to call', giving something like 'the one who keeps calling'.
