queen of Messenia in Greek myth

Merope of Messenia

Merope is a queen of Messenia in Greek myth, daughter of the Arcadian king Cypselus and wife of the Heraclid Cresphontes. Her story, marked by the murder of her family and her son's revenge, inspired ancient tragedy and many later works.

The myth

According to tradition, after the Heraclids invaded Arcadia, Cypselus gave his daughter Merope in marriage to Cresphontes, with Messenia as her dowry. When the king and his two older children were killed in an uprising, another Heraclid, Polyphontes, seized the throne and forced Merope to marry him.

Her youngest son, Aepytus, had been sent away for safety and vowed revenge. Once grown, he returned to Messenia at the request of the Arcadians, killed Polyphontes and the others involved in his father's and brothers' deaths, freed his mother and claimed the throne.

Legacy

Euripides based his lost tragedy Cresphontes on this myth, and only about fifty lines survive. In 1713 Scipione Maffei premiered his Merope in Verona, and it spread across Europe. Voltaire adapted it for the stage in 1743, and Matthew Arnold reworked it in 1858.

Ancient sources: Hyginus recounts it in his Fabulae (137), and Plutarch and Aristotle cite episodes from her story.

Trivia

An example in ethics. Aristotle mentions Merope in the Nicomachean Ethics as an example of an action done unknowingly, because she did not realise the circumstances: she could have taken her own son for an enemy.
The axe scene. According to Hyginus, in the plot where the son poses as his father's killer, Merope enters the room with an axe to strike the sleeping man, not knowing he is her own son.
