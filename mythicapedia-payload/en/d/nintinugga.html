Mesopotamian goddess of medicine and cleansing from Nippur

Nintinugga

Nintinugga was a Mesopotamian goddess linked to medicine, bandages, wounds and cleansing, worshipped in Nippur alongside Enlil and Ninlil. She is often compared with other healing goddesses, yet she kept a distinct identity.

The myth

Her name is conventionally rendered from Sumerian as "Mistress who revives the dead," though Barbara Böck warns that this may be a later scholarly etymology; it may originally have meant something like "lady of the lofty wine," and only from the reign of Uruinimgina was it written with the sign for "to die." She was invoked as "lady of life and death." According to a late medical incantation her father was Ninazu, and according to An = Anum her husband was Endaga, a little-known god. A lament presents her as the mother of Damu. Her link to the underworld led to the belief that she supplied the dead with clean water. She was also invoked against the demon Asag, and she was thought to keep dogs, animals associated with most healing goddesses.

Worship and religion

Her cult was centred in Nippur from the Early Dynastic period. She had a chapel in the temple of Ninlil and her own sanctuary, possibly called Eurusaga, as well as the Eamirku within Enlil's E.NI.gula. Physicians served as her cultic officials, which is considered early evidence of her healing role.

Ancient sources: She appears in god lists and in medical and ritual texts of ancient Mesopotamia, such as the incantations Letter-Prayer of Inanaka and A Dog for Nintinugga.

Trivia

An uncertain etymology. The traditional meaning of her name may be a later scholarly interpretation. Originally it could have meant something like "lady of the lofty wine."
Sacred dogs. She was believed to own dogs of her own, and a text from the Ur III period mentions a throne decorated with two of them prepared for her in Ur.
