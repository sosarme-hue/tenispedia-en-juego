Fornjót

Fornjót is an ancient giant in Norse mythology and the father of Ægir (the sea), Logi (fire) and Kári (wind). The sources portray him as a king of Kvenland and northern lands, and later genealogists used his figure as a starting point for legendary royal lineages.

The myth

According to the Norse tradition preserved in medieval sources, Fornjót ruled the far north, in lands known as Finnland, the land of the Sámi, and Kvenland. The versions do not fully agree: in the Orkneyinga saga and in Hversu Noregr byggðist, preserved in the Flateyjarbók, he appears as a ruler of Gotland or Jutland, depending on the editor, and he is the father of three sons: Logi, Kári and Hlér, whom we call Ægir.

Hversu describes how the domains were shared out: Hlér ruled the seas, Logi the fire and Kári the wind. Poets used his name as a poetic circumlocution. Þjóðólfr of Hvinir used it for fire in the Ynglinga tal, and Snorri noted in Skáldskaparmál that the wind could be called a son of Fornjót. Svein, in his Nordrsetu-drápa, speaks of Fornjót's misshapen sons who first began to fly.

In several sources Fornjót also appears as an ancestor of the kings of Norway and of the Yngling dynasty.

Legacy

His name may have left a trace in Old English. A plant called forneotes folm, meaning roughly 'Fornet's palm', appears in a glossary and a leechbook, possibly referring to Fornjót; the plant itself is not certainly identified, and a marsh orchid (Dactylorhiza) has been proposed. Some medieval genealogies also make him an ancestor of William I of England and of European noble and Icelandic families, a legendary construction with no historical basis.

Ancient sources: The Ynglinga tal of Þjóðólfr of Hvinir, Snorri Sturluson's Skáldskaparmál, the Nordrsetu-drápa of Svein, the Orkneyinga saga, and Hversu Noregr byggðist, preserved in the Flateyjarbók.

Trivia

A plant with his name. A glossary and an Old English leechbook mention the plant forneotes folm, which may derive from his name, though this is not confirmed.
An ancestor of kings. A legendary genealogy places him as a direct ancestor of William I of England, though it is a later construction rather than part of the old sources.
A poetic wind. Snorri records that the wind could be named in poetry as a son of Fornjót, an example of skaldic circumlocution.
