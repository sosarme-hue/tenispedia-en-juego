supreme god of Mazdaism and Zoroastrianism

Ahura Mazda

Ahura Mazda is the supreme deity and creator of Mazdaism, also known as Zoroastrianism, and the main god of the Persian Empire. His name is usually rendered as 'Lord Wisdom', and he embodies the opposition between good, which he represents, and evil, personified by Angra Mainyu.

The myth

According to Zoroastrian tradition, Ahura Mazda is an abstract, transcendent god with no fixed image. All lesser divinities are his creations, and the Amesha Spenta, who are better described as truths than as gods, help him govern creation.

Accounts of his family vary between sources. In some Zurvanist narratives, his mother is the wife of Zurvan, and he later married her and had children, including the sun and cattle. In non-Zurvanist Zoroastrian traditions, he married his daughter Spenta Armaiti, who bore Keyumars and later Mashya and Mashyana.

The central episode is Zoroaster's revelation. According to tradition, at the age of thirty, while drawing water at dawn for a sacred rite, he saw Vohu Manah, who led him into the presence of Ahura Mazda. There he learned the principles of the Good Religion. Zoroaster declared that some Iranian gods were daevas unworthy of worship, and that evil came from Angra Mainyu, the destructive spirit, whom Ahura Mazda surpasses but does not equal.

The story ends in victory. At the end of time, Ahura Mazda will defeat Angra Mainyu for good, a cosmic fire will purify the universe, and beings will attain immortality at the final judgment known as Frashokereti.

Worship and religion

Fire and light were regarded as manifestations of Ahura Mazda, and the term atar refers to sacred fire. In the Achaemenid period, his only representation at court was an empty chariot drawn by white horses, used to invite him to accompany the Persian army into battle. From the reign of Artaxerxes II he was invoked alongside Mithra and Anahita. By the Sasanian period, the images made of him had been removed in an iconoclastic movement. Today the worship survives among Zoroastrian communities in Iran and India.

Legacy

His name appears in inscriptions of Darius I and in Aramaic and Armenian texts, where the form Aramazd survives. In Middle and New Persian it was transliterated as Hormizd, Ohrmazd or Ormazd. Greek authors called him Oromazes.

Ancient sources: The Behistun inscription of Darius the Great, the Avesta, especially the Yasna and the Gathas, and the writings of Plutarch, who names him Oromazes.

Trivia

A god-less chariot. In the Achaemenid army, Ahura Mazda's presence was represented by an empty chariot drawn by white horses, symbolizing his company in battle.
Rats and Arimanius. Plutarch says that Arimanius, the god of evil, was linked to water-rats, so those who killed them were regarded as fortunate.
