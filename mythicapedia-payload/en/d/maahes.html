Egyptian lion-headed god of war

Maahes

Maahes was an ancient Egyptian god of war shown with a lion's head, linked to the protection of sacred places. His cult, tied to the feline goddesses Bast and Sekhmet, spread during the New Kingdom and makes him one of the most distinctive leonine deities of Egyptian religion.

The myth

According to Egyptian tradition, Maahes was a son of Ra and, in other versions, of the feline goddesses Bastet or Sekhmet, whose nature he shared. He accompanied the sun god on his solar barque and each night fought the serpent Apep, Ra's great enemy.

Because of his fearsome appearance he was regarded as the guardian of all sacred places. Titles from the New Kingdom such as "Lord of Slaughter" and "The Scarlet Lord" emphasise his warlike character, and he was associated with knives and lotus flowers.

Worship and religion

His main cult centre was Taremu (Leontopolis), capital of the eleventh nome of Lower Egypt, where tame lions were kept in a temple beside that of Bast. Osorkon III, of the Twenty-second Dynasty, built a temple to him at Per-Bast (Bubastis). In Memphis and Leontopolis he was identified with Nefertum.

Legacy

His Greek name, Mios or Miysis, is linked to the city name Leontopolis, which recalled the cult of lions. Scholars debate whether he had a foreign origin, perhaps related to the Nubian lion god Apedemak.

Ancient sources: The Pyramid Texts use his name as a synonym for lion, and the Greek historian Aelian describes the lion cult in Egypt.

Trivia

Sacred lions. His temple at Taremu kept tame lions, which were fed daily with ox meat.
A name with two meanings. His name begins with the hieroglyph for the male lion, though on its own it can also mean "one who can see in front".
