Aztec god of fire and the year

Xiuhtecuhtli

Xiuhtecuhtli was the Aztec god of fire, heat and time, regarded as father of the gods and lord of the year. His cult was one of the oldest and most revered in the indigenous pantheon.

The myth

In Aztec mythology, Xiuhtecuhtli embodied fire and warmth, and was shown as an old man carrying a brazier, with a red or yellow face. His symbol was the scorpion, whose sting burns. Sources give him many names reflecting his aspects: lord of volcanoes, of life after death, of warmth in cold and of light in darkness.

The Florentine Codex describes him as father of the gods, who dwelt in a turquoise enclosure at the centre of the earth. His wife is usually given as Chantico, though another account names Chalchiuhtlicue. He was linked with Huehuetéotl, the old god, and sometimes seen as a manifestation of the dual deity Ometecuhtli.

At the end of each 52-year cycle, the xiuhmolpilli, people feared the gods would abandon humanity. To prevent this, feasts were held and sacrifices made, in which a captive dressed in the god's garments had his heart removed, and fire was kindled on his chest.

Worship and religion

Xiuhtecuhtli was patron of the Aztec emperors, who at their enthronement were regarded as his living embodiment, and also of the pochteca merchants. A sacred fire burned permanently in his temples, and a small hearth was kept alight in his honour in every home. As thanks for fire, the first mouthful of each meal was thrown into the hearth. His annual festival took place in the month of Izcalli, and every four years it was held more solemnly at the great temple of Tenochtitlan. Every 52 years the New Fire Ceremony marked his renewal.

Legacy

His name contains the word xihuitl, meaning year, turquoise and fire at once. Six Royal Mail stamps issued in 2003 to mark the 250th anniversary of the British Museum featured him among their motifs.

Ancient sources: The Nuttall and Borgia codices depict him, and the Florentine Codex describes his role as father of the gods.

Trivia

Fire in every home. Every Aztec household kept a small permanent fire burning in honour of the god.
A god of turquoise. For Aztec priests turquoise was the symbolic equivalent of fire, and the god's name contains the word meaning year, turquoise and fire.
