jotunn and sea god in Norse mythology

Ægir

Ægir is a jotunn, a giant of the sea, and the personification of the ocean in Norse mythology. He is also a generous host to the gods, a role that links him to feasts and to the brewing of ale.

The myth

According to the stories, Ægir lives in a sea hall lit by gold that gleams like fire, where ale seems to pour without end. His wife is Rán, who also personifies the sea, and together they had nine daughters who stand for the waves. The sources do not fully agree on his parentage: in the Norwegian tradition he is one of the sons of the giant king Fornjót, along with Logi, fire, and Kári, wind. Other texts identify him with Gymir or with Hlér, lord of an island now called Læsø.

The best-known tale is the Hymiskviða, in the Poetic Edda. After a hunt, the gods are thirsty and look for a cauldron large enough to brew ale for everyone. Ægir agrees to host a feast, but he needs a huge kettle that the gods cannot find. Týr remembers a distant one, and Thor must go and fetch it, in a story the poem tells with humour and conflict.

At the feast recorded in the Lokasenna, Loki murders Fimafeng, one of Ægir's servants, after the gods praise his service. Loki then returns to the hall and stirs up quarrels among the guests until the gods drive him out. Snorri Sturluson, in the Skáldskaparmál, also records that gold came to be called Ægir's fire because of the brightness of his hall.

Legacy

The name of Ægir entered the poetic language of the skalds, who used it as a word for the sea. A ship could be called Ægir's horse, and waves could be called Ægir's daughters. The island of Læsø keeps in its name the memory of Hlér, one of the names linked to this figure. The sources also point to his presence in modern popular culture, though they give no specific examples.

Ancient sources: The Poetic Edda (Grímnismál, Hymiskviða and Lokasenna), Snorri Sturluson's Skáldskaparmál, and the Hversu Noregr byggðist, where he appears as a son of Fornjót.

Trivia

Ægir's fire. In the Skáldskaparmál, gold is called Ægir's fire because his hall shines with gold as if it were flame.
Ægir's horse. Norse poets called a ship Ægir's horse, an example of how his name became a word for the sea.
