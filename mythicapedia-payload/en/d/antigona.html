Theban princess, daughter of Oedipus

Antigone

Antigone is a Theban princess, daughter of Oedipus, who defies King Creon to bury her brother Polynices. Her story, staged by Sophocles in the tragedy that bears her name, has become a symbol of divine law set against human law.

The myth

Antigone was the daughter of Oedipus, king of Thebes, and of Jocasta, though in another variant her mother is Euryganeia. Her siblings were Eteocles, Polynices and Ismene. According to the tradition, a curse from Oedipus hung over the two brothers, who were to take turns on the throne but ended up at war. Eteocles refused to give up power, and Polynices, exiled, marched on Thebes with an army from Argos. Both died, each at the other's hand.

Their uncle Creon took the throne and forbade the burial of Polynices as a traitor. Antigone buried him anyway, arguing that divine law outranks human law. The king sentenced her to be walled up alive in a tomb, where she hanged herself. Her fiancé Haemon, Creon's son, tried to kill his father and then took his own life beside her; his mother Eurydice also killed herself. Creon, having changed his mind, arrived too late.

Other versions differ. In Euripides' lost play the disaster is averted by Dionysus and Antigone marries Haemon, while Hyginus tells of a son she bore with him and of Heracles pleading in vain for Haemon's pardon.

Legacy

George Steiner pointed to Antigone as the most extraordinary case of a dramatic theme being reworked in the West, since her story brings together the basic conflicts of drama: men and women, young and old, society and the individual, humans and gods, the living and the dead. It has inspired works by Brecht, Anouilh, Orff and Honegger, and Salvador Espriu turned it into a symbol of the Spanish Civil War. In Latin America, Leopoldo Marechal and Griselda Gambaro reinterpreted it, and the tragedy is still cited in debates over natural law and the right to burial.

Ancient sources: Sophocles stars her in Antigone (c. 442 BC), she appears briefly in Aeschylus' Seven Against Thebes, and Euripides wrote another Antigone, now mostly lost.

Trivia

A hidden son. In the account preserved by Hyginus, Antigone has a son with Haemon, named Maeon, who is recognized at funeral games by a dragon mark on his body, a sign borne only by descendants of the Spartoi, the first people of Thebes.
Two mothers. The sources do not agree on her mother: the most widespread version names Jocasta, but another variant names Euryganeia.
