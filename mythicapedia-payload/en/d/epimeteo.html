Titan of Greek mythology, brother of Prometheus

Epimetheus

Epimetheus is a Titan of Greek mythology, son of Iapetus and brother of Prometheus. His name means "afterthought", and his lack of foresight makes him the opposite of his brother; through his marriage to Pandora he is linked to the origin of the evils that befell humankind.

The myth

Epimetheus is the son of the Titan Iapetus and of Clymene, or, in some versions, Asia, and the brother of Prometheus, Atlas and Menoetius. His name means "afterthought", in contrast to Prometheus, whose name means "forethought". Both are portrayed as benefactors of humankind, but Epimetheus lacks his brother's prudence and does not heed his advice.

According to Hesiod, in the Theogony and in Works and Days, Epimetheus accepted Pandora as his wife, the woman shaped by Hephaestus at Zeus's command, even though Prometheus had warned him never to accept a gift from the Olympian. By the time he understood his mistake it was too late, and with Pandora troubles came upon mortals. Hesiod does not state the marriage outright; later authors inferred it.

In Plato's Protagoras, the gods entrust the two brothers with distributing abilities among the living creatures. Epimetheus gives animals strength, speed, defences and food, but he uses up all the resources and leaves humans unprotected. Prometheus then steals fire and craft skills from Hephaestus and Athena, and Zeus sends Hermes to bring moral sense and justice.

Epimetheus and Pandora had a daughter, Pyrrha, who married Deucalion, son of Prometheus; the two survived the flood. Other traditions name Prophasis, Excuse, and Metameleia, Regret. Pindar calls Epimetheus the father of Prophasis, and a scholion names Ephyra as his wife, though the accounts differ.

Legacy

In Psychological Types, Carl Jung uses the figure of Epimetheus to describe the false application of a mental function, as opposed to its healthy and creative use. Erasmus of Rotterdam, in his Adages, gives the modern phrase "Pandora's box" and makes Epimetheus the one who opens it, with the saying "malo accepto stultus sapit". Bernard Stiegler places Epimetheus at the centre of his philosophy of technogenesis and anthropogenesis, and faults Heidegger for forgetting him.

Ancient sources: Hesiod, in the Theogony and Works and Days; Plato, in the Protagoras; and Pindar, in the fifth of his Pythian Odes.

Trivia

A monkey made of clay. Boccaccio tells that Epimetheus was the first to model a human statue from clay, and that Jupiter turned him into an ape and chained him on the Pithecusae islands. Boccaccio reads this as a metaphor for someone who imitates without understanding.
A saying from Erasmus. In his Adages, Erasmus of Rotterdam writes "malo accepto stultus sapit", which can be rendered as "experience makes the fool wise".
