sacred bull and Egyptian god of fertility

Apis

Apis was the sacred bull of ancient Egyptian religion, worshipped in Memphis from very early times. Linked to the god Ptah, the Sun and fertility, he later became associated with Osiris and Sokar and came to guide the dead.

The myth

According to tradition, Apis was the son of the cow goddess Hathor, although some accounts name Isis as his mother. His mother was said to have conceived him through a flash of lightning from heaven or through moonbeams. Apis served as herald of Ptah, the chief deity of Memphis, and was regarded as a manifestation of the king, since the bull embodied strength, fertility and a fighting spirit.

The chosen calf had to bear specific markings: a white triangle on the forehead, the outline of a vulture's wing on its back, a crescent on its right flank, a scarab mark under its tongue and double hairs on its tail. The animal that matched these signs was taken to a temple and given a herd of cows. His movements were read as prophecies, and his breath was believed to cure illness. When he died, mourning lasted sixty days while his body was embalmed, and then priests searched for a successor. After death he was called Osorapis, Osiris-Apis, and Greek writers came to see him as an incarnation of Osiris.

Worship and religion

Worship began in Memphis, where Apis was honoured as a protector of herds and linked to the Sun and the Nile god. The bulls were buried in the Serapeum at Saqqara, where Auguste Mariette found the tombs of more than sixty animals, dating from the New Kingdom into the Ptolemaic period. Under Ptolemy I the cult spread to Alexandria and, through the figure of Serapis, became very popular among Greeks and Romans. It endured until around 400 CE, when Christians destroyed the Serapeum of Alexandria in 385 and the cult was later banned by decree.

Legacy

Apis gave rise to Serapis, a syncretic god combining Greek and Egyptian elements, whose name derives from Aser-hapi, meaning Osiris-Apis. The bull image also appeared on private coffins, where Apis was shown as a powerful protector of the dead.

Ancient sources: Manetho's Aegyptiaca places his adoption as a god in the reign of Kaiechos; Arrian records that Alexander the Great sacrificed to him, and Pliny the Elder describes the divinatory practices linked to his cult.

Trivia
