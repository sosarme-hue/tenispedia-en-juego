guardian lion of Burmese temples

Chinthe

A guardian creature in the form of a highly stylized lion, usually placed in pairs at the entrances of Buddhist pagodas and monasteries in Myanmar. It is the Burmese version of the guardian lions found across Southeast Asia.

The myth

According to the Mahavamsa, Princess Suppadevi had a son by a lion, who later abandoned her and spread terror across the land. Not knowing who he was, the son hunted the lion and shot it with an arrow, after three failed attempts in which the arrow bounced off the father's chest. Discovering the truth, he raised a statue of the lion as a temple guardian to atone for his sin.

Burma also has the manussiha, with a human head and a lion's body, comparable to the Western sphinx.

Worship and religion

Its statues guard pagodas and temples and remain very much part of popular devotion. Before coins existed, bronze weights shaped like it were believed to bring economic good fortune.

Legacy

It appears on royal thrones, bells, official seals and kyat banknotes. During the Second World War, Orde Wingate named his long-range forces “The Chinthes”, a name that became “Chindits”.

Ancient sources: The Mahavamsa, a Pali chronicle from Sri Lanka, records the origin legend of the lions guarding the temples.

Trivia

The Chindits. The British long-range forces in Burma took their name from the chinthe during the Second World War.
Leaps from nine directions. The manussiha is said to be able to leap on its enemies from nine different directions.
