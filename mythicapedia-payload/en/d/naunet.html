Egyptian goddess of the primordial waters

Naunet

Naunet (also Nunet) is the goddess of the primordial waters in Egyptian mythology, the female counterpart of Nun and one of the eight deities of the Hermopolitan Ogdoad. She embodies the watery chaos from which all life emerged, along with the light that shaped the world.

The myth

According to the Egyptian tradition, before creation there was only Nun, a chaotic and inert cosmic ocean from which the primordial mound rose. Naunet shared that original abyss with him. In different versions, the sun was born from these waters, sometimes directly from the Benben mound and sometimes from a lotus blooming on it, in the form of a falcon, a scarab or a child. At Heliopolis, however, creation was credited to Atum, who existed within the waters as a potential being.

The Memphite theology, inscribed on the Shabaka Stone, presents Ptah as creator, and its first verse calls him "Ptah-Nunet, the mother who gave birth to Atum". Art shows her as a woman with a snake's head, while Nun appears as a man or with a frog's head.

Worship and religion

Neither Nun nor Naunet had temples or a cult of their own. They were evoked through a sacred lake or an underground stream, as at Abydos, and sanctuaries such as Karnak and Dendera feature sacred cisterns that represent them. Their worship did not survive.

Ancient sources: The verse of the Shabaka Stone (Memphite theology) and the Book of Gates, where Nun appears in the twelfth hour holding aloft the solar barque.

Trivia

Frogs and snakes. When the Nile floods receded in the Delta marshes, frogs and snakes appeared. For this reason, frogs were associated with the male deities of the Ogdoad and snakes with the female ones.
