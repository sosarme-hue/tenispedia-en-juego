Roman god of beginnings and thresholds

Janus

Janus is the Roman god of beginnings, gates and transitions, usually shown with two faces, one looking back and one looking forward. He was invoked at the start of almost every ceremony, and the month of January takes its name from him, so his presence still lingers in the calendar.

The myth

According to Roman tradition, Janus is one of the oldest gods in the pantheon. Some ancient writers saw him as older than everything, linking him to Chaos and calling him the god of gods and the most ancient of the gods. For this reason, his parentage is not fixed in every version. Arnobius presents him as a son of Heaven and Trivia, while other accounts place his origin in Thessaly or near the Ionian Sea.

His best-known story concerns his alliance with Saturn. According to Macrobius, Saturn arrived by sea in Latium, Janus welcomed him, taught him farming, and shared the throne with him; in return, Saturn honoured him in turn. Earlier, Janus had ruled alongside a native king, Cameses, and after Cameses died he became sole ruler. He is also credited with inventing coinage, navigation and agriculture, which makes him a kind of cultural hero.

When the Sabines tried to seize the Capitol, Janus made boiling water spring up over the attackers and drove them back. From then on he was invoked at the start of wars, and the doors of his temple stood open while the conflict lasted so that he could come to the city's aid. In peacetime they were closed.

His two faces express his role: one looks at the past and the other at the future, and while one watches those who enter, the other watches those who leave. Ovid describes him as the doorkeeper of the heavenly court, accompanied by the Hours, able to see east and west at once. His epithets Patulcius, the opener, and Clusius, the closer, reflect this double function.

Worship and religion

Janus had no flamen or specialised priest; his ceremonies were carried out by the rex sacrorum, the King of the Sacred Rites. His presence in religious life was constant, since he was invoked at the beginning of every sacrifice, whatever the main deity of the occasion. He was honoured at the start of sowing, harvests, weddings, funerals and other beginnings, and he was also asked for access to the other gods. His temple and its doors had a visible political and military role in the city.

Legacy

The name of Janus passed to the month of January, from Latin Ianuarius. From Latin ianua, 'door', also comes the English word 'janitor'. The Janiculum, one of the hills of Rome, takes its name from this god. His two-faced image has also inspired modern interpretations, such as that of Georges Dumézil, who linked him to a god of beginnings within the Indo-European religious structure.

Ancient sources: Varro, Cicero, Ovid in his Fasti, Macrobius, Plutarch in his Parallel Lives and Horace all mention him as a god of beginnings.

Trivia

Doors of war. The doors of the Temple of Janus in Rome were opened in wartime and closed when peace arrived.
A month named for him. The month of January, Latin Ianuarius, is named after this god who opens the year.
