mythical region of the Peloponnese, symbol of a pastoral paradise

Arcadia

Arcadia was a mountainous region of the Peloponnese in ancient Greece that came to symbolise a simple, pastoral paradise in harmony with nature. Today its name designates any idyllic, peaceful place.

The myth

According to Greek mythology, Arcadia was the domain of Pan, god of the wild and of shepherds: a virgin wilderness home to dryads, nymphs and other spirits of nature. It was a version of paradise, though only as the abode of supernatural beings, not a resting place for dead mortals.

The Greek writers, however, described a very different reality. Its inhabitants were famed for their music, their rough virtue and their hospitality, but also for their great ignorance and low standard of living. Polybius painted it as a poor, barren, rocky and cold land, so poets set their bucolic verses in Sicily instead. Virgil later took up the tradition in his Eclogues, poems set in Arcadia, home of Pan, pipes and song.

Legacy

The idea was revived in the Renaissance by Jacopo Sannazaro's novel Arcadia, published in 1504, and taken up by poets such as Garcilaso de la Vega, Philip Sidney and Lope de Vega. Nicolas Poussin painted Et in Arcadia ego, and in the Rococo Jean-Antoine Watteau set his fêtes galantes in Arcadian landscapes. In the 19th century W. S. Gilbert used the idea in his musicals Happy Arcadia and Iolanthe, and in 1994 the composer Thomas Adès wrote the string quartet Arcadiana.

Ancient sources: Theocritus in the 3rd century BCE and Virgil's Eclogues.

Trivia

From Arcadia to Acadia. The explorer Giovanni da Verrazzano applied the name «Arcadia» to the North American Atlantic coast in the 16th century. Over time the word evolved into Acadia.
An American Arcadia. In the 1880s the painter Thomas Eakins produced a series of Arcadian works, including In Arcadia, which mixed mythology with photography.
