Norse god of kingship, fertility and good harvests

Freyr

Freyr (Old Norse 'lord, master') is a god of Norse mythology associated with kingship, peace, fertility, prosperity, fair weather and good harvests. He was one of the most venerated deities of Norse paganism, especially in Sweden, where he was regarded as a mythical ancestor of the royal house.

The myth

In the Icelandic Poetic Edda and Prose Edda, Freyr belongs to the Vanir and is the son of the god Njörðr and of an unnamed twin sister, who is also the mother of his twin, the goddess Freyja. The gods gave him Álfheimr, the realm of the elves, as a gift when his first teeth came through. He rides the shining boar Gullinbursti, crafted by the dwarves Sindri and Brok, and sails the ship Skíðblaðnir, which always catches a fair wind and can be folded up to fit in a pouch. He had three servants: Skírnir, Byggvir and Beyla.

The longest surviving myth tells how Freyr fell in love with the giantess Gerðr. To win her he gave up his sword, a weapon that fought by itself 'if wise be he who wields it'. Without it he defeated the giant Beli with an antler. However, lacking his sword, he will be killed by the fire giant Surtr during Ragnarök.

Snorri Sturluson called Freyr 'the most renowned of the Æsir'. In the Heimskringla he is presented as the first king of the Yngling dynasty, and his son Fjölnir, born to him and Gerðr, succeeded him.

Worship and religion

Adam of Bremen describes a phallic statue of Freyr in the temple at Uppsala and links him with peace and pleasure. He was also associated with a horse cult, and sacred horses were kept at his sanctuary in Trondheim, Norway. His veneration was especially common in Sweden and well known in Norway and Iceland. Today his worship is revived within the Heathenry movement, which seeks to restore the ancient Germanic gods.

Legacy

He appears alongside other figures from Norse mythology in Adam Oehlenschläger's Danish poem Nordens Guder (1819), and in Gerður Kristný's Icelandic Blóðhófnir (2010), a feminist retelling of the Eddic poem Skírnismál that won the 2010 Icelandic Literature Award. His name survives in many Norwegian and Swedish place names, such as Frösön, 'Freyr's island', in Jämtland, or Frösåker, 'Freyr's field', in Uppland.

Ancient sources: The Poetic Edda, the Prose Edda and the Heimskringla of Snorri Sturluson, along with Adam of Bremen's account of the temple at Uppsala.

Trivia

The Rällinge figurine. In 1904 a Viking Age figurine seven centimetres tall was found in Sweden and is interpreted as Freyr. It shows a bearded man seated cross-legged, wearing a pointed cap, with an erect phallus. It is now displayed at the Swedish National Heritage Museum.
Gold foil figures. Thin gold foil figures have been found across Scandinavia, often showing a man and a woman embracing or facing each other beneath a leafy branch. Some scholars link them to the union of Gerðr and Freyr, and nearly 2,500 turned up at a single site.
The Skog tapestry. A section of the tapestry from Skog Church in Hälsingland shows three figures interpreted as Odin, Thor and Freyr, though they have also been read as the Scandinavian saint-kings Canute, Eric and Olaf.
