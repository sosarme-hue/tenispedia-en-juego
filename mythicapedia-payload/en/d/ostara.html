Germanic goddess of spring

Ostara

Ostara, or Ēostre in Old English, is a Germanic goddess of spring. Her name gave rise to the English word for Easter, and she is known mainly through the testimony of the monk Bede the Venerable, whose account scholars still debate.

The myth

Stories about Ostara are scarce. The main source is Bede the Venerable, who wrote in the 8th century in The Reckoning of Time that the Anglo-Saxons called April eosturmonath, Ēostre's month, and that they held feasts in her honour. Beyond this, very little of her story survives.

Her figure is mostly reconstructed from etymology. Her name comes from a Proto-Indo-European root meaning 'to shine', which links her to Eos, the Greek goddess of dawn, to the Roman Aurora and to Uṣás, the dawn goddess of the Vedas. Jacob Grimm connected her with the east wind, Austri, mentioned in the Prose Edda. For this reason she is usually understood as a goddess of dawn and of the awakening of springtime fertility.

Worship and religion

According to Bede, the Anglo-Saxons held feasts in Ēostre's honour during the month named after her. With Christianization, this spring festival was eventually displaced by Easter. Today some Germanic neopagan groups venerate her.

Legacy

The name Ēostre survives in the English word Easter, and it has been suggested that it left traces in some English place names and personal names. Guido von List understood the festival of Ostern as the marriage of the Sun God and the Earth Goddess, and the name appears in modern popular culture and in neopagan festivals.

Ancient sources: Bede the Venerable, The Reckoning of Time (8th century), and the Prose Edda, which mentions the wind Austri.

Trivia

A month named after her. The Anglo-Saxons called April eosturmonath, Ēostre's month. Bede records this in the 8th century.
Inscriptions from 1958. In 1958, more than 150 Romano-Germanic votive inscriptions to a triad of goddesses were found near Morken-Harff in Germany, dating to around the 2nd and 3rd centuries CE.
