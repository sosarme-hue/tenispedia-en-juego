ancient Egyptian moon god

Iah

Iah is a lunar god of ancient Egyptian religion whose name simply means "Moon". He first appears in the Pyramid Texts, and his figure often blends with other moon deities, especially Khonsu and Thoth.

The myth

The surviving texts give him no lengthy story of his own, and his identity is defined mainly by his links with other gods. In the New Kingdom he was a minor lunar deity compared with Thoth or Khonsu, and because of that functional closeness he could be identified with either of them.

He was sometimes regarded as an adult form of Khonsu, and he was increasingly absorbed by him. His appearance resembled that of the child god, with the same lunar symbols, although he usually wore a full wig instead of the child's sidelock, and sometimes an atef crown topped with another symbol.

Later he became Iah-Djehuty, "god of the new moon", taking on the lunar aspect of Thoth, the god of knowledge, writing and calculation, and he is sometimes shown with an ibis head. He was also assimilated with Osiris, perhaps because the moon seems to renew itself over its monthly cycle.

Worship and religion

He had no single place of worship. Traces of a cult to him as a lunar deity survive on the west bank of the Nile near Thebes, and he often appears on amulets, although the sources do not describe rites or festivals.

Legacy

His name was borne by several real people. Ahmose I, founder of the Eighteenth Dynasty, had it in his name, as did his mother, Queen Ahhotep I, and other people called Ahmose.

Ancient sources: He is first mentioned in the Pyramid Texts of the Old Kingdom.

Trivia

A name meaning "Moon". His name, written jꜥḥ, simply means "Moon", which explains his role in the pantheon.
Moon segments as fractions. The segments of the Eye of Horus, linked to the god Thoth, were used as fractional symbols in Egyptian writing.
