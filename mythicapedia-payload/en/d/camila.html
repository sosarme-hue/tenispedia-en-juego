Volscian warrior maiden of the Aeneid

Camilla

Camilla is the warrior queen of the Volsci in Virgil's Aeneid, a swift huntress and fierce ally of Turnus in his fight against Aeneas. Her origin within the Roman tradition is uncertain, yet she became one of the most influential models of the warrior heroine in European literature.

The myth

In Virgil's account, King Metabus was driven from his throne and fled with his infant daughter in his arms. At the river Amasenus, fearing for the child's life, he bound her to a spear, vowed her to Diana as a warrior virgin, threw her to the far bank and then swam across himself.

The girl was suckled by a mare and raised as a huntress among shepherds in the woods. She was so swift that she could run over a wheat field without bending the stalks or over the sea without wetting her feet. As an adult she fought beside Turnus against the Trojans. Arruns, an Etruscan ally of Aeneas, stalked her on the battlefield and struck her down when she was distracted by pursuing the Trojan Chloreus. Diana sent her attendant Opis, who avenged Camilla by killing Arruns.

Legacy

Dante mentions her in the Inferno, among the heroines of Limbo, and Boccaccio devoted a chapter to her in De mulieribus claris. Torquato Tasso drew on her to create Clorinda in Jerusalem Delivered. Giovanni Bononcini wrote the opera Camilla (1696), which was a major international success.

Ancient sources: She appears in Books 7 and 11 of Virgil's Aeneid.

Trivia

Ritual name. Some scholars link her name to camillus, the young attendant who assisted the priest in Roman sacrifices.
Mistaken for Minerva. In an 1499 inventory, a figure in Botticelli's Pallas and the Centaur was called Camilla; by 1516 she was identified as Minerva.
