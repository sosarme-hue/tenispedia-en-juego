Titan of Greek mythology, father of the Sun, Moon and Dawn

Hyperion

Hyperion is a Titan of Greek mythology, son of Uranus and Gaia, linked to light and sight. He is best remembered as the father of the celestial lights Helios, Selene and Eos, and his figure is sometimes identified with the sun god himself.

The myth

According to tradition, Hyperion was one of the twelve Titans, children of Uranus (Sky) and Gaia (Earth). Hesiod makes him the husband of his sister Theia, with whom he had three children: Helios (Sun), Selene (Moon) and Eos (Dawn). Like most Titans, he has almost no stories of his own; his role is mainly that of father to these celestial deities.

He took part in the Titanomachy, the war against the Olympian gods, and after their defeat he was imprisoned in Tartarus as punishment. His name is usually read as 'the one above' or 'the one who walks on high', a link to the bright sky.

His relationship with Helios varies by author. In the Iliad, Homer often calls the sun god 'Helios Hyperion', while in the Odyssey and Hesiod's Theogony Hyperion is Helios' father. Later writers consistently treat them as separate figures, father and son. Diodorus Siculus gives a rationalizing version in which Hyperion is a mortal, the first person to understand the movements of the sun and moon. In his account, Hyperion's jealous brothers kill him along with his two children, who are later honored as the sun and moon.

Worship and religion

Sources indicate that Hyperion played an almost nonexistent role in Greek cult: no temples or festivals of his own are known, and he appears mainly in the list of the twelve Titans.

Legacy

The Romantic poet Friedrich Hölderlin took his name for the novel Hyperion (1797-1799), whose hero struggles between staying with the people he loves and joining the Greek fight for independence from Ottoman rule, hoping to restore Greece's lost beauty. John Keats left his epic poem Hyperion unfinished, with the Titan at its center. One of Saturn's moons also bears his name.

Ancient sources: He appears in Hesiod's Theogony, in Homer's Iliad and Odyssey, and in the Homeric Hymn to Demeter.

Trivia

A name of height. Hyperion derives from the Greek preposition hypér, 'above', so his name roughly means 'the one above'. Homer sometimes uses it as an epithet of Helios.
A possible Mycenaean mention. A Linear B tablet found at Knossos may contain his name, although it has also been proposed that it refers to Apollo.
