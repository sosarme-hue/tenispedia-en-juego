Roman god of the underworld

Dis Pater

Dis Pater, also called Dis or Dite, was the Roman god of the underworld, ruler of the dead alongside Proserpina. Originally linked to fertile farmland and mineral wealth, he was later equated with Pluto (Hades) and Orcus. His name came to mean the underworld itself, as in Dante's City of Dis in the Divine Comedy.

The myth

According to Roman tradition, Dis Pater became associated with death because precious metals and gems came from deep underground, where the realm of the dead lay. He was therefore identified with the Greek Pluto and took on some of his attributes. He was one of three sons of Saturn and Ops, along with Jupiter and Neptune, and he ruled the dead with his wife Proserpina, the Greek Persephone.

His name has several explanations. In De natura deorum, Cicero derived it from dives, 'rich', reading it as 'father of riches', an etymology some modern scholars think may have been ironic. Varro linked him with Februus, another infernal deity. In literature, his name was used as a poetic way of referring to death itself.

Worship and religion

In 249 and 207 BC, the Roman Senate ordered special festivals to appease Dis Pater and Proserpina, and a festival in his honour was celebrated every hundred years. According to legend, a round marble altar dedicated to both was miraculously found underground in the Campus Martius. Sacrifices were offered at it during the Secular Games and, after the festivities, it was buried again, a distinctly chthonic form of worship. It was rediscovered in 1886-1887 beneath the Corso Vittorio Emanuele in Rome.

Legacy

His name became a poetic synonym for death and a term for the underworld. Dante used it for the City of Dis, which comprises Lower Hell in the Divine Comedy.

Ancient sources: Cicero (De natura deorum), Julius Caesar (Commentaries on the Gallic War) and Varro mention this god.

Trivia

An altar underground. According to legend, the altar was found about 6 metres down while foundations were being dug. After three days of games, it was buried again.
Ancestors of the Gauls. Julius Caesar wrote that the Gauls claimed descent from Dis Pater, though he was probably referring to a Gaulish god that the Romans linked with him.
