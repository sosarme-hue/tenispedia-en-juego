son of Daedalus in Greek mythology

Icarus

Icarus is a figure of Greek myth, the son of the craftsman Daedalus, architect of the Cretan labyrinth. His story of a young man who flies too close to the sun and falls into the sea has become a symbol of reckless ambition and one of the most recognisable images of antiquity.

The myth

According to the tradition, King Minos of Crete held Daedalus and his son captive, suspecting that they had helped Theseus escape the labyrinth. To leave, Daedalus built two pairs of wings from feathers bound with thread and beeswax, and before takeoff he warned Icarus not to fly too low, lest the sea soak the feathers, nor too high, lest the sun melt the wax.

Carried away by the thrill of flight, the boy soared higher than he should have. The heat softened the wax, the feathers came loose, and Icarus flailed his bare arms in vain until he plunged into the sea and drowned. Daedalus mourned his son and named the nearby land Icaria; the sea there later became known as the Icarian Sea. The father reached Sicily, where he hung his wings as an offering in a temple of Apollo.

Pausanias offers a plainer version: father and son fled in small boats, Daedalus invented the sail, and Icarus, a clumsy sailor, was wrecked off Samos, where Heracles gave him burial. A scholion on Euripides adds that the boy thought himself greater than Helios, the Sun, who punished him with his rays.

Worship and religion

The sources say little about a cult of Icarus. His clearest religious link is the temple of Apollo in Sicily, where Daedalus left his wings as an offering to the god. Tradition places his tomb in the land that bears his name, but no shrines dedicated to him are known, and there is no living cult today.

Legacy

The myth has inspired poets and artists since antiquity. Ovid told it in the Metamorphoses, and his version shaped English literature, from Chaucer to Shakespeare and Milton. In the twentieth century, the fall of Icarus inspired W. H. Auden's Musée des Beaux Arts and William Carlos Williams's Landscape with the Fall of Icarus, both based on a painting attributed to Pieter Bruegel the Elder. In music, Iron Maiden released the single Flight of Icarus in 1983, and the 1979 film I... como Ícaro, directed by Henri Verneuil, takes its name from him. The idiom fly too close to the sun describes excessive ambition, and the psychologist Henry Murray proposed the term Icarus complex. The Icarian Sea and the island of Icaria still keep his name on the map.

Ancient sources: Pseudo-Apollodorus, Diodorus Siculus, Hyginus, Virgil and, above all, Ovid in the Metamorphoses, along with Lucian, who mentions him in passing.

Trivia

A ritual origin. According to the scholar Robert Graves, the myth may derive from a ceremony in which a person dressed in partridge wings was thrown from a cliff, called the pharmacos.
A dance with a labyrinth. On Crete an annual dance called the dance of the partridge was performed, with participants following a labyrinthine pattern drawn on the ground at the Knossos complex.
