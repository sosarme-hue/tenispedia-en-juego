daughter of Cecrops, heroine of Greek myth

Aglaurus

Aglaurus is one of the three daughters of Cecrops, the first king of Attica, in Greek mythology. Her story combines the tale of the hidden basket of Erichthonius with a legend of heroic self-sacrifice that explained Athens's military rites.

The myth

According to tradition, Aglaurus was the daughter of Cecrops and of another Aglaurus, daughter of Actaeus, and the sister of Herse and Pandrosus. Her son with Ares was Alcippe, although sources differ on her genealogy, and later versions also give her a son, Ceryx, with Hermes.

The best-known episode begins when Hephaestus tries to rape Athena and his semen falls on the earth, impregnating Gaia. Erichthonius is born, and Gaia refuses to raise him, so Athena entrusts him to the three sisters in a sealed basket with strict orders not to open it. Aglaurus and Herse disobey, see the child with a serpentine lower body, go mad and throw themselves from the Acropolis. According to Hyginus, they fell from a cliff. Another version says that an angry Athena dropped a rock that became Mount Lycabettus over them.

Ovid tells a different ending: Hermes loves Herse, and Aglaurus blocks his way into the house. Athena sends Envy, who possesses her, and the god turns her into a black stone.

Another tradition, recorded by Philochorus, presents her as a heroine. Athens was locked in a long war, and an oracle said it would end only if someone sacrificed themselves. Agraulos threw herself from the Acropolis and saved the city.

Worship and religion

The three sisters were honored at the Arrephoria in Athens as protectors of agriculture. Aglaurus had her own sanctuary, the Aglaurion, near the Acropolis, where young ephebes swore to defend their homeland when receiving their first arms. A festival and mysteries were held in her honor, and one Attic deme took her name. Porphyry adds that she was worshipped in Cyprus, where human sacrifices were offered to her until a late period, a custom replaced by a bull under King Diphilus.

Legacy

Dante places her on the second terrace of Purgatory, beside Cain, as an example of envy. She appears in many 17th-century paintings by Rubens and Jordaens depicting the discovery of Erichthonius.

Ancient sources: Euripides mentions her in his Ion, and Ovid recounts her transformation into stone in the Metamorphoses.

Trivia

Shared name. According to some sources, mother and daughter could both be called Agraulos, and the form Aglauros was more commonly used for the daughter.
A mention in Euripides. Euripides refers to her in his play Ion, the earliest mention of this character.
