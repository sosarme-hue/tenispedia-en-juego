Sumerian creation and flood myth

Eridu Genesis

The Eridu Genesis is a Sumerian account of how humanity was created, how the first cities of Mesopotamia arose, and how a great flood came. It is one of the oldest versions of the flood myth and a text that researchers often compare with the Biblical Genesis.

The myth

According to the Sumerian tradition, the gods created humankind, and the goddess Nintur called people out of their wandering nomadic life to build cities and temples. The first cities were Eridu, under Enki's leadership, followed by Badtibira, Larak, Sippar and Shuruppak, organised as economies without money.

In time, humans made so much noise that Enlil, lord of the universe, could not sleep, and he decided to destroy them with a flood. Enki warned Ziusudra, probably a priest, to build a boat to save his family and one pair of every animal. After a storm lasting seven days and nights, Ziusudra opened a window, sacrificed an ox and a sheep, and received "breath eternal" from the gods. In the Epic of Ziusudra he ends up living in Dilmun, where the sun rises.

Ancient sources: The Nippur tablet (c. 1600 BCE), a fragment from Ur and a bilingual Sumerian-Akkadian fragment from the Library of Ashurbanipal (c. 600 BCE), all written in Sumerian or Sumerian-Akkadian.

Trivia

A boat that drifts downriver. In the Epic of Ziusudra, the boat does not come to rest on a mountain but floats down the Euphrates into the Persian Gulf.
A word with three meanings. In Sumerian, KUR means mountain, but also land, especially a foreign country, and the Underworld. Akkadian translators read it as mountain.
