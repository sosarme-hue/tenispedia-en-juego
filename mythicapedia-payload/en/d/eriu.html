Celtic goddess of sovereignty of Ireland

Ériu

Ériu is a goddess of Irish mythology, a member of the Tuatha Dé Danann and the matron figure who gave Ireland its name. She matters because she is linked to the sovereignty of the island and to modern Irish national identity.

The myth

According to the stories, Ériu was a daughter of Ernmas, though some texts make her a daughter of Delbaeth or Fiachra. Together with her sisters Banba and Fódla she formed a triad of goddesses, and the three were linked to three brothers as husbands. She is named as the wife or lover of Mac Gréine, the Son of the Sun, and also of Elatha, a prince of the Fomorians, with whom she bore Bres. She also appears as the lover of Lugh, Bres's enemy.

One striking tradition concerns her upbringing. In the Rennes Dindsenchas, her foster-father Codal fed her on a peak called Benn Codail, and the land of Ireland rose towards the sky, nearly becoming that mountain. When the Milesians arrived from Galicia, each of the three sisters asked that her name be given to the country. Their request was granted, but Ériu's name became the main one in use.

Legacy

The name Ériu lies behind the usual English name for Ireland, combined with a Germanic word for land. Its older form also gave rise to the Greek Iernē and the Latin Hibernia. Banba and Fódla are still used as poetic names for Ireland. As a national personification, Erin appeared in nineteenth-century satirical press, especially in cartoons about British and Irish politics.

Ancient sources: She appears in the Banshenchas, a medieval text about legendary women of Ireland, and in the Rennes Dindsenchas, a medieval collection of tales about the origins of place names.

Trivia

Greek and Latin name. The older form of the goddess's name passed into Greek as Iernē and into Latin as Hibernia, the classical name of the island.
A mountain that grows. According to the Rennes Dindsenchas, the land of Ireland rose as Ériu was fed on the peak Benn Codail, almost becoming the mountain itself.
Three sisters, one country. Banba and Fódla also asked for their names to be given to the island, and their names are still used today as poetic forms for Ireland.
