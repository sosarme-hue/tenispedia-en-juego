Celtic goddess of fire, poetry and inspiration

Brigid

Brigid, or Brigit, is a goddess of pre-Christian Ireland, daughter of the Dagda and associated with poetry, healing, smithing and fire. She matters because she merged with the Christian saint of the same name, and many of her roles survive in the devotion to Saint Brigid.

The myth

In Irish tradition, Brigid belongs to the Tuatha Dé Danann and is the daughter of the Dagda. With Bres of the Fomorians she had a son, Ruadán. When Ruadán was killed fighting for the Fomorians, Brigid mourned him, and the battle account of Cath Maige Tuired credits her with inventing keening, a blend of wailing and singing. The same passage gives her a whistle used for night travel.

The texts also link her to animals and places: two oxen, Fea and Femen, and the kings of the boars and of the rams. Sources differ on her sisters. Cormac's Glossary, written by Christian scribes in the 9th century, speaks of two sisters also named Brigit, one a healer and one a smith, which suggests a triple goddess. She was said to have been born with a flame over her head.

Worship and religion

Poets (filid) revered her as their goddess, and Cormac's Glossary presents her as the patron of inspiration. At Kildare, according to Gerald of Wales, nineteen nuns kept a perpetual fire enclosed by a hedge that no man could cross. Sacred wells were also linked to her, and customs such as dressing the wells or tying cloth strips near trees still survive in some areas. Her feast, 1 February, grew out of Imbolc, the Gaelic celebration of spring's arrival.

Legacy

Her figure merged with Saint Brigid, who inherited many of her roles. In Ireland white cloths are placed in windows in her honour. Venus has a tholus named after her since 1985, and the name and its variants, such as Bridget and Brigitte, spread across Europe.

Ancient sources: Cormac's Glossary (9th century) and the Lebor Gabála Érenn (11th century), along with the battle account of Cath Maige Tuired, mention her.

Trivia

Perpetual flame. In the late 12th century Gerald of Wales described the perpetual fire at Kildare, kept by nineteen nuns and ringed by a hedge no man could cross.
A feature on Venus. In 1985 the International Astronomical Union named a tholus on Venus after Brigit, referring to the goddess rather than the saint.
