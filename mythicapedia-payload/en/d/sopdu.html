ancient Egyptian sky god and guardian of the eastern borders

Sopdu

Sopdu is an ancient Egyptian god of the sky and guardian of the eastern borders, shown as a falcon or as a Near Eastern warrior. He matters for his role in protecting Egypt's eastern frontier and for his link with the star Sirius.

The myth

According to tradition, Sopdu was the son of Sah and Sotis (Sopdet), and he was considered the husband of Jensit, a minor goddess and daughter of Ra. His name was linked to the heat that arrives when Sirius reappears on the horizon after a period of invisibility, so he was associated with sharpness and cutting.

In the Pyramid Texts he is connected with the king's teeth, which needed protection. In the Middle Kingdom he appears as a bearded warrior who guarded the pharaoh, and in the New Kingdom, in some places, he merged with Horus as Har-Septu. He was called Lord of the East and Smiter of Asiatics, and he watched over the Sinai deserts, the gateway from Asia into Egypt, and the turquoise mines there.

Worship and religion

The greatest cult centre lay in the easternmost nome of Lower Egypt, called Per-Sopdu, meaning place of Sopdu, whose capital later moved to Arsinoe, modern Fakussa. He also had shrines in Egyptian settlements in Sinai, such as the turquoise mines at Serabit el-Khadim. He was especially venerated during the Twenty-second Dynasty. The sources do not indicate whether his cult survives today.

Ancient sources: The Pyramid Texts mention him in relation to the king's teeth and to Horus-Sopdu, and later texts record his epithets as guardian of the east.

Trivia

A sharp name. His name is written with the hieroglyph for something pointed, a sharp triangle, and its literal translation is 'the sharp ones'.
Falcon on a standard. He was depicted as a falcon perched on a religious standard, sometimes with a flail over his shoulder.
