Celtic goddess of healing and thermal springs

Sirona

Sirona was a Celtic goddess of healing, linked to curative and thermal springs and worshipped in eastern Gaul, the Germanies and along the Danube. She matters because her snake and bowl of eggs offer a rare window into how Gallo-Roman communities understood cure and renewal.

The myth

No classical author mentions her, so her story survives only in inscriptions and reliefs. Her best-known partnership is with Apollo, often under the surname Grannus, a god of healing and thermal waters. Cassius Dio reports that the emperor Caracalla sought a cure from Apollo Grannus, as well as from Asclepius and Serapis.

Sirona often appears beside Apollo, yet many dedications name her alone, suggesting a standing of her own rather than being merely his companion. Her name is usually linked to the Indo-European root for 'star', though another theory connects it with a Celtic word for 'ox'. Her snake and eggs are read as emblems of healing and renewal, and in some images she holds grapes or grain.

Worship and religion

Her most important sanctuary lay at Hochscheid in the Hunsrück, in the land of the Treveri. In the 2nd century AD a temple was built around a spring that fed a small cistern, and visitors left figurines, coins and other offerings. Two cult statues from the site rank among the finest works of Romano-Celtic art. Her devotees included imperial procurators, town councillors, a legionary veteran, temple wardens and, frequently, women. According to one scholar, her cult was later displaced by that of Borvo over much of the Celtic world.

Ancient sources: No classical author names her; the evidence is some thirty Latin inscriptions and a number of reliefs.

Trivia

A difficult letter. Her name is spelled in several ways, including Đirona, because Latin could not easily represent its initial sound, possibly a Celtic combination of s or t.
A Gallo-Roman divine couple. Apollo often bears a Roman name with a Celtic surname, while Sirona keeps a purely Celtic name, a pattern common among these divine pairs.
