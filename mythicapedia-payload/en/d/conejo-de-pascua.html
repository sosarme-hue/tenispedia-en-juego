folkloric figure and symbol of Easter

Easter Bunny

The Easter Bunny, also called the Easter Rabbit or Easter Hare, is a folkloric figure and symbol of Easter who, according to custom, brings colored eggs, candy and sometimes toys to children on the night before Easter Sunday. Its roots lie in German traditions, where spring fertility blended with the Christian calendar.

The myth

In German tradition, the creature was not always a gift-giver. In its earliest form it acted almost as a judge, assessing whether children had behaved well at the start of the Easter season, much like the naughty-or-nice list attributed to Santa Claus. Over time it came to leave eggs and sweets in the nests that children built in gardens or in their caps and bonnets.

A German legend explains the custom: a poor woman, unable to offer sweets to her children, hid decorated eggs in the garden, and the children, seeing a rabbit, believed it had laid them. Another version, from German Catholic tradition, places the rabbit in the tomb with Jesus; it witnessed the Resurrection and, when it left the cave, spread the news to the children by handing out Easter eggs, whose eating had been forbidden during Lent. Christians of Hebrew tradition and much of southern Europe never adopted this legend.

The link to a spring goddess is a modern hypothesis. Jacob Grimm proposed it in 1835, connecting Easter with the goddess Ostara or Eostre. However, Bede, the only ancient writer to mention Eostre, does not link her to any animal, and there is no solid evidence for the connection.

Worship and religion

There was no cult devoted to the rabbit itself. The hare appeared in traditions of fertility and renewal after winter, and its reputation for abundant reproduction linked it to new life. It was a common motif in medieval Christian art, and it was believed that hares could reproduce without losing their virginity, which associated them with the Virgin Mary. Today its role is mainly familial and festive rather than religious.

Legacy

The Easter Bunny is now a widespread figure in popular culture across Europe and North America. German immigrants carried the custom to Britain and America, where in Pennsylvania it was called the Osterhase or Oschter Haws. In the nineteenth century, Easter cards, toys and books helped popularize it across Europe. Germany produces chocolate and sugar bunnies, which are sold in pastry shops in northern Europe and, through globalization, around the world.

Ancient sources: The earliest testimony is a German text from 1572 that mentions the custom, and Georg Franck von Franckenau's De ovis paschalibus records it in 1682 (other sources date this work to 1678).

Trivia

Three hares, one motif. One of the best-known medieval images is the three hares motif, which appears in a stained-glass window in Paderborn Cathedral in Germany.
A hermaphrodite hare. Pliny, Plutarch, Philostratus and Aelian all wrote that the hare was believed to be a hermaphrodite, an idea that helped link it to the Virgin Mary.
