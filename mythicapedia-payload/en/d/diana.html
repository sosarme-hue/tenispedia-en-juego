Roman goddess of the woods, the hunt and the Moon

Diana

Diana is the Roman goddess of woodlands, hunting and crossroads, linked to the Moon and to childbirth. Romans identified her with Artemis, although her cult had Italic roots and kept distinctive features.

The myth

Ancient accounts disagree about her parentage. According to Cicero, there were as many as three goddesses called Diana: the first, daughter of Jupiter and Proserpina, was said to have borne Cupid to Mercury; the second, the best known, was born of Jupiter and Latona; the third was the child of Upis and Glauca. The most widespread version makes her the daughter of Jupiter and Latona and the twin sister of Apollo. In Hyginus's telling, Latona gave birth to both on the island of Ortygia, clinging to an olive tree while Python pursued her; the island was later called Delos. Vulcan gave the twins their arrows.

Diana punished pride and insults to her honour harshly. Niobe boasted of having more children than Latona, and Diana and Apollo killed Niobe's children, except Chloris. Actaeon, who saw her bathing, was turned into a stag and torn apart by his own hounds. Callisto, who had vowed chastity, was transformed into a bear. Orion, who boasted he was the greater hunter, died at the goddess's hand, and she asked Jupiter to set him among the stars. Agamemnon, after killing a sacred deer, caused a storm that trapped the Greek fleet at Aulis. To appease the goddess he was prepared to sacrifice his daughter Iphigenia, but Diana wrapped the girl in mist, left a deer in her place and carried her to the land of the Taurians, where she made her priestess of her temple.

Other stories show her rewarding those who kept their vows. Procris, who had fled from Cephalus, received from Diana a javelin that never missed and the hound Laelaps. Hippolytus, son of Theseus, was brought back to life and given the name Virbius. In myth, Diana formed a trinity with Egeria, a water nymph and midwife, and with Virbius, god of the woods.

Worship and religion

Diana had a temple at Aricia, one of the oldest and most famous in ancient Italy, and a sanctuary by Lake Nemi, where she was worshipped as a triple goddess from the late 6th century BCE. Oak groves were especially sacred to her. A festival was held on 11 August. According to tradition, King Servius Tullius dedicated an altar to her on the Aventine Hill, outside the pomerium, which suggests her cult remained partly foreign, like that of Bacchus. At Aricia, worshippers left terracotta votives shaped like babies and wombs, and her temple also cared for puppies and pregnant dogs. Today some neopagan movements, including Wicca and Stregheria, venerate her.

Legacy

Her link to the Moon gave rise to titles such as Lucifera, "light-bearer", and Lucina, invoked at births. Cicero records that some regarded Diana and the Moon as one and the same, and Balbus counted her among the seven planets, which is why she was also called Omnivaga, "wandering everywhere". Virgil called Lake Nemi Trivia, and Horace addressed her as the "three-formed goddess". In Seneca's tragedy Medea, the sorceress calls on Trivia to cast her spells. Her image as a triple goddess, joined to the Moon and the underworld, has inspired contemporary neopagan imagery.

Ancient sources: Cicero discusses her in On the Nature of the Gods and Hyginus in his Fables, while Virgil, Horace and Catullus refer to her under her epithets.

Trivia

Three Dianas. According to Cicero, tradition distinguished up to three goddesses called Diana, each with different parents.
A deer in place of Iphigenia. In the myth, Diana replaced Iphigenia with a deer at the moment of sacrifice and carried her off to the land of the Taurians.
