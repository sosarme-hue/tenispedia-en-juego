Greek goddess venerated at the sanctuary of Ephesus

Artemis of Ephesus

A local form of the goddess Artemis worshipped in Ephesus, on the western coast of Anatolia, whose great temple was counted among the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World. Her cult, tied to the protection of the city, was equated with the Roman Diana.

The myth

Tradition linked the sanctuary to the Amazons, warrior women who, according to the stories, sought the goddess's protection there after fleeing punishment, first from Dionysus and later from Heracles. The historian Pausanias thought the site was older than the arrival of the Ionians, while Callimachus, in his Hymn to Artemis, credited the first sacred enclosure to the Amazons, who had venerated an image of the goddess there.

The Ephesian Artemis was considered distinct from the Hellenic version of the goddess, with her own features and objects. Her wooden cult image, or xoanon, was among the most revered.

Worship and religion

The temple, rebuilt several times, was the centre of religious and social life in Ephesus. It drew merchants, kings and visitors, who offered jewellery and goods. It also offered sanctuary to those fleeing persecution. The sanctuary was destroyed for the last time in the 3rd century AD, but worship of the goddess at the site continued until around 401 AD.

Legacy

The temple, burned in 356 BC, gave rise to the term herostratic fame, used for notoriety gained at any cost.

Ancient sources: Callimachus in his Hymn to Artemis, Pausanias and Diogenes Laertius, among other ancient authors.
