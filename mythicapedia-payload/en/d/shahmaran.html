half-woman, half-serpent creature from Turkish, Kurdish and Persian folklore

Shahmaran

Shahmaran is a half-woman, half-serpent creature from Turkish, Kurdish and Persian folklore, whose name means 'king of snakes'. Her story, told mainly in southeastern Turkey, is among the best-loved legends of the region.

The myth

According to the tradition, a young man named Jamasp, known in other versions as Cihanşah or Cami Sap, gets trapped in a cave while searching for honey with friends. Venturing further, he finds an underground garden of white serpents and Shahmaran, with whom he falls in love. She teaches him the uses of medicinal herbs.

Years later, Jamasp longs for the surface and promises to keep the place secret. When the king of Tarsus falls ill, his vizier learns that the cure requires Shahmaran's flesh. Jamasp reveals where she lives, and she gives instructions: her flesh must be cooked and the broth divided, the first portion going to the vizier and the rest to the sultan. The king recovers and the vizier dies, while Jamasp drinks the remaining broth, gains her wisdom and becomes a healer.

Worship and religion

The Kurds regard the serpent as a symbol of luck and strength, and images of Shahmaran still appear on glass and metalwork that they hang on their walls.

Legacy

Her story appears in One Thousand and One Nights, where the serpent queen is called Yamlîkhâ and the hero Hasîb, a name some scholars link to Jamasp. Her image decorates embroidery, rugs and jewellery in Turkey and Kurdish areas, and since around 2016 LGBTQ supporters have used it as a symbol. Dutch singer Sevdaliza wrote a song called 'Shahmaran', and a 2023 Netflix series is built around the legend.

Ancient sources: She appears in the Camasb-name and in the One Thousand and One Nights collection, as well as in many versions passed down orally.

Trivia

Yılankale in Adana. In Adana, in southern Turkey, the Yılankale («Snake Castle») is known locally as the home of Shahmaran.
A bathhouse with her name. In Tarsus there is a historic hamam called Şahmeran Hamam, where the legend places her death.
