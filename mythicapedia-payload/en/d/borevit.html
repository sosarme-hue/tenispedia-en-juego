Slavic god of the forests

Borevit

Borevit is a Slavic forest god, often compared with Pan in Greek myth and with Faunus or Silvanus in Roman religion. He matters because ancient sources mention him only rarely, and the surviving traditions present him as a guardian of travellers and a punisher of those who harmed nature, until Christianity turned him into a demon.

The myth

According to traditional accounts, Borevit could appear as a goat or as a bearded man, with stag's horns and large genitals, symbols of the fertility of nature. His most distinctive trait was the ability to change his height to suit his surroundings: tiny beside a mushroom and tall and imposing beside a tree.

Tradition credits him with looking after travellers who got lost in the forest and with punishing those who felled trees without restraint or mistreated animals. The guilty lost their way home, were led into marshes and swamps, or were struck by falling logs. A modern writer, Czesław Białczyński, names Leša, also called Borana, as Borevit's sister and wife, but this genealogy does not appear in the ancient texts.

Worship and religion

The early Slavs baked a ritual bread called borys in his honour, made with herbs and wild plants. With the arrival of Christianity, his figure was identified with Satan or reduced to that of a demon.

Legacy

His memory survives mainly in the demon Boruta, the figure into which the new religion transformed him. His name has been linked to Slavic words such as barč (beehive) and bartnik (beekeeper), though this etymology is only a hypothesis.

Ancient sources: Few ancient sources mention him: the Gesta Danorum and the Knýtlinga saga, which describe a statue of him with five faces and no weapons.

Trivia

A changing height. According to tradition, Borevit could grow or shrink until he matched the size of a mushroom or a tree.
Bread with herbs. The ritual bread borys was prepared with herbs and wild plants gathered in the countryside.
