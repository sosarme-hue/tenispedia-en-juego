Bolthorn

Bolthorn is a giant (jötunn) in Norse mythology, whose name is usually translated as 'evil-thorn'. He matters because of his place in the divine family tree: according to the sources, he is the father of Bestla, mother of Odin, and possibly also the father or grandfather of the wise being Mímir.

The myth

According to the Scandinavian sources, Bolthorn was a jötunn, one of the giants of Norse mythology. Tradition also presents him as a being derived from the primordial figure Ymir, placing him at the very origins of the Norse cosmos.

His offspring are at the heart of his role in the myths. In the Prose Edda, compiled by Snorri Sturluson in the 13th century, he appears as the father of Bestla, who married Borr and became the mother of Odin, Vili and Vé. In this way, the great god Odin would have giant blood through his mother's side.

The Poetic Edda offers a more debated detail. In the Hávamál, an unnamed giant, Bestla's brother and Bolthorn's son, taught Odin nine magical spells (galdr). Many scholars suggest that this anonymous figure may be Mímir, which would make Bolthorn his father or grandfather depending on the source.

The versions also differ in the name. The Prose Edda uses Bölþorn, while the Hávamál has Bölþor, a form that would have had no clear meaning to medieval Scandinavians.

Ancient sources: The Hávamál in the Poetic Edda, where he receives his only mention, and Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda, in the Gylfaginning.

Trivia

A maternal uncle. Several scholars see in Bolthorn a reflection of the bond with the maternal uncle, a tie that the Roman historian Tacitus had already noted as important in Germanic society.
A medieval proverb. A medieval Icelandic proverb said that men turn out most like their maternal uncles, an idea some apply to Odin, whose mother was a giantess.
