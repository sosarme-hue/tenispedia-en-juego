Ancient Buddha in Buddhist tradition

Koṇāgamana

Koṇāgamana is one of the Buddhas who came before Gautama and the second of the five Buddhas of the present kalpa, known as the bhadrakalpa or Auspicious Aeon. He belongs to Buddhist tradition, which remembers him as a teacher from an earlier age of the world.

The myth

According to Theravāda tradition, his life story appears in the twenty-third chapter of the Buddhavaṃsa, one of the books of the Pali Canon. He was born in Sobhavati, capital of King Sobha, within the Subhagavati Park. His parents were the Brahmin Yannadatta and Uttara. He spent three thousand years at court, in three successive palaces, and his chief wife Rucigatta bore him a son named Satthavaha.

He renounced the world riding an elephant and practised austerities for six months. He received milk-rice from the Brahmin Aggisoma's daughter and grass for his seat from the grain-watcher Tinduka. He attained enlightenment under an udumbara tree and died at the age of thirty thousand in Pabbatārāma. Sources do not agree on his place in the count: some traditions make him the twenty-sixth of twenty-nine named Buddhas, others the twenty-sixth of twenty-eight.

Worship and religion

The sources link him to a specific place. According to the Chinese pilgrim Xuanzang, his relics were kept in a stupa at Nigali Sagar, in what is now Kapilvastu District in southern Nepal. In 249 BCE, Emperor Ashoka visited the site, enlarged the stupa dedicated to the Kanakamuni Buddha and erected a pillar bearing an inscription in Brahmi.

Legacy

The Ashoka pillar, still partly buried in the ground, is the most visible physical trace of his veneration. His name belongs to the list of the Buddhas of the present kalpa, alongside Kakusandha, Kassapa, Gautama and Maitreya.

Ancient sources: The Buddhavaṃsa of the Pali Canon recounts his biography, and Ashoka's inscription at Nigali Sagar documents his veneration in the 3rd century BCE.

Trivia

The Wednesday pedestal. According to Burmese mythology he was born on a Wednesday, so he occupies the pedestal of that day.
Twenty cubits tall. The Jataka tales give him a height of twenty cubits, roughly nine metres.
