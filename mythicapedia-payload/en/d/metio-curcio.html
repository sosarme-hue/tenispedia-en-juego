Sabine chief in early Roman legend

Metius Curtius

Metius Curtius is a semi-legendary figure from Rome's early history, a Sabine leader from Cures serving King Titus Tatius. He matters because he embodies the war between Romans and Sabines that followed the Rape of the Sabine Women.

The myth

According to the tradition recorded by Livy, Curtius took part in the war sparked by the Rape of the Sabine Women. When the Romans, pressed by the Sabines, retreated to the Capitol, Curtius defeated Hostus Hostilius in single combat. Hostus was a companion of Romulus and an ancestor of Tullus Hostilius, Rome's future king.

During the battle of Lake Curtius, the warrior rode ahead of his troops and nearly sank with his horse into the treacherous mud. He escaped by a miracle, and the lake was named after the event. Plutarch places Hostus's death in the battle but does not mention the duel with Curtius.

Ancient sources: Livy describes the duel and the battle of Lake Curtius in his History of Rome; Plutarch mentions the death of Hostus Hostilius.

Trivia

A lake with a name. Lake Curtius took its name from the episode in which the rider nearly sank into the mud with his horse.
Many spellings. His name appears in several Latin forms, such as Mettius, Mevius or Metius Curtius.
