magical object of Finnish mythology

Sampo

The sampo is a magical artifact in Finnish mythology, a mill that produces grain, salt and gold without end. It matters because it condenses the myth of the prosperity of the land of Kaleva, and it has been read in many ways as a symbol of the order of the world.

The myth

In the Kalevala, the smith Ilmarinen forges the sampo in order to win the hand of the maiden of Pohjola. Louhi, the sorceress mistress of Pohjola, wants to use it to feed and enrich her people, and she guards it closely.

Later, the heroes of Kaleva, led by Väinämöinen, set out to recover it. During the battle at sea the mill breaks and most of it sinks beneath the waves. Väinämöinen manages to save some fragments, which he buries on the way home; according to the tale, these pieces guarantee the future prosperity of Kaleva and, allegorically, of Finland.

Legacy

Its most lasting presence lies in the work of Lönnrot, who compiled the Kalevala and read the sampo as a mill that made flour, salt and gold out of thin air. From the early twentieth century, scholars such as Setälä, Harva and Pekka Ervast argued that it was a world pillar or world tree; Julius Krohn identified it with the Sun, and others compared it to a compass, an astrolabe or a decorated shield. Its name also survives in Estonian runic songs, where it appears as Sammas.

Ancient sources: The best-known versions come from runic songs collected in Karelia, Savonia and Ostrobothnia, and from the Kalevala; the oldest written version of the theft of the sampo was recorded from the Forest Finns in 1817.

Trivia

Other names. In the runic songs the sampo also goes by the names Samppu, Samppi or Kirjokansi.
Similarity to Yggdrasil. In Estonian songs the Sammas is an oak or an ash standing at the center of the world, much like the Norse Yggdrasil.
