founder and first queen of Carthage

Dido

Dido, also known as Elissa, was the legendary founder and first queen of Carthage, the Phoenician city on the coast of North Africa in present-day Tunisia. She is best known from Virgil's Aeneid, where her doomed love for the Trojan hero Aeneas made her one of the most famous women in Western literature.

The myth

According to the tradition, Dido was a daughter of Mattan I, king of Tyre, and sister of Pygmalion, who inherited the throne, and of the young Anna. She married Sychaeus (or Acerbas), a priest of the temple of Melqart, the god the Greeks identified with Heracles. When Pygmalion learned that Sychaeus had hidden a great treasure, he had him murdered. Dido, who had been used as a pawn, gained control of the riches and fled Tyre with her sister, a band of maidens and her husband's friends, until they reached the African coast.

There she asked Iarbas, king of the Gaetulians, for a piece of land that could be enclosed by an oxhide. She cut the hide into thin strips and encircled a hill, Byrsa, where she built a fortress that later grew into Carthage, a name meaning 'New City' in Phoenician. Timaeus dated the founding to 814 BC. According to Justin, who summarized Trogus Pompeius, Iarbas demanded her hand in marriage, and Dido, unwilling to betray Sychaeus, threw herself onto a funeral pyre.

The best-known version is Virgil's. Juno and Venus bring Dido and Aeneas together, but Jupiter sends Mercury to remind the hero that his destiny lies in Italy. Aeneas leaves, and Dido, in despair, orders a great pyre built and drives his sword into her breast. In the poem she foretells the enmity between Carthage and Rome, which would lead to the Punic Wars and to the avenger Hannibal. In the underworld, Aeneas finds her among the souls of those who died for love, but she turns away without listening to him. Ovid imagined her, in his Heroides, writing a letter of farewell to Aeneas before her death.

Worship and religion

According to Justin, after her suicide the Carthaginians deified her and worshipped her as a goddess for as long as Carthage endured. No detailed records of her rites survive, and there is no evidence that her cult continues today.

Legacy

Dido's story has inspired painting, literature and opera continuously from the Renaissance into the twenty-first century. It also appears in mathematics: the clever use of the oxhide gave rise to a famous problem, that of finding the shape of the largest area that can be enclosed by a given perimeter, known as the isoperimetric problem.

Ancient sources: Timaeus of Tauromenium (lost work), Trogus Pompeius as preserved in Justin's epitome, Virgil's Aeneid and Ovid's Heroides.

Trivia

A name with Phoenician roots. Elissa, her alternative name, seems related to El, the Phoenician creator god. In Greek it was rendered as Theiossô.
Two forms of one name. Virgil used Dido in the nominative and Elissa in the other cases, a distinction inherited from earlier tradition.
