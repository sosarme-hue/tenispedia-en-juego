Greek goddess of rage and madness

Lyssa

Lyssa is the Greek spirit of rage, fury and madness, and in animals the frenzy caused by rabies. In Greek tragedy she is the force that drives gods and heroes into violent, uncontrolled madness.

The myth

In Euripides' Heracles, Lyssa is the daughter of Nyx and of the blood of Uranus, while the Roman writer Hyginus lists her, under the name Ira, among the children of Aether and Terra. In Euripides' play, Hera orders Lyssa, through the messenger Iris, to drive Heracles mad. Lyssa protests and tries in vain to persuade Iris to refuse; in the end she gives in and sends the hero into a frenzy in which he kills his wife and children.

Aeschylus names her as the agent Dionysus sent to madden the daughters of Cadmus, who tore their kinsman Pentheus apart. Euripides pictures her with serpents on her head and blazing eyes, and in some depictions she appears as a dog. Valerio Flaco places her in a retinue that includes Fear, Deceit and Rage, and she is closely linked with Mania, Madness, the two figures often confused.

Legacy

Several Greek vases show her at the death of Actaeon, whose own hounds, driven rabid, turn on him. On an Apulian vase from about 350 BC she has wings and replaces Dionysus as the cause of Lycurgus' madness. The viral genus Lyssavirus, which includes the agent of rabies, takes its name from her, and the feminine name Alyssa has her as its etymological origin.

Ancient sources: Euripides (Heracles), Aeschylus, Hyginus and Valerius Flaccus mention her, each with a different account of her parentage and role.

Trivia

Origin of the name Alyssa. The feminine name Alyssa derives from Lyssa through the alyssum flower, which was named after rabies because its seeds were used, unsuccessfully, to treat the disease.
A virus named after her. The viral genus Lyssavirus, which includes the causative agent of rabies, was named after this goddess.
