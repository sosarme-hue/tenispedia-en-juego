Horned god of Wicca and Neopaganism

Horned God

The Horned God is a male deity worshipped in Wicca and other modern Neopagan traditions, linked to nature, hunting, fertility and the cycle of life, death and rebirth. He is the consort of the Triple Goddess, and his name is a syncretic term coined in the early twentieth century, drawing on horned gods from many different cultures.

The myth

In Wiccan belief the Horned God is born in winter, fathers a child with the Goddess, dies in autumn and winter, and is reborn at Yule. This yearly cycle is marked by the eight sabbats of the Wheel of the Year, though groups disagree on which festival marks his death: some choose Lammas, others Mabon or Samhain. In traditional British Wicca he is a dual god of light and dark, summer and winter, known as the Oak King and the Holly King. Some systems split him into three aspects: the Youth or Warrior, the Father and the Sage. As Lord of Death, he is said to comfort the dead and rule the underworld, or Summerland, until they are reborn.

His horns are often read as a symbol of this duality, and his imagery sometimes borrows antlers from the Green Man or solar rays from the Sun God.

Worship and religion

Within Wicca he is honoured in the seasonal rituals that mirror his relationship with the Goddess through the year. Gardner described him as an under-god, a mediator between an unknowable supreme deity and humanity. He remains a central figure for many Neopagan groups today.

Legacy

The figure draws on Margaret Murray's now-discredited theory of a pan-European fertility cult, and its image has merged with that of the Devil. Eliphas Lévi's 1855 Baphomet drawing, inspired by the Devil card of the Marseille tarot, helped shape that fusion. The Horned God also recurs in fantasy literature and has been explored in several psychological theories.

Trivia

A statue with a false reputation. Robert Howard displayed a wooden head of Atho that he claimed was 2200 years old. The piece was stolen in 1967, and his son later admitted that Howard had carved it himself.
Relatives in English folklore. Puck, Robin Goodfellow and the Green Man are considered related to the Horned God, although they are not always shown with horns.
