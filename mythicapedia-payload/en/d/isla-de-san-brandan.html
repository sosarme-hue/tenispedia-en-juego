phantom island of the North Atlantic linked to Saint Brendan

Saint Brendan's Island

A mythical island in the North Atlantic that appears and vanishes in the mist, tied to the Irish monk Saint Brendan and his quest for the earthly paradise. Its legend crossed centuries and maps, and in the Canary Islands people still speak of it as San Borondón.

The myth

According to tradition, Saint Brendan of Clonfert set out with fourteen monks in a small boat to search for the earthly paradise. For seven years they sailed the ocean, faced sea monsters and found many islands, until they reached a land of marvellous fruit.

One of the best-known episodes takes place on an island with no vegetation that is really the giant sea monster Jasconius. The monks celebrate Easter on its back, but when they light a fire to roast a lamb the creature wakes and moves off. Later, obedient to Brendan, it carries them close to Paradise. The island is identified with the fish, and like it, vanishes from those who search for it.

The Latin Navigatio offers a more precise route than the Anglo-Norman poem by Benedeit, where the journey is circular. Scholars debate whether the tale reflects real voyages to Iceland, Greenland or Newfoundland, or is an allegory. A modern reading takes the crystal column to be an iceberg and the coagulated sea to be a frozen sea.

Legacy

The island appears on medieval and Renaissance maps, such as the Hereford map, the Pizigani brothers' chart, Behaim's globe and Mercator's atlas, placed in very different spots. An Argentine bay, Samborombón, is thought to have been named by sailors from Magellan's expedition. In the Canary Islands, the island called San Borondón still attracts sightings and popular tradition.

Ancient sources: The Latin Navigatio Sancti Brendani Abbatis from the ninth century, and Benedeit's Anglo-Norman poem, around 1121.

Trivia

A bay with its name. Members of Magellan's expedition are thought to have named a bay on the Argentine coast Samborombón, attributing its nearly semicircular shape to a detachment from the mainland.
Official sightings. In 1566, the First Regent of the Royal Canary Islands Court ordered the justices of La Palma, El Hierro and La Gomera to investigate the phenomenon.
