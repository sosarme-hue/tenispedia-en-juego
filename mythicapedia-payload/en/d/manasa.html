Hindu goddess of snakes and poison

Manasa

Manasa is a Hindu goddess of snakes and poison, worshipped mainly in Bengal and northeastern India. She is invoked to prevent and cure snakebites, and also for fertility and prosperity.

The myth

According to tradition, Manasa is the sister of Vasuki, king of the Nagas, and the wife of the sage Jaratkaru, with whom she had a son, Astika. Her origin varies by source. In the Puranas she is born from the mind of the sage Kashyapa, which gives her name its meaning, "mind-born". In the Mangalkavyas of Bengal, however, her father is Shiva. The stories always present her as a figure shaped by rejection: her father scorns her, her husband leaves her after a quarrel, and her stepmother Chandi wounds her, leaving her blind in one eye.

Jaratkaru, a hermit who had resolved never to marry, agreed to the union so that his son could free his ancestors from their misery through funeral rites. Astika fulfilled that duty and later saved the Naga race from extinction during the sacrifice ordered by King Janamejaya. In another episode, Manasa saved Shiva after he drank the poison from the churning of the ocean of milk, and for this she is venerated as the destroyer of poison.

When she came down to Earth to win human devotees, the people mocked her. Manasa punished those who refused to honour her with calamities, but the merchant Chand Sadagar, a devotee of Shiva, resisted. She killed six of his sons and, on the wedding night of his youngest, Lakhindar, she killed him too.

Worship and religion

She is worshipped mainly in Bengal and northeastern India, where devotees ask for protection from snakebite and blessings of fertility and prosperity. Her cult is still alive. Originally a tribal, or adivasi, goddess, she entered the Hindu pantheon as a minor deity worshipped by lower caste groups, and later, in the 14th century, was absorbed into Shaivism as a goddess of fertility and marriage rites.

Legacy

Her stories inspired the Mangalkavyas, devotional poems composed in Bengal between the 13th and 18th centuries. In Assam, her story is performed in the ojapali dance, based on the Padmapurana.

Ancient sources: The Puranas, the first scriptures to speak of her birth; the Mahabharata, which mentions her in passing under the name Jaratkaru; and the Bengali Mangalkavyas, such as Bipradas Pipilai's Manasa Vijaya (1495).

Trivia

One eye. According to the story, Chandi burned one of her eyes, which is why she is known as "the goddess of one eye".
Born of the lotus. In some texts from Bengal and Assam she is called Padmavati, because Shiva's seed reaches the land of the serpents through the stalk of a lotus.
