Himalayan cryptid, known as the Abominable Snowman

Yeti

The yeti is a large, bipedal, hairy ape-like creature which, according to reports, inhabits the Himalayan mountains. It belongs to the folklore of Sherpa, Tibetan and Nepali peoples and has become one of the best-known creatures in Western cryptozoology.

The myth

Accounts describe the yeti as a large, bipedal being covered with brown, grey or white hair, sometimes with large, sharp teeth. Tibetan lore distinguishes several kinds: the nyalmo, with black fur and the largest and fiercest; the chuti, of medium size, living at lower altitudes; and the rang shim bombo, with reddish-brown fur and the smallest.

The name seems to come from Tibetan words meaning something like "man-bear" or "rocky bear". Himalayan peoples use other names such as migoi, "wild man", or kang admi, "snow man". One local belief holds that anyone who sees a yeti dies or is killed.

Among the Lepcha, a "glacier being" was said to be a god of the hunt, and followers of the Bön religion believed the blood of the wild man had ritual uses. Later the yeti was absorbed into Tibetan Buddhism as a nonhuman animal, human enough to follow the Dharma. Some stories show it as a helper and disciple of religious figures, while seeing one is often considered a bad omen.

In the nineteenth century, the explorer B. H. Hodgson described a dark-haired creature fleeing from his guides, and in 1899 Laurence Waddell recorded footprints he attributed to a bear. In the twentieth century, Everest expeditions multiplied the sightings, although most come from second-hand accounts.

Worship and religion

In the Buddhist monasteries of Khumjung, Pangboche and Namche Bazaar in Nepal, scalps attributed to the yeti are kept as relics. In 1961 the Khumjung one was shown to belong to a serow, a goat-antelope relative of the chamois, rather than an unknown primate. It is a cap worn by monks in ritual dances, yet the monks venerate it for its age of more than three centuries.

Legacy

The yeti appears in Hergé's comic Tintin in Tibet and in numerous films, from The Abominable Snowman (1957) to recent Indian and European productions. Its name is often used to describe an elusive being that is hard to prove.

Ancient sources: Ancient written sources are scarce; the yeti appears mainly in oral traditions of the Himalayan peoples and Tibetan stories, without an early written corpus of its own.

Trivia

A doubtful name. The term "Abominable Snowman" arose in 1921 after a British journalist mistranslated the Tibetan word metoh, meaning "man-bear".
Goat relics. An analysis of the Khumjung relic showed it came from a serow, a relative of the chamois, not from a primate.
