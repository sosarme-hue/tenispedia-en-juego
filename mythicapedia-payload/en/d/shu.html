Egyptian god of dry air and light

Shu

Shu is the Egyptian god of dry air, light and the space between sky and earth. He belongs to the Heliopolitan Ennead, and his task of holding the sky apart from the earth keeps the cosmic order intact.

The myth

In Heliopolitan cosmology, Shu is the son of Atum, the creator god, and he is paired with his twin sister Tefnut. Together they produced Nut, the sky, and Geb, the earth. When the two were locked in an embrace, Shu forced them apart and kept them separate, creating the space in which life can exist. From this separation come the world's dualities: above and below, light and darkness, good and evil. Before the separation, Nut had already given birth to Isis, Osiris, Nephthys and Set, which makes Shu the grandfather of Osiris and Isis.

He is usually shown as a man wearing one or more ostrich feathers on his head, a symbol of lightness and emptiness. Sometimes he appears with a lion's head when he acts as a defender of the sun god. A much later tale says that Tefnut, after a quarrel, went off to Nubia. Shu missed her, but she had turned into a cat that destroyed anyone who came near. Thoth eventually persuaded her to return.

Worship and religion

Originally from Leontopolis, he was worshipped at Heliopolis, Dendera, Memphis and Edfu, and he enjoyed great veneration in the Ramesside period. As a funerary god he acted as a prosecutor at the Judgment of Osiris, and he appears in the Coffin Texts and the Book of the Dead. Like the rest of Egyptian religion, his cult faded away centuries ago.

Legacy

The Greeks linked him with Atlas, the Titan who held up the celestial vault, because both are shown holding up the sky. They also identified him with Agathodaimon. During Roman rule he was associated with Ra, and at Tinis he was identified with Onuris.

Ancient sources: The Coffin Texts present him as a creator god, and the Book of the Dead records his role in the Judgment of Osiris.

Trivia

Ostrich feathers. The ostrich feather he wears on his head symbolises lightness and emptiness, ideas tied to his role as the air.
A counterweight with his face. A bronze menat counterweight, dated between 722 and 332 BCE, shows his head beside that of Tefnut. It was believed that the two deities protected the wearer of the necklace from worldly dangers.
