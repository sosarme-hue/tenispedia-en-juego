Canaanite god of death and the Underworld

Mot

Mot is the Canaanite god of death and the Underworld, also linked to the dry heat of summer. Known mainly from the texts found at Ugarit, he appears as a rival of the rain god Baal and embodies death itself in the region's pantheon.

The myth

According to the Ugaritic texts, Mot is a son of El and lives in a city called Hmry, where his throne is a pit and his inheritance is filth. When Baal invites him to a feast, Mot replies that his appetite is like that of lions in the wilderness and threatens to devour Baal himself.

In a later episode, Mot seems to make good on his threat. Baal's sister Anat seizes him, splits him with a blade, winnows him, burns him, grinds him and scatters what remains over a field for birds to eat. El dreams that Baal is alive and sends the sun goddess Shapash to bring him back, since the land has dried up. After seven years, Mot returns seeking revenge and demands one of Baal's brothers. After a combat, Shapash rebukes him, warning that El will turn against him, and Mot concedes.

In the Phoenician account preserved by Eusebius from Philo of Byblos, who cites Sanchuniathon, Mot is a son of El, whom the Phoenicians called Thanatos and Pluto. In an earlier cosmogony, Mot arises from a wind joined with its desire, and from him come light, the sun, the moon and the stars.

Legacy

The word mot means 'death' in Ugaritic and belongs to a family of cognates in other Semitic and Afro-Asiatic languages, such as Arabic mawt and Hebrew mavet. Some scholars have proposed that Passover may have connections with the myth of Mot and Baal, a view other scholars dispute.

Ancient sources: The Ugaritic texts of the Baal cycle, the Phoenician cosmogony of Sanchuniathon transmitted by Philo of Byblos, and some references in the Hebrew scriptures.

Trivia

A name for death. In Ugaritic, mot simply means 'death', and the same root appears in languages such as Arabic and Hebrew.
