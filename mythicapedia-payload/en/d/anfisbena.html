two-headed serpent of universal mythology

Amphisbaena

The amphisbaena is a mythical serpent with a head at each end of its body, found in Greco-Roman tradition and in European medieval bestiaries. It matters because it embodies the lasting image of the two-headed monster, and because its supposed uses in folk medicine made it a creature of superstition.

The myth

According to Greco-Roman tradition, the amphisbaena was born from the drops of blood that fell from the head of the Gorgon Medusa as Perseus flew over the Libyan Desert carrying it. Lucan counts it among the serpents that Cato's army met on its march through Libya, and it was said to feed on the corpses left behind.

The creature was described as a serpent with a head at both ends. Isidore of Seville said it could move in either direction with a circular motion, while Nicander described it as slow and dull-eyed. Pliny the Elder gave it venom, and tradition also credited it with the ability to rejoin itself if cut in two.

Medieval bestiaries and later drawings often showed it with bird's feet, feathered wings or horns, and sometimes with the second head at the end of its tail. Some authors placed it almost always in Africa.

Legacy

It appears in poems by John Milton, Alexander Pope, Percy Bysshe Shelley and Alfred Tennyson, and Dante lists it among the reptiles that torment thieves in the Inferno. Milton turns some fallen angels into amphisbaenae in Paradise Lost. Jorge Luis Borges includes it in his Book of Imaginary Beings. In popular culture it appears in Dungeons & Dragons and in video games such as La-Mulana. The name of a group of real lizards, the amphisbaenians, comes from it.

Ancient sources: Nicander, Lucan, Pliny the Elder and Isidore of Seville mention it among the serpents and creatures of classical tradition.

Trivia

Greek name. The name comes from Greek and means roughly 'going in two directions', referring to its two heads.
Snake of India. A Portuguese codex shows a two-headed snake of India with a caption saying it is harmless.
