dream-devouring creature of Japanese folklore

Baku

The baku is a Japanese supernatural being said to devour nightmares and evil spirits. Originating in the Chinese mo, its composite and shifting appearance has made it one of the most beloved yōkai in Japan.

The myth

According to legend, the baku was made from the spare pieces left over when the gods finished creating all the other animals. It is a chimera whose form varies: early descriptions give it an elephant's trunk and tusks, rhinoceros ears, a cow's tail, a bear's body and tiger's paws, while classical woodblock prints favour an elephant's head with tiger claws.

Its defining trait is its ability to devour dreams. A child who wakes from a nightmare can call out three times, "Baku-san, come eat my dream", and the creature will come into the room to swallow the bad dream so the child can sleep peacefully. The tale warns, however, that it should be summoned sparingly: if it remains hungry, it may also devour one's hopes and desires, leaving a person to live an empty life.

Worship and religion

The baku appears in Japanese shrines and temples, carved in wood or stone, and its image was placed at the bedside as a talisman against bad dreams. In the 1910s it was common for Japanese children to keep one by their bed. Today it is not an active cult but a popular custom and a protective figure for children.

Legacy

In modern usage baku also names the Malayan tapir, the only tapir species in Asia. The creature has inspired characters in anime, manga, video games and card games, including Drowzee and Munna in Pokémon, Tapirmon in Digimon and Doremy Sweet in Touhou. The writer Lafcadio Hearn described it in the Meiji period.

Ancient sources: The baku is described in the 17th-century Japanese manuscript Sankai Ibutsu, which presents it as a shy Chinese chimera that protects against pestilence and evil, though without the ability to eat nightmares.

Trivia

A name with two meanings. In Japan, the word baku refers both to the mythical creature and to the Malayan tapir, and is used with either meaning.
A phrase to summon it. Japanese children would repeat three times the phrase "Baku-san, come eat my dream" to ask for help after a nightmare.
