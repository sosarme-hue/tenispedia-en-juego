underworld of Mesopotamian mythology

Irkalla

Irkalla is the underworld of Sumerian and Babylonian mythology, a dark and vast realm to which all the dead descend. The name also designates its ruler, the goddess Ereshkigal, so the place and its sovereign are closely bound together.

The myth

According to Mesopotamian tradition, Irkalla lay deep beneath the earth, even below the Abzu, the body of freshwater believed to lie under the world. It was described as a gloomy, empty place of dim light where souls continued a faded version of earthly life. Reaching it meant passing through seven gates, each guarded by the gatekeeper Neti, who collected a toll and made travellers remove their garments and ornaments at every threshold.

In the older stories the ruler was Ereshkigal, who governed alone from the palace of Ganzir. Her husband varied between versions: Gugalanna in the early tales and Nergal, god of war, in later ones, where he was sent below and seduced the goddess, eventually becoming lord of the underworld. Her messenger was Namtar.

The texts note that the dead were neither judged nor punished or rewarded for their deeds. Spirits wandered forever, and those who died young, by accident or violence, might return as ghosts. The dead survived on nothing but dust, so their descendants had to pour libations for them.

Worship and religion

Relatives poured libations through clay pipes into the grave, and having many children was considered essential to ensure the dead had food and drink. Those who died without descendants suffered most. Rich tombs held offerings also meant for Utu and the Anunnaki, to win the dead special favours in the afterlife.

Ancient sources: It appears in the Sumerian myth Nergal and Ereshkigal and in the tale of Inanna and Utu, as well as in Akkadian and Babylonian texts that record its names and the figure of Ereshkigal.

Trivia

Many names. The Sumerians used many names for the underworld, including Arali, Kukku, Kur, Kigal and Ganzir, which later passed into Akkadian.
Land of no return. One of its best-known Akkadian names, erṣetu la târi, means the "earth of no return", summing up the belief that no one came back from there.
