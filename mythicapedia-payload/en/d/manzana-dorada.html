Golden apple

The golden apple is a mythical object that appears in legends from several cultures, usually guarded or stolen by an antagonist and recovered by a hero. Its presence in Greek, Norse and Celtic myths, and in European folk tales, makes it one of the most recurring motifs in folklore.

The myth

In Greek tradition, the huntress Atalanta agreed to marry only the man who could beat her in a footrace. Hippomenes, also called Melanion, asked Aphrodite for help, and she gave him three golden apples; dropping them one at a time, he distracted Atalanta, won the race and her hand.

In the Garden of the Hesperides, in the west, golden apples grew that granted immortality. Hera had placed there Ladon, a hundred-headed dragon that never slept, and the eleventh labour of Heracles was to steal them.

A golden apple also set off the Trojan War: Eris, left out of the wedding of Peleus and Thetis, threw one inscribed "for the most beautiful". Paris had to choose between Hera, Athena and Aphrodite, and he picked the last.

In Norse mythology, Iðunn's apples kept the gods young until Loki handed her over to a giant and had to win her back. In Celtic lore, the island of Avalon is named, according to a medieval interpretation, after the word for apple.

Legacy

Wagner gave them a place in Das Rheingold, where Freia, keeper of the apples, is claimed by the giants. Stravinsky set golden apples in the garden of The Firebird, and Yeats, Eudora Welty and David Mitchell used them in poems and novels. The modern religion Discordianism takes Eris and her apple as a symbol. In many languages, the word for orange comes from "golden apple", as in Italian pomodoro for tomato.

Trivia

A famous inscription. The apple thrown by Eris bore the Greek inscription "for the most beautiful", which sparked the judgement of Paris.
Words with history. In several languages, the name of the orange derives from "golden apple", such as Greek chrisomilia or German Pomeranze.
