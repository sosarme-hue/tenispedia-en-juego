Sesostris

Sesostris is the name the Greeks gave to a legendary Egyptian pharaoh credited with vast military campaigns across Asia and Europe. He matters because his greatly enlarged figure was built from Greek accounts and is now seen as a blend of memories of several real pharaohs.

The myth

According to Herodotus, who passed on what Egyptian priests told him, Sesostris carried out two great campaigns: a naval one against peoples of the Red Sea and a land expedition that crossed Syria and Asia Minor and reached Scythia and Thrace. On his conquests he had monuments set up that marked peoples who surrendered without a fight with a female sexual image, as a form of mockery.

On his return he left colonists by the river Phasis, said to be the ancestors of the Colchians. Later, the tradition tells how his brother betrayed him by setting fire to his palace, and Sesostris saved his family by laying two of his children over the flames as a bridge. He is also credited with dividing the land, the origin of the land tax and the roots of geometry, as well as colossal statues before the temple of Hephaestus. Herodotus adds that his successor was Pheron.

Legacy

The name survived in classical tradition, which credited him with conquering the world and founding institutions. The Alexander Romance calls Alexander the new Sesostris, and a 1728 play drew on his legendary life.

Ancient sources: Herodotus (Histories, Book II) is the main source; then Diodorus Siculus, Strabo, Pliny the Elder and Manetho.

Trivia

Shaming monuments. Herodotus says Sesostris had pillars carved with a female image on the monuments of peoples who surrendered, to shame them.
A real origin. Sesostris is thought to be based on Pharaoh Senusret III, whose name is a Greek corruption of Senusret.
