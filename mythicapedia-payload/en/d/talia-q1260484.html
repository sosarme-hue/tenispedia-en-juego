Thalia

Thalia is one of the three Charites or Graces of Greek mythology, alongside her sisters Aglaia and Euphrosyne. She is linked to festivity, rich banquets and abundance, which makes her a meaningful figure of celebration and fertility in Greek belief.

The myth

According to tradition, Thalia was a daughter of Zeus, although sources disagree on her mother. Most accounts name the Oceanid Eurynome, but other versions give Eurydome, Eurymedousa or Euanthe, and some link her parentage to Dionysus and Koroneia, or to Helios and the Naiad Aegle.

She has no cycle of adventures of her own. She is said to love song and, like her sisters, presides over vegetation and growth. Her name is an adjective applied to banquets, meaning rich, plentiful and abundant, which explains her close ties to celebration.

Worship and religion

The Charites were honoured together as deities of grace and charm, and they were often shown in art dancing in a circle. Thalia's association with festivity and banquets linked her to the shared enjoyment of food and celebration.

Legacy

The Greek word for festivity and banquet has passed into several modern languages. The image of the three Graces dancing in a circle has been a recurring theme in European painting and sculpture, including Antonio Canova's famous group.
