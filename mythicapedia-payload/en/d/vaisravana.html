Buddhist god of the north and of fortune

Vaiśravaṇa

Vaiśravaṇa, known as Vessavaṇa in Pali, is one of the Four Heavenly Kings of Buddhism and guardian of the northern direction. Rooted in the Hindu god Kubera, he became Bishamonten in East Asia and is one of Japan's Seven Gods of Fortune.

The myth

According to Buddhist accounts, Vaiśravaṇa dwells in the northern quarter of the world, including the land of Uttarakuru, and rules the yakshas, beings somewhere between fairy and ogre. He is married to Bhuñjati, with whom he has five daughters, and he has a nephew named Puṇṇaka. The Pali Canon tells that in a past life he was a wealthy mill owner in Sri Lanka who gave the produce of one of his mills to charity, and that this good deed led to his rebirth in the heaven of the Cātummahārājika under the name Kuvera.

When Gautama Buddha was born, Vaiśravaṇa became his follower and reached the stage of sotāpanna. He protected the Buddha, carried messages from gods and humans to him, and gave him the Āṭānāṭā verses, which forest monks recited against wild yakshas. Since the name designates an office rather than a fixed person, each Vessavaṇa is mortal and is replaced at death.

Worship and religion

In the early years of Buddhism he was worshipped at trees dedicated to him as shrines, and some people asked him for children. Today he remains present in Chinese temples, where he is venerated in the Hall of the Heavenly Kings, and in Japan, where he often guards the entrance gate of many Mahayana temples alongside his counterpart. In Tibet he appears in temple murals beside the main door.

Legacy

In Japan he is one of the Seven Gods of Fortune and is linked to war and the protection of warriors. His iconography includes a spear and a small pagoda, a symbol of the divine treasure house he guards and gives away. In China, Taoist belief conflates him with the god Li Jing.

Ancient sources: The suttas of the Pali Canon of Theravāda Buddhism mention him as Vessavaṇa, with his yakkha retinue, his daughters and his city of Ālakamandā.

In art

Trivia

Mongoose and jewels. He is sometimes shown with a mongoose ejecting jewels from its mouth. The mongoose is the enemy of the snake, a symbol of greed or hatred, so the image expresses generosity.
Seven lives to enlightenment. According to tradition, upon reaching the stage of sotāpanna he had only seven more lives before enlightenment.
