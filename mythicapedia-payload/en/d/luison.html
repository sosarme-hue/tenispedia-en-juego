Luisón

Luisón is a monstrous figure in Guaraní mythology, the seventh and last son of Tau and Kerana, regarded as the most accursed of his seven brothers. His legend spread to northeastern Argentina and inspired a legal custom there.

The myth

According to the story, Luisón inherited the greatest curse borne by his parents. On Tuesdays and Fridays, as night falls, he leaves his human form and becomes a huge, repulsive dog with sharp teeth and red eyes. He haunts cemeteries to feed on corpses, and when he meets living people he kisses them on the forehead and crawls beneath their legs to turn them into new Luisones.

At dawn he returns to human shape, but exhausted, filthy and with a sorrowful gaze. Tradition says that at his birth the Siete Cabrillas shone in the sky, a sign that his parents' curse had ended.

Legacy

In Paraguay people feared that the seventh male child could turn into a Luisón, and some families even sacrificed their sons out of that fear. Since 1907 the President of Argentina has served as godfather at official baptisms, and in 1973 Decree 848, issued by Juan Domingo Perón, gave legal form to this custom, known as the presidential sponsorship law. Lawmakers have since moved to repeal it as outdated.

Trivia

The Siete Cabrillas. This is the name given to a cluster of stars that, according to the myth, appeared in the sky at the birth of Luisón.
A wolf without wolves. Wolves do not naturally occur in South America. The European werewolf image was linked to the maned wolf, a local canid that fitted that depiction.
The Alma mula. In Santiago del Estero, Argentina, a similar description is applied to the Alma mula, a demonic spirit originally shaped like a mule.
