Chinese local earth god and village tutelary deity

Tudigong

Tudigong is a tutelary deity of Chinese folk religion, guardian of a specific place and the community that lives there. Ranked among the lowest divinities, he is nonetheless one of the most familiar to ordinary people, and his cult has survived in villages and Chinese communities across the world.

The myth

Tudigong is tied to the land, and tradition traces his roots to the ancient cults of the soil, the sheshen. The oldest accounts mention Gonggong and his son, whom Zhuanxu appointed as god of the soil.

Each Tudigong rules only one territory. One Tudigong is not the god of another place, and when people move, they bid farewell to their local deity and honour the one of their new home. Often, someone who did a great service to the community becomes a Tudigong after death. He is usually shown as a smiling old man with a white beard, and beside him stands his wife Tudipo, the Grandmother of the Soil. A popular story says the Jade Emperor gave her to him as a wife so she could watch over the blessings he distributed, which is why some believe she can hold back wealth.

Worship and religion

He is venerated in temples, in small shrines beneath the main altar or beside house doors. Devotees ask for health and prosperity, and it is customary to honour him before burials, thanking him for the use of his land. In Taiwan his festivals fall on the second day of the second lunar month, regarded as his birthday, and on the fifteenth day of the eighth month. Many shrines were destroyed during the Cultural Revolution, though several have since been rebuilt and the cult remains alive.

Legacy

His presence endures among Chinese communities in Southeast Asia, where the name Tua Pek Kong is widely used for the same deity. He is linked to wish trees and to ritual opera.
