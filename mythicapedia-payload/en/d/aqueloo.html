Greek god of the Achelous River

Achelous

Achelous is the god of the river of the same name, between Aetolia and Acarnania, and one of the oldest and most powerful water spirits of ancient Greece. His contest with Heracles for the hand of Deianeira made him one of the best-known figures in Greek myth.

The myth

According to Hesiod, Achelous was a son of the Titans Oceanus and Tethys, like the other river gods. Other versions make him a son of Earth, or of Oceanus and Earth, and some sources give him an unnamed mother. He courted Deianeira, daughter of Oeneus, king of Calydon, and to win her he changed into a serpent and a bull. Heracles defeated him, and in the struggle the god lost one horn, which he recovered only in exchange for the horn of Amaltheia.

Ovid tells that the Naiads filled that horn with fruit and flowers, turning it into the cornucopia. Heracles forced the defeated god to take refuge in the river Toas, which from then on was called Achelous. For Strabo, the tale reflects the floods of this river, which Heracles tamed with embankments, and the fertility of the region came from that.

Tradition also gives him many children. According to Lycophron and Ovid he was the father of the Sirens, and with Perimede he had Hippodamas and Orestes. He was also the father of nymphs of famous springs, such as Castalia at Delphi and Pirene at Corinth. He helped the matricide Alcmaeon to be purified and gave him his daughter Callirrhoe as a wife.

Worship and religion

Achelous was greatly respected in ancient Greece. His name was invoked in prayers, sacrifices and oaths, and the oracle of Zeus at Dodona ordered sacrifices to him after each response. According to Virgil, he came to represent all rivers and fresh waters, the source of agriculture and human life. He was shown as an old man with horns and a beard from which water flows, and on some coins from Acarnania as a bull with a human head.

Legacy

His story left a lasting image in later culture: the cornucopia, or horn of plenty, a symbol of abundance. From Propertius onward, Latin poets turned him into the stereotype of the unlucky suitor.

Ancient sources: Hesiod, Pindar, Sophocles, Ovid, Strabo, Lycophron, Apollodorus and Virgil mention him, with variations in his genealogy and in the details of the combat.

Trivia

The cornucopia. According to Ovid, the Naiads filled the horn the god had lost with fruit and flowers, and it became the horn of plenty.
An island born from a plea. According to Ovid, Achelous saved Perimele, whom her father had thrown off a cliff, and Poseidon turned her into an island.
