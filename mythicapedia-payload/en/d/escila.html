Scylla

Scylla is a female sea monster of Greek mythology who lived on one side of a narrow strait between Italy and Sicily, opposite her counterpart Charybdis. Her appearance in the Odyssey made her one of the most feared dangers for sailors, and the phrase "between Scylla and Charybdis" is still used in English today.

The myth

In Homer, Scylla is a creature with six heads and twelve feeble legs who lives in a cave high on a cliff and snatches sailors passing below. Circe advises Odysseus to keep closer to Scylla than to Charybdis, since losing six men was better than losing the whole crew. As the hero sails through the strait, Scylla seizes six companions from the deck and devours them. The poem does not explain where she comes from.

Later versions tell of a beautiful nymph who was once Scylla. The sea god Glaucus, formerly a fisherman, fell in love with her, but she fled inland and he could not follow. He asked the sorceress Circe for a love potion, but Circe herself desired him, and jealous of her rival she poured a poisonous brew into the pool where Scylla bathed. The nymph was transformed into a monster with dog heads springing from her body. Ovid tells this story in his Metamorphoses, and Hyginus repeats it.

Another tradition blames Amphitrite, who was jealous of Poseidon's love for Scylla. Hyginus also records a version in which Heracles killed Scylla after she stole cattle from Geryon, and her father Phorcys brought her back to life by applying burning torches to her body. Her parentage varies too. Homer and Apollodorus make Crataeis her mother, other authors name Hecate, and some name Typhon and Echidna as her parents.

Legacy

The Calabrian town of Scilla takes its name from this figure. The expression "between Scylla and Charybdis", meaning caught between two equally dangerous choices, comes from her story. Jean-Marie Leclair adapted the tale into the opera Scylla et Glaucus (1746), and John Keats reworked it in Endymion (1818), where Circe simply murders the nymph.

Ancient sources: Homer in Book XII of the Odyssey, Ovid in the Metamorphoses, and later accounts by Virgil, Hyginus and Apollodorus.

Trivia

A rationalist reading. Palaephatus argued that Scylla was really a swift Tyrrhenian ship that bore her name on its prow. Procopius suggested instead that the name came from a fish abundant in the strait.
A Byzantine description. The Byzantine encyclopedia Suda describes her as a woman as far as the eyes, with six dog heads on each side and a serpent body.
