Roman god of honor and military virtue

Honos

Honos was, in Roman religion, the deified personification of honor, chivalry and justice, especially as a military virtue. He was usually worshipped alongside Virtus, and the pair show how Rome turned human qualities into deities.

The myth

Honos is better described as an abstract personification than as a character in stories: the sources state that no myths existed about him or similar deities such as Fides or Spes. He was shown holding a spear and a cornucopia, sometimes with an olive branch, and at times he was identified with Virtus or depicted beside her.

His story survives mainly through his temples. According to tradition, Quintus Fabius Maximus dedicated a shrine to him in 234 BC after his victory over the Ligures, just outside the Porta Capena. Years later, Marcus Claudius Marcellus vowed a joint temple to Honos and Virtus after his victory at Clastidium and the capture of Syracuse. The College of Pontiffs refused to let the two gods share one building, so he restored the temple of Honos and added a separate shrine to Virtus beside it. His son dedicated it in 205 BC.

Worship and religion

His worship centred on urban sanctuaries in Rome. Marcellus's temple was enriched with treasures brought from Syracuse, and another temple, built by Gaius Marius in 101 BC from spoils taken from the Cimbri and Teutones, stood on the slopes of the Capitoline Hill. The sources do not describe his rituals or festivals, and no living cult is known.

Legacy

His name became linked with Virtus and the Roman ideal of civic and military virtue. His temples also served as political settings: in the Capitoline shrine the Senate voted to recall Cicero from exile in 57 BC. Marcellus's temple was restored by Vespasian and was still standing in the fourth century.

Trivia

A god without myths. According to the sources, Honos and other personifications of virtues such as Fides or Spes had no associated myths; they are known mainly through their symbols and temples.
A temple filled with war loot. Marcellus's shrine was filled with treasures looted from Syracuse, although many of them disappeared over the following two centuries.
