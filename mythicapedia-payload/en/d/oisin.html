poet and warrior of the Fianna, son of Fionn mac Cumhaill

Oisín

Oisín is, according to Irish legend, the greatest poet of Ireland and a warrior of the Fianna, the band of heroes in the Ossianic or Fenian Cycle. The demigod son of Fionn mac Cumhaill and Sadhbh, he narrates much of that cycle and is credited with many of its poems, which keeps him central to Ireland's literary identity.

The myth

His name literally means "young deer" or fawn. The story tells that his mother Sadhbh was turned into a deer by a druid, Fear Doirche. The young hunter Fionn caught her without killing her, and she regained human form; they settled together, but the druid transformed her again and she returned to the wild. Seven years later Fionn found their child, naked, on Benbulbin.

Oisín's most famous tale is the otherworld voyage known as an echtra. There he meets Niamh of the Golden Hair, daughter of Manannán mac Lir, a sea god, whose head had been turned into a pig's head by a prophecy. To free her, Oisín agrees to marry her, and the two live in Tír na nÓg, the land of the young, where he becomes king. They have a son, Oscar, a daughter, Plor na mBan, and another son, Finn.

After what seems to him three years, but was really three hundred, Oisín returns to Ireland on Niamh's white horse Embarr. She warns him never to touch the ground, but while helping lift a stone he falls from the saddle and ages at once. In some versions he meets Saint Patrick before dying and tells him the deeds of the Fianna; in others the encounter is tense, and the two argue over faith.

Legacy

Yeats wrote the poem The Wanderings of Oisin about him. In the eighteenth century James Macpherson presented his "Ossianic" poems as based on Oisín; they influenced Goethe and the young Walter Scott, though their authenticity was widely disputed. He also appears in modern novels, comics and films.

Ancient sources: The Irish Fenian cycle, including the tale Acallam na Senórach (Tales of the Elders), in which Oisín speaks with Saint Patrick.

Trivia

Name of a fawn. His name means "fawn", linked to his mother's transformation into a deer.
Three centuries. In Tír na nÓg he believes three years have passed, but in fact three hundred have gone by.
