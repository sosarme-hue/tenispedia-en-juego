Nabataean god of the mountain

Dushara

Dushara, also written Dusares, was a pre-Islamic Arabian god worshipped by the Nabataeans, especially at Petra and Hegra (Mada'in Saleh), where he was the city's patron. His name, "Lord of the mountain", hints at a link with the local mountains, though its exact meaning remains disputed.

The myth

Safaitic inscriptions suggest that Dushara was the son of the goddess Al-Lat and that he gathered with other deities in the heavens. One inscription calls him "Dushara from Petra", reflecting his close ties to that city.

Tradition held that he could bring justice to those who called on him with the correct ritual. Over time he came to be identified with other deities: in the Greek period he was linked with Zeus, as chief of the Nabataean pantheon, and also with Dionysus.

Worship and religion

Safaitic inscriptions mention animal sacrifices offered to Dushara in return for a variety of favours. A shrine to him has been found in the harbour of ancient Puteoli in Italy, a busy trading city with links to the Near East that had a Nabataean presence by the mid first century BCE. His cult continued in some form into the Roman period and possibly as late as the Islamic era.

Ancient sources: Nabataean and Safaitic inscriptions name him directly, and the Muslim historian Hisham ibn al-Kalbi records him in his Book of Idols (Kitab al-Asnam), describing him as an idol of a tribe of the Azd.

Trivia

A shrine in a Roman port. A shrine to Dushara has been discovered in Puteoli, the ancient Roman port of Italy, showing that his cult reached places far from Petra.
A god with many names. In the Greek period he was identified with both Zeus and Dionysus, and classical sources spell his name in different ways.
