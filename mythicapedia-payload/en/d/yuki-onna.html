snow spirit of Japanese folklore

Yuki-onna

The yuki-onna, or "snow woman," is a yūrei or yōkai from Japanese folklore linked to winter and blizzards. She is often confused with the yama-uba, but the two are distinct, and she remains a familiar figure in Japanese manga, anime and literature.

The myth

Tradition places the yuki-onna on snowy nights, when she appears as a tall, striking woman with pale, almost translucent skin and long hair. She drifts over the snow without leaving footprints, and some tales say she has no feet at all. Her gaze can terrify, and if threatened she can dissolve into a cloud of mist or snow.

Her temper changes from tale to tale. In many stories she waits for travelers caught in blizzards and freezes them with her icy breath, leaving them as statues of ice, or leads them astray until they die of cold. Other times she holds a child: whoever takes it in their arms freezes in place, and parents searching for their lost children are especially vulnerable. In harsher versions she slips into houses on a gust of wind to kill sleepers, though some insist she must first be invited in. Some say she drains blood or life force, or takes the form of a succubus to drain men of weak faith.

Not every tale is grim. In one popular legend she spares a young man for his looks and youth, on the condition that he never speak of their meeting. Years later, when he breaks his promise, his wife reveals herself as the snow woman. She leaves, but entrusts the children to him and warns that she will return without mercy if they are mistreated. In another story she melts her husband once he discovers her true nature.

Legacy

Her figure has inspired works of literature, film, manga and animation in Japan. The writer Lafcadio Hearn devoted one of his tales to her, and the name yuki-onna remains tied to the image of the ghostly woman of the snow.

Ancient sources: The renga poet Sōgi mentions her in his Sōgi Shokoku Monogatari from the Muromachi period, where he recounts seeing one while staying in Echigo Province.

Trivia

Many names. She goes by many names depending on the region, such as yuki-musume, "snow daughter," or tsurara-onna, a name related to icicles.
Seen by a poet. The renga poet Sōgi wrote that he saw a yuki-onna while staying in Echigo, showing that the legend already existed in the Muromachi period.
The warrior of Hirosaki. In a legend from Hirosaki, a warrior held a short sword close to the head of the child the yuki-onna handed him and was rewarded with many treasures.
