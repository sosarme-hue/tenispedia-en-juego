Norse goddess of youth

Iðunn

Iðunn is one of the Ásynjur of Norse mythology, known for keeping apples that restored the gods' youth. She is attested mainly in the Eddas, and she matters for the story of her abduction and for the many interpretations it has inspired.

The myth

According to the sources, Iðunn is the wife of the skaldic god Bragi, and she keeps his apples in a box made of ash wood. When the gods begin to grow old, they eat the fruit and become young again, a cycle that continues until Ragnarök. The Prose Edda presents her in this role in Gylfaginning, where Gangleri remarks that the gods depend greatly on her good faith and care.

In Skáldskaparmál, the jötunn Þjazi, in eagle form, seizes her with the help of Loki, who had lured her out of Asgard with the promise of even finer apples. Without her, the Æsir grow grey and old, and they force Loki to rescue her. Borrowing Freyja's falcon plumage, he finds her alone at Þjazi's home, turns her into a nut and flies back.

Þjazi returns, gives chase in eagle form, and the gods build a fire near the walls of Asgard. The eagle cannot stop, plunges into the flames with its feathers alight, and the gods kill him. The Haustlöng, a poem by Þjóðólfur úr Hvini preserved in Skáldskaparmál, describes the scene on a painted shield and calls Iðunn the maiden who understands the eternal life of the Æsir, without mentioning apples.

In the Poetic Edda, the poem Lokasenna shows her as Bragi's wife at a feast given by Ægir. Loki accuses her of sleeping with the killer of her brother, an episode found in no other source; scholars read it as playful mockery. The late poem Hrafnagaldr Óðins, which has no parallels elsewhere, describes her as an elf-descended dís who dwells in the valleys.

Legacy

Her name has drawn scholarly interest. Jacob Grimm proposed a possible link to the idisi, and C. M. Yonge argued that the Old English name Idonea derives from Iðunn. One of the mountains on Venus, Idunn Mons, is named after her. Her story has inspired artworks and appears in modern popular culture.

Ancient sources: Poetic Edda (Lokasenna and Hrafnagaldr Óðins), Prose Edda (Gylfaginning and Skáldskaparmál), and the poem Haustlöng.

Trivia

A mountain on Venus. One of the mountains on Venus is named after the goddess, Idunn Mons.
Apples in Germanic graves. The scholar H. R. Ellis Davidson links her apples to baskets of fruit found in the Oseberg ship burial in Norway.
