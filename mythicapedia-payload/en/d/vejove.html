Roman god of Etruscan origin

Vejove

Vejove was a god of Roman religion with Etruscan roots, shown as a young man holding arrows or lightning. He was seen as a kind of gloomy counterpart to Jupiter, and his name has sparked debate about his true role.

The myth

Ancient sources say little about his story. He is described as a young man holding a bundle of arrows (sometimes lightning bolts), accompanied by a goat. The Romans regarded him as one of their oldest gods and often identified him with Jupiter or Apollo. As a god of healing, he was also linked to Asclepius.

The best-known explanation of his name comes from Aulus Gellius, who in the Attic Nights, written around 177 CE, compares him to Summanus and calls him an ill-omened counterpart of Jupiter. Gellius notes that the prefix ve- signals the opposite, as in vesanus ("insane"), so Vejove would be the "anti-Jove". This is an ancient interpretation, not a proven fact.

Worship and religion

He had temples on the Capitoline Hill, between its two peaks, and on the Tiber Island, and was also worshipped at Bovillae. His statue on the Capitoline stood beside one of a she-goat. In spring, several goats were sacrificed to him to avert plagues. Gellius mentions the sacrifice of a female goat ritu humano, an obscure phrase that could mean "after the manner of a human sacrifice" or "in the manner of a burial". He had three festivals in the Roman calendar: 1 January, 7 March and 21 May. There is no information on whether his cult survived.

Ancient sources: Aulus Gellius, Attic Nights (around 177 CE), which compares him with Summanus and explains his name.

Trivia

The prefix ve-. Aulus Gellius noticed that the prefix ve- in his name also appears in Latin words such as vesanus, meaning "insane", and from this concluded that he was the opposite of Jupiter.
Goats against plagues. In spring several goats were sacrificed to Vejove to ward off plagues, and the goat was also his usual companion in his iconography.
