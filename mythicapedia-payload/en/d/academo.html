Attic hero of Greek mythology

Academus

Academus was an Attic hero of Greek mythology whose name became attached to the grove outside Athens where Plato founded his Academy. He matters chiefly because his name gave rise to the English word «academy» for an institution of higher learning.

The myth

According to Plutarch, in his life of Theseus, the Athenian king abducted Helen when she was only twelve, long before she married Menelaus and before Paris took her to Troy. Her twin brothers, Castor and Pollux, invaded Attica to rescue her and threatened to destroy Athens.

Academus saved the city by telling them that Helen was hidden at Aphidnae. For this service, Tyndareus, Castor's father and Helen's stepfather, showed him great gratitude. Tradition adds that when the Lacedaemonians invaded Attica and laid waste the countryside, they spared the Academy out of regard for Academus.

Worship and religion

The site, known earlier as Ecademia and later as Akademeia, was sacred to Athena and had sheltered her cult since the Bronze Age, perhaps linked to the Dioskouroi and to the hero himself. Cimon turned the arid ground into a well-watered grove with running tracks and shaded walks. Its grove supplied the olive oil given as prizes at the Panathenaic Games, in the decorated amphorae presented to the winners.

Legacy

Plato gave his lectures among these trees, and from there comes the word academy. Horace, in his Epistles, speaks of seeking truth in the groves of Academe. Milton echoes the phrase in Paradise Regained, and Mary McCarthy titled her satirical novel The Groves of Academe after it.

Ancient sources: Plutarch, in his Life of Theseus, is the main source; Horace, Diogenes Laertius and Timon of Phlius also mention the site.

Trivia

A grove made into a garden. According to Plutarch, Cimon turned a dry, barren spot into a well-watered grove with running tracks and shaded walks.
Prizes of oil. Olive oil from the grove was awarded as a prize at the Panathenaic Games, in decorated amphorae.
