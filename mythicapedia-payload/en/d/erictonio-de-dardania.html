mythical king of Dardania, father of Tros

Erichthonius of Dardania

Erichthonius is a mythical king of Dardania, the realm that preceded Troy in Greek tradition. He is the grandfather of Ilus and Ganymede, and his story matters because it links the origins of the Trojan royal house with Homer's account.

The myth

According to Homer, Erichthonius was the son of Dardanus, founder of Dardania, and of Batea, although other versions name Olizone, daughter of Phineus, as his mother. He succeeded his father on the throne, since his elder brother Ilus died young and childless. Homer presents him as the wealthiest man of his time, owner of three thousand mares that grazed in the water-meadows. Boreas, the north wind, fell in love with them and, taking the form of a dark-maned stallion, covered them. From this union twelve foals were born, so light that they could run across the ripe wheat without bending it or gallop over the crests of the waves.

Erichthonius fathered Tros, king of the Trojans, who had three sons: Ilus, Assaracus and Ganymede, the most beautiful of mortals, whom the gods carried off to serve Zeus as cupbearer. Later traditions give Astyoche, a naiad daughter of the river god Simoeis, as his wife. One account also names him the father of Ganymede, which shows that genealogies were never fixed. According to tradition, he reigned for forty-six years, or sixty-five according to others.

Strabo records, but rejects, the claim that an Attic Teucer came from a deme called Troes. Modern scholars suggest that the Trojan and Athenian Erichthonius may originally have been the same figure, later split into two regional legends.

Ancient sources: Homer, Iliad (book 20), the main and almost only ancient source that describes Erichthonius in detail.

Trivia

Twelve swift foals. According to Homer, the twelve foals born to Erichthonius's mares were so light that they could run over ripe wheat without bending it.
Two Erichthonii. Strabo notes that some later writers thought the Trojan and Athenian Erichthonius might be the same figure.
