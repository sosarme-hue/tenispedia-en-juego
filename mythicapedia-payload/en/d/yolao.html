Theban hero, nephew and charioteer of Heracles

Iolaus

Iolaus is a Theban hero of Greek mythology, son of Iphicles and nephew of Heracles, who served as his charioteer, squire and companion in many of his exploits. He matters as the inseparable helper of the great hero and for the heroic cult he received in Thebes and Sardinia.

The myth

According to tradition, Iolaus was the son of Iphicles and Automedusa, daughter of King Alcathous. In the myths he helped Heracles against the Lernaean Hydra: as the hero cut off each head, Iolaus cauterized the neck with a torch so that new heads could not grow. For this reason Eurystheus refused to count that labour among the ten. He also drove his uncle's chariot on the expedition to seize the cattle of Geryon and in other episodes.

Plutarch and Euripides present him as the beloved (erômenos) of Heracles, and Heracles gave him Megara as his wife, though in some versions she was considerably older. According to Pausanias, citing Hesiod, Iolaus had a daughter, Leipephilene, whose name is corrupt in the manuscripts and was emended by editors, and through her he was linked to the kings of Corinth.

In Euripides' Heraclidae, an elderly Iolaus defends Heracles' children against Eurystheus with Athenian help. In one version he begged the gods of the underworld to restore his youth for a single hour so he could defeat Eurystheus; another credits Hebe with the gift. Other accounts say he lit the pyre on which Heracles died after Deianira's poisoning.

Worship and religion

In Thebes, Iolaus was honoured as a hero alongside Heracles. His tomb became a shrine beside a gymnasium and a stadium named after him. There the Iolaia, an annual athletic festival with gymnastic and equestrian contests, was held, and its winners were crowned with myrtle garlands. In Sardinia, where according to Diodorus he led a colony of Heracles' sons and the Thespiades, the inhabitants built him a temple and addressed him as "Father Iolaus". Aristotle links the island to the practice of sleeping beside heroes' tombs to cure nightmares.

Legacy

A genus of lycaenid butterflies is named after him. An exoplanet orbiting the star HAT-P-42, now named Lerna, also bears his name through the IAU's NameExoWorlds project. A card and board game published by Hasbro is named after him, a variant of gin rummy.

Ancient sources: Pausanias, Diodorus Siculus, Plutarch, Euripides in the Heraclidae, Hyginus and Ovid mention him; Pausanias cites Hesiod for his descendants.

Trivia

Cauterizing the Hydra. Iolaus burned the severed necks of the Hydra with a torch to stop new heads from growing.
Vows at his tomb. According to Plutarch, lovers made reciprocal vows of affection at his tomb in Thebes.
