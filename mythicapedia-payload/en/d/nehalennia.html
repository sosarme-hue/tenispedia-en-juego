Germanic goddess of sailors and the North Sea

Nehalennia

Nehalennia is a goddess worshipped along the North Sea coast, at the mouths of the Scheldt and the Rhine, who was believed to protect sailors and traders. Her origin and the meaning of her name remain debated, with scholars weighing Germanic against Celtic roots.

The myth

According to the tradition preserved on her altars, Nehalennia was a young goddess who watched over voyages on the North Sea. No continuous myth survives; what we know comes mainly from inscriptions and images.

Her most distinctive role is that of helper of seafarers. During storms, sailors called on her and, if they reached safety, promised her an altar. The stone carvings, sometimes made of imported and costly material, show her seated on a throne or standing at a ship's prow, with a basket of apples or loaves in her lap and a dog at her side. Some scholars believe that, judging by her attributes, she was originally a fertility goddess.

Worship and religion

Her worship centred on sanctuaries at Domburg, on the island of Walcheren, and at Colijnsplaat, on the Oosterschelde, which drew visitors from places as distant as Besançon and Trier. More than 160 votive altars are known, many dedicated in thanks for a safe crossing, mostly dating to the 2nd and 3rd centuries, although her cult was older. The Colijnsplaat sanctuary was eventually destroyed by the sea. Her worship does not survive as such, but the goddess has been revived within modern Dutch paganism.

Legacy

Her name lives on in astronomy: an asteroid was named after her in 1960. In 2005 a replica of her temple was built in Colijnsplaat, using authentic materials and techniques as far as possible, based on finds from the surrounding area.

Trivia

A dog on the altars. The dog appears on thirteen of the twenty-one altars catalogued by Ada Hondius-Crone, who described it as a kind of greyhound.
Imported stone. The Netherlands has no stone quarries, so the altars were carved from stone brought from elsewhere, and their inscriptions were written in Latin.
