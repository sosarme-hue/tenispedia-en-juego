Athenian princess in Greek mythology

Philomela

Philomela is an Athenian princess in Greek mythology who, after suffering violence at the hands of her brother-in-law Tereus, is transformed into a bird. Her story of revenge and metamorphosis is one of the most influential myths in Western literature.

The myth

Philomela was the younger daughter of King Pandion of Athens and the naiad Zeuxippe, and sister of Procne, who was married to King Tereus of Thrace. According to Ovid, Procne asked to see her sister, and Tereus sailed to Athens to bring her back. On the return voyage he became obsessed with her, forced her in a lodge in the woods and, to keep her silent, cut out her tongue.

Philomela wove her story into a tapestry and sent it to Procne, who, enraged, killed their son Itys and served him to Tereus at a meal. When the furious king chased the sisters, they prayed to the gods, who turned them into birds, while Tereus became a hoopoe. Sources disagree on which sister became the nightingale and which the swallow: Greek authors gave Philomela the swallow and Procne the nightingale, while Roman writers reversed them. Ovid does not specify.

Legacy

Shakespeare drew on her story for the character of Lavinia in Titus Andronicus. Lope de Vega wrote the poem La Filomena (1621), and Rojas Zorrilla wrote the play Progne y Filomena (1636). In poetry, "filomela" and "filomena" are synonyms for nightingale. The name Progne is used in zoology for a genus of martins, and in Iberian and Latin American oral tradition the tale survives under names such as Filomena and Blanca Flor.

Ancient sources: Ovid tells the story in Book VI of the Metamorphoses; the Bibliotheca attributed to Pseudo-Apollodorus and Sophocles' lost tragedy Tereus present earlier versions.

Trivia

Swallows named after a myth. The swallow genus Progne takes its name from Procne, the sister who in Latin tradition becomes a swallow.
A silent nightingale. The female nightingale does not sing; only the male sings, which contrasts with the usual image of the bird in the myth.
