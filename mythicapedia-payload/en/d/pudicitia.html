Roman goddess of modesty and sexual virtue

Pudicitia

Pudicitia was the Roman personification of modesty and chastity, a central virtue in Roman sexual ethics, especially for women. Romans honored her as protector of women's pudicitia, and her importance shows in temples, coins and the writings of authors such as Livy and Ovid.

The myth

In the literary tradition of Rome, Pudicitia embodied an ideal that ancient writers examined closely. Livy presents Lucretia as the supreme example of the virtue: although Tarquin raped her, she remained loyal to her husband. For the Romans, virtue was not only a matter of the mind but also visible in appearance and public behavior, so a woman could lose her reputation over seemingly minor gestures.

Valerius Maximus describes her as a protector of children, young people and married women, placing her beside Juno and Vesta as guardian of female chastity. Juvenal tells how, at the end of the Iron Age, Pudicitia left the earth along with her sister Astraea, goddess of justice, and returned to the heavens.

Worship and religion

According to Livy and Festus, Pudicitia had two shrines in Rome: the older Temple of Pudicitia Patricia, near the Temple of Hercules in the Forum Boarium, and the Temple of Pudicitia Plebeia along the Vicus Longus. Only women married once (univirae) could take part in her rites, though no written account of them survives. The plebeian shrine arose from a dispute in 296 BCE and was abandoned when women of doubtful morals began to worship there.

Legacy

The figure appears on imperial coins, especially those of empresses such as Faustina and Julia Domna, who associated themselves with the goddess to display the purity of their character. Her image, veiled and wearing the stola, became a model of matronly virtue.

Ancient sources: Livy (Ab Urbe Condita), Valerius Maximus, Ovid (Fasti), Juvenal and Festus.

In art

Trivia

Two temples. Rome had two shrines dedicated to the goddess, distinguished by the lineage of those who attended them: one patrician and one plebeian.
Veil over the face. On coins of the 2nd and 3rd centuries CE the goddess is shown drawing a veil over her face, a gesture linked to matronly modesty.
