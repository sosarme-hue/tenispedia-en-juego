Aztec god of rain and water

Tlaloc

Tlaloc is the Mexica god of rain, heavenly water, and fertility, one of the oldest deities in Mesoamerica. His image, with fangs, ringed eyes, and a lip shaped like a moustache, appears in temples and codices, and his worship shaped the farming calendar of the central highlands.

The myth

According to tradition, Tlaloc is one of the oldest and most widespread gods of Mesoamerica. His cult is thought to have begun at Teotihuacan, passed to Tula, and spread among the Nahua peoples. The Mexica, once settled around Lake Texcoco, adopted him as a farming deity. Texts describe him as lord of lightning, rain, and earthquakes, a being who both gives life and can destroy: he sends the rain that fertilises the maize fields, but also hail, floods, droughts, and frosts.

The god worked through his helpers, the tlaloques, who lived inside the mountains and released four kinds of rain from four vessels set in the four directions. His dwelling was in the Templo Mayor of Tenochtitlan and in the Tlalocan, the mountain interior that bears his name.

In Tlaxcalan cosmology, Tlaloc first married Xochiquetzal, goddess of beauty, but Tezcatlipoca abducted her. He later wed Matlalcueye, and in this version he has an elder daughter or sister, Huixtocihuatl.

Worship and religion

The Mexica dedicated several festivals of their calendar to him, among them Atlcahualo, which opened the year in February. According to Sahagún, children were sacrificed on mountain summits during that month, and their tears were read as a sign of abundant rain; captives were also killed. Other festivals, Tozoztontli and Atemoztli, involved offerings in caves and rites for the tlaloques. Cerro Tlaloc was the main shrine of the Valley of Mexico. Today, in Coatlinchan near Texcoco, residents still celebrate the giant statue of the god, about 168 tons, and some treat it as a patron.

Legacy

Tlaloc remains present in Mexican culture. His images appear across the country, from Tijuana to the Yucatán, and the Coatlinchan statue serves as a symbol of the nation. The murals José Clemente Orozco painted at Dartmouth College, between 1932 and 1934, show him among the principal deities, and a sixteenth-century church in Tlatelolco preserved stones bearing his image.

Ancient sources: Bernardino de Sahagún's General History of the Things of New Spain, written in the sixteenth century, describes his festivals and sacrifices.

Trivia

A ritual incense flower. The Mexican marigold, known to the Nahua as cempohualxochitl, was burned as incense in ceremonies honouring Tlaloc.
Tears as a omen. During Atlcahualo, the crying of children led to sacrifice was read as a sign that abundant rain would come.
A 168-ton statue. The giant Coatlinchan statue, long thought to depict Tlaloc, was moved in 1964 to the National Museum of Anthropology in Mexico City.
