Roman goddess of the Moon

Luna

Luna is the divine embodiment of the Moon in Roman religion and mythology. She is presented as the female counterpart of the Sun, Sol, and is often identified with the Greek Selene, whose myths Roman literature later adopted under her own name.

The myth

In Roman mythology Luna is the female complement of the Sun, and she is shown driving a biga, a two-yoke chariot pulled by horses or oxen. In Roman art she is regularly paired with the Sun, who drives a four-horse chariot, or quadriga. Isidore of Seville explained the difference: the quadriga stands for the sun's course through the four seasons, while the biga represents the Moon, since it travels a twin course with the sun or because it is visible both by day and by night, yoked to one black horse and one white.

Roman tradition linked her to Selene, and at times Luna is less a separate goddess than an epithet that specializes another deity, such as Diana or Juno. She also belongs to the triple goddess, the diva triformis, alongside Diana and either Proserpina or Hecate. The myth of Endymion, loved by Selene, was a popular subject in Roman wall painting.

Worship and religion

According to tradition, the cult of Luna at Rome dated back to the days of the kings. Titus Tatius was said to have brought her worship from the Sabines, and Servius Tullius was credited with founding her temple on the Aventine Hill, just below a temple of Diana. Its anniversary was celebrated on March 31. As Noctiluna she also had a temple on the Palatine Hill, though little is known about it.

Varro counted her among the visible gods, the twelve gods of agriculture, and the twenty principal gods of Rome, the di selecti.

Legacy

Luna was linked to the Imperial cult, where Sol and Luna could stand for the extent of Roman rule over the world and the goal of guaranteeing peace. Her image driving a biga appears in Mithraic art, on lamps, cameos and late antique reliefs, and even on a Carolingian roundel.

Ancient sources: Varro, Virgil, Horace in the Carmen Saeculare, Isidore of Seville and Servius.

Trivia

A nocturnal temple. On the Palatine Hill she had a temple as Noctiluna, the Night-Shiner, which Varro described as shining by night, though its exact purpose is unknown.
A temple struck by lightning. According to the sources, her Aventine temple was struck by lightning in 84 BC, the same day the popularis leader Cinna was murdered by his troops.
