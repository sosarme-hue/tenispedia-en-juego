wise hero of the Akkadian flood epic

Atrahasis

Atrahasis is the wise priest who gives his name to the Akkadian epic composed around the 18th century BC. His story is one of the oldest versions of the flood myth that spread throughout Mesopotamia.

The myth

According to the tale, the upper gods, the Anunnaki, and the lower gods, the Igigi, shared the work of farming the land and digging irrigation canals. Worn out by the labour, the lesser gods rebelled against Enlil, who looked for a way to avoid open battle. He asked Mami, leader of the seven divine wombs, to create humans to do the hard work, and she could only do so with Enki's help. After the gods purified themselves, the creation went ahead.

Humans multiplied without restraint and caused an overpopulation crisis. The upper gods resolved to wipe them out with a great flood. Yet a single fertile couple survives thanks to an act of sabotage by one of the lower gods. In the end, the rulers of Mesopotamia agree on a method to bring human reproduction down to a tolerable level.

Legacy

The epic was assembled by George Smith, who published it as The Chaldean Account of Genesis. In 1899 Heinrich Zimmern corrected the hero's name to Atra-Hasis. Its flood account passed into the Biblical and Qur'anic traditions, and in 1965 Wilfred G. Lambert and Alan Millard published new texts that allowed the first nearly complete English translation.

Ancient sources: The Sumerian King List names him as a ruler of Shuruppak, and copies of the epic survive on Old Babylonian tablets and in the Library of Ashurbanipal.

Trivia

A corrected name. The hero first appeared under another name in early translations. Heinrich Zimmern corrected it in 1899 to Atrahasis, meaning 'exceedingly wise'.
A city before the flood. The Sumerian King List places him as ruler of Shuruppak, one of the earliest city-states before the great flood.
