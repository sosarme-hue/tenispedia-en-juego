Inca creator god of the universe

Pachacamac

Pachacamac is the creator and animator of the entire universe in Inca mythology, and the one who kept the balance of the world. His worship was especially important on the central coast, where he was known before being incorporated into the Inca pantheon.

The myth

According to Inca tradition, Pachacamac, whose Quechua name can be rendered as "he who sustains or creates the world", held a wide range of powers: fire, the sky, night, earth and earthquakes. Some sources regarded him as a re-edition or manifestation of the god Viracocha, although the texts acknowledge that they are distinct beings within the Andean pantheon.

In the hymn Yayallay, transcribed by Blas Valera, a young noble student asks Illapa, Inti and Pachacamac for many children of royal blood, while affirming that Pachacamac has neither beginning nor end. Late Inca myths make him the father of Inti and Mama Killa, and husband of Mama Pacha.

Chroniclers describe him as an invisible and unknown god, omnipotent and omniscient, though some scholars think this portrait resembles an abstract universal principle more than a personal deity. According to Garcilaso de la Vega, the nobility venerated him even above the Sun. Murúa and Garcilaso also record doubts about the Sun's supremacy voiced by Incas such as Pachacútec, Túpac Yupanqui and Huayna Cápac.

In the myth of Wakon and the Willkas, several animals helped the sons of Pachacamac defeat Wakon, and the fox received from him cunning and the ability to shelter its young underground. In the Huarochirí texts, however, the fox was cursed by Cuniraya Huiracocha for failing to help him in his search for Cahuillaca. The fox is linked to the night, one of Pachacamac's domains.

Worship and religion

Pachacamac was associated with a major shrine on the central coast, near present-day Lima, which drew pilgrims for centuries. According to the sources, the Incas incorporated his cult after subjugating the Ichma, and his two-faced idol carved in algarrobo wood suggested duality. The original idol was destroyed after the arrival of the Spanish conquistadors, and the cult no longer survives as such.

Legacy

Pachacamac gives its name to an important archaeological site south of Lima, in the Lurín valley, and to a district of the Peruvian capital.

Ancient sources: His attributes are recorded in Garcilaso de la Vega's Royal Commentaries, Martín de Murúa's General History of Peru and the Huarochirí texts.

Trivia

Two-faced idol. Inside his temple, an algarrobo-wood idol with two faces looking in opposite directions was found, a symbol of duality.
A god nobody had seen. According to Garcilaso, worshippers built no temples and offered no sacrifices to Pachacamac because nobody had seen him, and they honored him in their hearts.
