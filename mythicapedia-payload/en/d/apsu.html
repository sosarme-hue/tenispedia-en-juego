Mesopotamian primordial deity of fresh water

Apsu

Apsu (Abzu in Sumerian) is the primordial ocean of fresh water in Sumerian and Akkadian cosmology, from whose depths, according to tradition, lakes, springs, rivers and wells arose. He plays his most prominent role in the Babylonian poem Enuma Elish, where he appears as the male principle alongside Tiamat, the salt sea.

The myth

According to the Enuma Elish, at the beginning there was only Apsu, the fresh water, and Tiamat, the salt water, mixing their waters before any dry land or reed marsh existed. From their union came the first gods, among them Lahmu and Lahamu, and then further pairs that gave rise to the sky, to Anshar and Kishar, to An (Anu in Akkadian) and eventually to Enki.

When the younger gods began to make a commotion, Apsu and Tiamat resolved to destroy them. Enki, known as Ea in Akkadian, struck first: he subdued Apsu with a spell, cast a deep sleep over him and then killed him. Ea built his home above the waters of Apsu, the abyss of the deep, and there lived with his wife Damgalnuna, his mother Nammu and his advisor Isimud, along with other subservient creatures. Enraged, Tiamat gave birth to monsters and waged war on her children, until Marduk defeated her and shaped heaven and earth from her body.

In other accounts, such as the Atrahasis epic, the Abzu is the source of the waters that could bring about the great flood.

Worship and religion

In Eridu, the city of Enki, his temple was called E2-abzu, the house of the deep waters, and stood at the edge of a swamp regarded as an abzu. In Babylonian and Assyrian temple courtyards, tanks of holy water also bore the name apsu and were used for ritual washing, similar to Jewish mikvot, the washing pools of mosques and the baptismal font of Christian churches. The sources do not indicate that his worship survives today.

Legacy

His name lives on mainly in modern popular culture. Abzû, a 2016 adventure game inspired by Sumerian mythology, takes its title and its underwater setting from him. In scholarship, the term remains in use in Assyriology and the history of religions.

Ancient sources: The Enuma Elish, a Babylonian creation poem preserved in copies from the library of Assurbanipal, and the Atrahasis epic.
