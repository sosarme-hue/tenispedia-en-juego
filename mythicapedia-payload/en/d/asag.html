demon of Sumerian and Mesopotamian mythology

Asag

Asag, known as Asakku in Akkadian, is a monstrous demon of Mesopotamian mythology whose mere presence, it was said, made fish boil alive in the rivers. His story survives mainly in the Sumerian poem Lugal-e, which recounts his defeat at the hands of the god Ninurta.

The myth

According to the poem, Asag was the offspring of the sky god An and the earth goddess Ki, and he dwelt in the Kur, a mountainous realm. He was linked to diseases such as malaria, and he was said to drain wells and split the earth into hollows filled with poison. Asag allied himself with his brood of stone offspring, born from his union with the mountains, and stirred up revolts against Ninurta, the god of Nippur.

After many setbacks, Ninurta defeated him, having first sought the counsel of his father Enlil. He used the storm of the eight winds and, as the decisive blow, the enchanted mace Sharur. After his death, Asag was turned into a vast bed of zalaqu stones, so deep that it reached the abode of the dead, and the rising waters of the Kur flooded Sumer and brought famine. In another version of the myth, the victor is Adad (Iškur).

Legacy

Scholars read the struggle against Asag and his stone allies as a mythical expression of the unease felt by the people of the Mesopotamian plain toward the peoples of the Zagros Mountains. The battle appears on large stone reliefs that Ashurnasirpal II, in the 9th century BC, had set up in the temple of Ninurta at Kalhu (Nimrud), where a god hurls thunderbolts at a lion-dragon beast. A similar scene appears on Neo-Assyrian seals.

Ancient sources: He is known mainly from the Sumerian poem Lugal-e, also called The Exploits of Ninurta.

Trivia

Boiling fish. According to the poem, his mere presence made fish boil alive in the rivers.
The Seven Asakku. A group of demons called the Seven (or Eight) Asakku, offspring of An, were also said to have been defeated by Ninurta.
