Ibaraki-dōji

Ibaraki-dōji is an oni, a demon or ogre, from Japanese tales of the Heian period. He was the most important servant of Shuten-dōji and is one of the best-known oni in Japanese tradition.

The myth

According to the tales, Ibaraki-dōji was born with teeth and a strength that frightened his family. In a Settsu version, he was abandoned in Ibaraki, Osaka, and taken in by Shuten-dōji, who gave him his name. In another, from Echigo, he became an oni after licking blood from a love letter.

Alongside Shuten-dōji, he attacked the capital and kidnapped young women until Minamoto no Yorimitsu and his four guardians destroyed the gang at Mount Ōe. Ibaraki-dōji escaped, but Watanabe no Tsuna cut off one of his arms with the sword Higekiri. The tradition says he came back to retrieve it, an episode sometimes set at the Ichijō Modorihashi bridge.

Worship and religion

At Yahiko Shrine in Niigata there is a small shrine dedicated to Ibaraki-dōji, and the sword linked to Higekiri is kept at Kitano Tenmangū Shrine in Kyoto.

Legacy

He appears in theatre works such as the Noh play Rashōmon and the kabuki plays Ibaraki and Modorihashi, as well as in the nagauta Tsunayakata.

Ancient sources: Stories of Ibaraki-dōji and Watanabe no Tsuna appear in the Genpei Jōsuiki, the Taiheiki, the Tale of the Heike (Tsurugi no Maki) and the Zentaiheiki, and also in the Otogi-zōshi.

Trivia

The sword Higekiri. Kitano Tenmangū Shrine in Kyoto keeps a sword traditionally linked to the episode of the severed arm. Depending on the legend, it is also called Onikiri, Onimaru, Shishinoko or Tomokiri.
A bridge named after him. In Ibaraki, Osaka, there was a bridge called Ibaraki-dōji Sugatami-bashi, where according to legend he saw his face turned into that of an oni. The bridge no longer exists, but a monument marks its former site.
