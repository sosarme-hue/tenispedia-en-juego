impious king in Greek mythology, son of Aeolus

Salmoneus

Salmoneus is a king of Greek mythology, son of Aeolus and founder of the city of Salmone on the banks of the Alpheius. He is remembered above all as an example of arrogance punished by Zeus.

The myth

According to tradition, Salmoneus left Aeolis with a band of Aeolians and founded a city in Elis, on the river Alpheius, which he named after himself. Sources disagree about his mother, who is called Enarete, Iphis or Laodice depending on the author. He first married Alcidice, daughter of Aleus, and had a daughter, Tyro. After Alcidice died he took Sidero as his second wife, and she treated Tyro cruelly.

Salmoneus and his brother Sisyphus hated each other. Sisyphus learned from an oracle of Apollo that Tyro's children would kill Salmoneus. He married Tyro and had a son by her, but Tyro killed the boy once she understood the danger. Later, in the version the sources preserve, she bore Pelias and Neleus to Poseidon.

Arrogant and impious, Salmoneus demanded that his subjects worship him as Zeus. To imitate thunder he drove his chariot across a bronze bridge, dragging dried hides and cauldrons, and he hurled torches into the air to suggest lightning. Zeus struck him down with a thunderbolt and destroyed the town. Virgil later places him in Tartarus, where he suffers eternal punishment.

Worship and religion

No cult of Salmoneus himself is known. Frazer linked his story to the early Greek kings, who were expected to bring rain for the crops and who imitated thunder and lightning in the manner of Zeus. At Crannon in Thessaly, a bronze chariot was shaken in time of drought while prayers for rain were offered.

Legacy

His story survives mainly in ancient literature, where Virgil places him among the damned in Tartarus. As a figure of hubris punished, his tale has often been read as a warning against the arrogance of the powerful.

Ancient sources: Virgil's Aeneid places him in Tartarus; later sources record his rivalry with Sisyphus and the fate of Tyro.

Trivia

Children of Poseidon. According to the sources, Tyro bore Pelias and Neleus to Poseidon, who later appear in other stories of Greek myth.
A thunderbolt by mistake. According to S. Reinach, the lightning story may come from misreading a painting that showed a Thessalian magician calling down rain and lightning.
