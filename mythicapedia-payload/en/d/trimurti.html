Hindu triad of Brahma, Vishnu and Shiva

Trimurti

The Trimurti is the triple deity of supreme divinity in Hinduism, in which the cosmic functions of creation, preservation and destruction are personified as three gods: Brahma, Vishnu and Shiva. The word means 'three forms' in Sanskrit, and the grouping is a Puranic-era idea that has had far more prominence in Western accounts than in everyday Indian worship.

The myth

According to Puranic tradition, Brahma is the creator of the universe, usually shown with four heads, and should not be confused with Brahman, the impersonal ultimate reality. Vishnu is the preserver, with four arms, and often takes avatars such as Rama or Krishna. Shiva is the wild god who dissolves the world at the end of each cycle.

The Vishnu Purana mentions all three together in a single verse, assigning each a role in the cosmic cycle. The stories, however, do not always keep that division: Brahma appears rarely, and in many tales Vishnu or Shiva takes the part that might have been his. The oldest Vedic texts do not know this trio; in the Rig-veda the leading gods are others, such as Indra, Agni and Soma, or, according to Yaska, Indra, Agni and Surya.

The sage Dattatreya is sometimes regarded as an avatar of Vishnu, Shiva and Brahma at once, shown with three heads on one body.

Worship and religion

Most Hindus in India do not worship the three as a group. They honour each god separately, along with their avatars and consorts. Brahma has very few temples, the best known being at Pushkar. Images show the three side by side, seated or standing, or merged into one body with three heads and six arms.

The consorts complete the scheme: Lakshmi with Vishnu, Parvati or Shakti with Shiva, and Sarasvati with Brahma. Together they form the Tridevi, the feminine counterpart. In Shaktism the triad is recast with goddesses, while in Shaivism the three appear as forms of Shiva. Vaishnavism generally rejects the concept and places Vishnu at the top.

Legacy

The term was popular among British Orientalists, perhaps because of its resemblance to the Christian Trinity, and it passed into Western popular writing from there. In Hinduism, the Om symbol is sometimes read as an allusion to the Trimurti, since its sounds A, U and M are linked with creation, preservation and destruction.

Ancient sources: The Rig-veda, Yaska's Nirukta, the Vishnu Purana and the Maitri Upanishad mention these gods or their functions, although the Puranic trio became established in the Puranas.
