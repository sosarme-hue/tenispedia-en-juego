Egyptian sky and kingship god

Horus

Horus is one of the most important deities of ancient Egypt, associated with the sky, kingship, protection and healing. Depicted as a falcon or as a man with a falcon head, he served as the ideological model for the pharaoh, who was identified with him in life and with Osiris after death.

The myth

According to the best-known myth, Horus is the son of Osiris and Isis. Osiris was murdered by his brother Set, who dismembered his body and scattered the pieces. Isis gathered the fragments, revived her husband with magic and conceived Horus in the marshes of the Nile Delta, where she hid from Set. According to Plutarch, Osiris's penis was never recovered and was eaten by a fish, so Isis supposedly fashioned a magical phallus to conceive. The stories often present Horus as a vulnerable child who grows into the legitimate heir. In other traditions, Hathor is his mother and sometimes his wife.

Horus fought Set for years, both to avenge his father and to decide who should rule Egypt. These struggles, told in The Contendings of Horus and Seth, ended with Horus as the victor, and he came to be associated with Lower Egypt, while Set was linked to Upper Egypt. In the most archaic myth, by contrast, Horus and Set formed a rival divine pair who wounded each other, and Thoth is said to have emerged from their conflict. In the Osirian account, Horus was crowned pharaoh after defeating Set, and his father became king of the afterlife.

As a child, Horus, or Harpocrates, was a fragile being who repeatedly escaped death, which made him a healing and protective god. His left eye, the Udjat, was wounded by Set and cured by Thoth; it represents the Moon, while the right eye is the Sun. The eye thus became a symbol of protection and rebirth.

Worship and religion

Horus was worshipped throughout Egypt from very early times. His first center was Nekhen, the City of the Falcon, in Upper Egypt. In the Ptolemaic temple of Edfu, one of the most beautiful, he received the yearly visit of the statue of Hathor of Dendera and formed a divine triad with Harsomtus. At Kom Ombo he was linked to Sobek, the crocodile god, and his cult spread to Nubia. From the late period, the Isis cults spread the figure of Harpocrates throughout the Mediterranean. As a falcon, his flight was understood as that of the Sun, and his name, meaning the distant one, referred to the daytime star. He is no longer worshipped as a living religion.

Legacy

The Eye of Horus, or wedjat, was one of the most common funerary amulets, believed to protect its wearer; the jewelry of the pharaoh Shoshenq II included it as a central element. Egyptian and Near Eastern sailors painted it on the bows of their ships to ensure a safe voyage. The name Horus also survives in theophoric names such as Harsiese, meaning Horus, son of Isis.

Ancient sources: The Pyramid Texts describe the pharaoh as Horus in life and Osiris in death; The Contendings of Horus and Seth recounts his struggles with Set; Plutarch distinguishes Horus the Elder, and Claudius Aelianus associates Horus with Apollo.

Trivia

Horus the Elder. Plutarch distinguished between Horus, son of Osiris and Isis, and Horus the Elder, a primordial form that the Egyptians equated with the Greek Apollo.
A falcon on Narmer's palette. The sacred falcon appears on the palette of King Narmer at the start of the historical period, and from then on it was constantly associated with royal power.
