Orphic god in Greek mythology

Zagreus

Zagreus is a figure of Greek mythology linked to Orphism, sometimes identified with an Orphic Dionysus, son of Zeus and Persephone, who was torn apart by the Titans and reborn. His myth, widely regarded as the central story of Orphism, explains the origin of humanity and of evil.

The myth

According to Orphic tradition, Zeus, in the form of a serpent, lay with his daughter Persephone and Zagreus was born. In some versions the infant was entrusted to Apollo and the Curetes, or to Rhea, in the hope of making him heir to Olympus. Hera, jealous, incited the Titans, who lured the child with toys and a mirror, tore him apart and devoured his limbs. Only the heart survived, saved by Apollo or by Athena depending on the telling. Zeus recovered it and the child was born again, this time from Semele, under the name Dionysus.

Zeus struck the Titans with lightning, and from their ashes, mixed with the remains of Zagreus, humanity arose. This is why people carry the stain of the Titans and also a spark of the god. Aeschylus placed him in the underworld: in one fragment he seems to be a son of Hades, and in another he is identified with Hades himself.

Worship and religion

Euripides, in his lost play Cretans, describes initiates who lived a pure life after entering the mysteries of Idaean Zeus on Crete, taking part in nocturnal rites with a feast of raw flesh. Scholars link the myth to ancient sacrifices and to rites of passage for young men. There is no evidence that a cult dedicated to him survived.

Ancient sources: Fragments of Aeschylus (Sisyphus and Egyptians), the Alcmeonis, Euripides (Cretans), Callimachus, Plutarch and Nonnus of Panopolis.

Trivia

Two Dionysuses. The historian Diodorus Siculus claims there were two gods named Dionysus, an older one, son of Zeus and Persephone, and a younger one, son of Zeus and Semele, and that people confused them because they shared a name.
An uncertain etymology. The scholar Martin West proposes that Zagreus comes from zagre, a pit for trapping animals, which would make him the god of pitfalls. Other scholars link the name to Greek words for life or to the Zagros Mountains.
