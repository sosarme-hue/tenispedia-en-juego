Hindu goddess of marital happiness and first wife of Shiva

Sati

Sati is the Hindu goddess of marital happiness and long life, and the first wife of Shiva. Her reincarnation is Parvati, and her story, especially her death at her father's sacrifice, shapes key traditions of Shaivism and Shaktism.

The myth

According to the Puranas, Sati was the youngest and most beloved daughter of Daksha, a prajapati or agent of creation and son of the creator Brahma, and of Prasuti, daughter of Swayambhuva Manu. From childhood she adored Shiva and refused the marriage her father had planned for her. She withdrew into the forest to practise austerities until Shiva agreed to marry her, despite Daksha's opposition, since he scorned the ascetic's unusual looks and conduct.

The break came when Daksha held a yajna, a fire sacrifice, to which he invited all the gods except Sati and Shiva. She went anyway, but Daksha humiliated her before the assembly and mocked her husband. To sever ties with her father and defend Shiva's honour, Sati immolated herself. Grief-stricken, Shiva performed the tandava, the dance of destruction, and created the fierce beings Virabhadra and Bhadrakali, who razed the sacrifice. Daksha was beheaded, but Shiva later restored him to life with a goat's head.

Shiva then carried Sati's body across the world. To halt him, Vishnu hurled his Sudarshana chakra, which cut the body into 51 pieces that fell in different places. These are the Shakti pithas, sacred sites for Hindus. Sati was reborn as Parvati, who, like her, drew Shiva out of his ascetic isolation and back into the world.

Worship and religion

Sati is mainly venerated by Hindu women who pray for long life for their husbands. She is regarded as an aspect of Devi, the great goddess. Her name, from sat, 'truth' or 'true', is usually rendered 'faithful' or 'chaste'. Around a thousand names are attributed to her, listed in the Lalita Sahasranama of the Brahmanda Purana, among them Uma and Aparna. She is linked to the sati custom, in which widows were tied to their husband's funeral pyre as a supposed act of loyalty.

Legacy

Her story inspired Kalidasa's epic poem Kumarasambhava. In English, the word 'suttee' was long used for the practice, an older spelling of the term sati.

Ancient sources: The Taittiriya Samhita is the first text to mention Daksha's sacrifice; the story later appears in the Ramayana, the Mahabharata, the Puranas and Kalidasa's Kumarasambhava.

Trivia

Fifty-one sites. According to the story, Sati's body parts fell in 51 different places. Today they are called Shakti pithas and are considered sacred by Hindus.
A thousand names. Sati has around a thousand names, listed in the Lalita Sahasranama, a sacred text of the Brahmanda Purana.
