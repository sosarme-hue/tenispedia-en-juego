Freyja's field in Norse mythology

Fólkvangr

Fólkvangr is the place where the goddess Freyja receives half of the warriors who fall in battle, while the other half go to Odin. It belongs to the Norse tradition, and its name, meaning roughly "field of the host" or "people-field", points to its link with war and heroic death.

The myth

In Norse tradition, Fólkvangr was the dwelling of Freyja in Asgard, the world of the Æsir. From there the goddess chose each day half of those slain in combat, while the other half were received by Odin in Valhalla. The fallen warriors were thus divided between two heroic paradises.

The poem Grímnismál, preserved in the Poetic Edda, mentions it in its list of the gods' dwellings, and Snorri Sturluson describes it in the Prose Edda, where Freyja's hall Sessrúmnir lies within Fólkvangr. The Saga of Egil adds a scene in which Egil's daughter Þorgerðr resolves to starve herself so she can join the goddess.

Legacy

The name inspired the "Folkwang-Gedanke", Karl Ernst Osthaus's idea of reconciling art and life in the early twentieth century. Institutions such as the Museum Folkwang in Essen, the Folkwang-Verlag publishing house and the Folkwang University of the Arts, which covers music, theatre, dance and design, grew from it.

Ancient sources: The Poetic Edda (the poem Grímnismál) and Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda, which describes it in chapter 24 of Gylfaginning.

Trivia

A ship in a field. Scholars Joseph S. Hopkins and Haukur Þorgeirsson connect Fólkvangr and Sessrúmnir with the stone ships of Scandinavian burial sites, an image that may reflect a ship in a field of the dead.
