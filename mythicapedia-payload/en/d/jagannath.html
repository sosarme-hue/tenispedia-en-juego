Hindu god, abstract form of Krishna

Jagannath

Jagannath, also spelled Jagannatha, is a form of Krishna revered as the supreme god in Odia Vaishnavism, worshipped alongside his brother Balabhadra and his sister Subhadra. His wooden idol, with an abstract face and no arms or legs, makes him one of the most distinctive deities in Hinduism.

The myth

According to tradition, King Indradyumna of Puri found a huge log on the shore of the Indian Ocean. He claimed Krishna had appeared to him and ordered him to carve a deity from it. He hired a Brahmin sculptor, who asked not to be disturbed. When the workshop fell silent, the impatient king opened the door and found the sculptor gone, leaving the image unfinished. Another version credits Vishvakarma, the architect of the gods, who was dissatisfied with his own rough work.

Another account explains the limbless figure: on hearing Krishna's youthful deeds, Subhadra and Balarama fell into ecstasy, and their arms and legs drew into their bodies.

Worship and religion

The great temple at Puri, built in the Kalinga style, is one of Hinduism's most important centres and one of the Char Dham sites. Its best-known festival, the Ratha Yatra, carries the three idols on huge wooden chariots pulled by volunteers to the Gundicha Temple, drawing close to a million devotees. Worship continues today, and the sanctuary is closed to non-Hindus.

Legacy

The English word juggernaut comes from the name Jagannath, through the chariot processions that British observers described. Similar processions are now held at temples around the world.

Ancient sources: The Puranas, such as the Bhavishya Purana, which recommends neem wood for images of Vishnu, and the Srimad Bhagavatam, which describes Krishna ascending to heaven in his own body.

Trivia

Sacred wood. The idol is carved from neem wood and replaced with a new one roughly every 12 or 19 years.
Vishnu's discus. The image is accompanied by the Sudarshana Chakra, the characteristic weapon of Vishnu.
