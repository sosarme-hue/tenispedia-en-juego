Athenian hero of Greek mythology

Lycus

Lycus is a hero of Greek mythology, a son of Pandion II, king of Athens, and brother of Aegeus. He is remembered mainly for his role in the struggle for power in Athens, his flight to Messenia, and the name he gave to Lycia in Asia Minor. Ancient authors do not always agree on his family.

The myth

According to the tradition, after Pandion's death, Lycus and his brothers Aegeus, Nisus and Pallas seized Athens from Metion, who had usurped the throne from their father. They split the government four ways, and Lycus received the island of Euboea. Aegeus, however, eventually outmaneuvered his brothers and became king of all Attica.

Driven out by Aegeus, Lycus took refuge in Messenia. Pausanias reports that he came to Aphareus and, together with his wife Arene, initiated him and his family into the rites of the Great Goddesses, Demeter and Persephone. Later, according to tradition, Sarpedon welcomed him to the land of the Termilae, which was renamed Lycia in his honour, a name Herodotus also records.

Worship and religion

In Athens he was venerated as a hero, and the name of the Lyceum is said to derive from his, a place tied to the city.

Legacy

His name survives in Lycia, the region of Asia Minor that bears it according to Herodotus, and in the word Lyceum, which comes from his. Some modern scholars, such as Treuber, have questioned whether his family tree was ever real and have suggested political motives behind the tradition.

Ancient sources: Herodotus records that he gave his name to Lycia, formerly known as Termilae, and Pausanias describes how he initiated Aphareus into the rites of the goddesses.

Trivia

A country named after him. According to Herodotus, the region of Lycia in Asia Minor took its name from Lycus, formerly known as Tremilis or Termilae.
A myth under suspicion. The German scholar Treuber considered the story of Lycus a pseudo-myth of no great antiquity, perhaps fostered by political motives.
