serpent-like creature of Nordic and Central European folklore

Lindworm

The lindworm is a creature of Nordic, Germanic and Central European folklore, a serpent-like relative of the dragon. Its name combines lind with worm, a word meaning snake or dragon, and it is one of the most characteristic beasts of northern European forest and mountain tales.

The myth

According to tradition, the lindworm is a large reptile that lives hidden in the forest, among rocks or in mountain gorges. In Swedish lore it had no limbs, a dark back, a mane or fin-like ridge along the spine, and could spit a substance that blinded its enemies. In Central Europe it is usually shown as a dragon with two or four legs, sometimes with wings.

The most widespread belief holds that whatever lies beneath a lindworm grows at the creature's pace, which gave rise to tales of dragons guarding treasure to grow richer. Legends distinguish two kinds: a benevolent one linked to luck, often a cursed prince transformed into a beast, and a dangerous one that devours any human who crosses its path. To pursue its prey it can bite its own tail and roll like a wheel at great speed.

Legacy

The lindworm appears in heraldry, where a wingless or four-legged dragon serves as the emblem of some towns, such as Klagenfurt in Austria. In that city, the 16th-century Lindwurm Fountain has a head that some have speculated was modelled on a woolly rhinoceros skull found in a nearby quarry in 1335. In the 19th century, the Swedish folklorist Hyltén-Cavallius collected sightings in Småland and offered a reward that nobody ever claimed. The English legend of the Lambton Worm also features a serpentine monster related to this myth.

Ancient sources: The word already appears in the Nibelungenlied in a form meaning roughly lin-dragon, and the late 13th-century Old Norse Þiðreks saga uses the term linnormr.

Trivia

A skull as model. It has been suggested that the head of the Lindwurm Fountain in Klagenfurt was inspired by a woolly rhinoceros skull found in a quarry in 1335, which makes it one of the earliest reconstructions of an extinct animal.
An unclaimed reward. In 1884, the folklorist Hyltén-Cavallius offered a cash prize for a captured lindworm, dead or alive. Nobody claimed it, and the episode ended in failure.
