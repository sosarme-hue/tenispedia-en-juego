Hindu goddess of clouds and dusk

Saranyu

Saranyu, also known as Sanjna, is a Hindu goddess, the chief consort of the sun god Surya and mother of Yama, Manu and the twin Ashvins. Her name, linked to swiftness and flowing, has been read as a storm cloud, and her flight from her husband is one of the most enigmatic stories in the Rig Veda.

The myth

According to the tradition, Saranyu was the daughter of the divine craftsman Tvashtr, admired for her beauty, virtue and ascetic powers. She married Vivasvant, the Sun, but could not endure the intensity of his blazing form. To escape, she created a double with her own appearance, called Chhaya, entrusted her children to her, and fled in the shape of a mare.

The Sun discovered the deception, took the form of a stallion and followed her. Their union produced the twin Ashvins, half horse and half human. In the Rig Veda she abandons her mortal children, Yama and Yami, and the newborn Ashvins. In the Nirukta she flees of her own will, and the Brhaddevata adds that she inhaled the seed fallen on the ground to conceive the twins.

In the Harivamsa the story takes a different turn. The name becomes Samjna and the double becomes her shadow. While the true wife wanders the northern lands, Chhaya raises the children, favoring her own son, Manu Savarni, which leads to conflict with Yama.

Legacy

Her name has been connected with Sarayu, the river goddess. Max Muller and Adalbert Kuhn compared her with Demeter, and some scholars linked her to Helen of Troy because of her abduction. For the Indologist Wendy Doniger, the shift from Saranyu to Samjna reflects a reflection on identity, shadow and appearance, and her double may represent twilight, Sandhya, figured as a second wife of the sun.

Ancient sources: Rig Veda, Yaska's Nirukta, Brhaddevata, Harivamsa and Skanda Purana.

Trivia

A sun named dead egg. In the Harivamsa, Surya is called Martanda, 'dead egg', because his intense heat has disfigured his limbs and darkened his skin.
A double named shadow. The name Chhaya means 'shadow'. In the Harivamsa, the double takes the goddess's place as mother of the children.
