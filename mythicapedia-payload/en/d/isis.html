Egyptian goddess of magic, wisdom and motherhood

Isis

Isis is one of the major goddesses of ancient Egyptian religion, daughter of Geb and Nut and wife of her brother Osiris. Her figure, linked to magic, motherhood and the protection of the dead, matters because her worship spread across the whole Greco-Roman world and lasted for centuries.

The myth

According to the myth, Isis is the daughter of Geb, god of the earth, and Nut, goddess of the sky, and she belongs to the Ennead of Heliopolis, a family of nine deities descended from the creator Atum or Ra. She is married to her brother Osiris, who inherits the world's authority and becomes king. Their other brother, Set, kills him and, in several versions, dismembers his corpse.

Isis, together with her sister Nephthys and other deities such as Anubis, searches for the pieces of the body and reassembles them. According to some texts, she must also protect the body from further desecration. Her grief as a widow, her love and even her anger at a husband who has left her, voiced through magic words, help bring him back to life. Afterwards, Isis restores his breath and conceives Horus with him, the son who will avenge his father. From then on, Osiris lives only in the Duat, the underworld. She was also regarded as the divine mother of the pharaoh, who was likened to Horus. This tradition made Isis the model of the mourning widow and the mythic basis for mummification and other funerary rites of ancient Egypt.

Worship and religion

At first Isis played a limited role in royal rituals and temple rites, while she was more prominent in funerary practices and magical texts. She was invoked in healing spells to help ordinary people, and she was believed to assist the dead in entering the afterlife. Her magical power was considered greater than that of all other gods, and she was said to govern the heavens and the natural world. Rulers of Egypt and Nubia built temples dedicated mainly to her, and her sanctuary at Philae became a religious centre for both regions.

In the Hellenistic period her worship, alongside that of the god Serapis, spread across the Mediterranean. Greek devotees credited her with traits taken from other goddesses, such as the invention of marriage and the protection of ships at sea. In Rome her followers developed their own festivals, such as the Navigium Isidis, and initiation ceremonies resembling those of other Greco-Roman mystery cults. Her cult came to an end with the rise of Christianity. Some scholars have suggested it may have influenced the veneration of Mary, although the evidence for this is ambiguous and much debated.

Legacy

Isis continues to appear in Western culture, particularly in esotericism and modern paganism, where she is often seen as a personification of nature or as the feminine aspect of divinity. Her name, transcribed into the Latin alphabet, is the basis of her modern name.

Ancient sources: The Pyramid Texts mention her and link her with the Nile Delta, New Kingdom literature developed her role in the Osiris myth, and Ptolemaic funerary texts stress her active part in the conception of Horus.

Trivia

Hathor's headdress. During the New Kingdom, Isis took on traits that had originally belonged to Hathor, the preeminent goddess of earlier periods, and began to be shown wearing her headdress: a sun disk between the horns of a cow.
A name with a throne. Her name is written with the sign of a throne, which she also wears on her head. The Egyptologist Kurt Sethe believed she was originally a personification of thrones, although other scholars have questioned this idea.
