storm bird of Mesopotamian mythology

Anzû

Anzû, also known as Imdugud, is a being from Sumerian and Akkadian mythology, half bird and half lion, personifying the south wind and the thunderclouds. He is best known for the story of stealing the Tablet of Destinies.

The myth

According to tradition, this bird-demon, described in Babylonian texts as an eagle with a lion's head that could breathe fire and water, was conceived by the waters of Apsu and the Earth, or was considered a son of Siris. The oldest Sumerian stories show him as a half-human storm bird who stole the Tablet of Destinies from Enlil, which conferred supreme power, and hid it on a mountaintop.

The gods feared the monster, and an assembly decided that Ninurta should confront him. According to one version, Marduk killed him; according to another, he died from Ninurta's arrows. Defeated, he was banished, and the city of Ur, where he was worshipped, was destroyed. Anzû also appears in the story of Inanna and the Huluppu Tree and in the tale of Lugalbanda.

Worship and religion

He was worshipped in Ur, according to the texts. Some scholars link him to the god Abu, the "Father Pasture", associated with the storms that make the spring fields grow. His cult does not survive today.

Legacy

In modern fantasy, Faith Hunter revives him in her Jane Yellowrock novels, where anzu are beings able to take human or bird form and are linked to vampires. His name has also sparked philological debate over the correct reading of the cuneiform signs.

Ancient sources: The Epic of Anzû in its Old Babylonian (Susa) and Late Assyrian (Nineveh) versions, and the story of Inanna and the Huluppu Tree, in the preamble to the Sumerian poem of Gilgamesh.

Trivia

Two names. For a long time he was read as Zu because of confusion with the sign AN, and some researchers now argue that Imdugud was the original pronunciation.
Bird or lion-headed bird. According to Jacobsen, the early god was a thundercloud shaped like an eagle, to which a lion's head was later added to evoke thunder.
