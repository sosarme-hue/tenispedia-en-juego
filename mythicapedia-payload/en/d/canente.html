Roman nymph, personification of song

Canens

Canens is, in Roman mythology, the personification of song: a nymph of Latium, daughter of Janus and Venilia, whose story of love and loss links her to the origin of Faunus.

The myth

According to Roman tradition, Canens was a nymph of great beauty born on the Palatine. Among the nymphs of Latium she stood out for her singing, so powerful that wild beasts followed her and her voice could move stones and trees or soften the temper of men. She was the daughter of Janus and the nymph Venilia, and she married Picus, king of the Latins.

Circe tried to seduce Picus, but he remained faithful to his wife. In revenge, the sorceress turned him into a woodpecker. Canens searched for him for six days without eating or sleeping, through forests and mountains. Exhausted, she sang her misfortune one last time and lay down on the banks of the Tiber, where she died consumed by grief. According to Ovid, in the Metamorphoses, her body vanished into the air and only her voice remained. The place where her life ended was named after her. From her union with Picus, Faunus was born.

Legacy

The story gave her name to the place where she died, according to tradition, and her figure became associated with a voice that outlives the body.

Ancient sources: Ovid mentions her in the Metamorphoses, where he describes her death on the banks of the Tiber.
