Greek god of wine and ecstasy

Dionysus

Dionysus is the Greek god of wine, the vine and fertility, and one of the twelve Olympians in the classical tradition. The Romans knew him as Bacchus, and his cult, built on sacred intoxication and theatre, pushed against the limits of social and civic order.

The myth

According to Hesiod, Dionysus was born in Thebes, the son of Zeus and Semele, daughter of King Cadmus. Other traditions make him the son of Zeus and Persephone, and Orphic writers present him as the son of Zeus who was born twice. The Homeric Hymns speak of his double birth and of the Nysiads, the nymphs who raised him on Mount Nysa and who, at Hermes' command, made him immortal.

According to Homer, he made a mythic journey through Thrace and other lands to assert his divine nature, which is why he often appears in tragedy as a foreigner arriving in Greece. He was credited with discovering the vine, and he boasted of having spread the drinking of wine.

In the Homeric Hymns, pirates kidnap him, but the god makes a vine heavy with grapes and a stream of fragrant wine flow across the ship, and his captors are driven mad. The best-known story comes from Euripides' Bacchae: King Pentheus of Thebes opposes his cult, and Dionysus drives him into madness until his own mother, Agave, tears him apart, believing him to be a lion. The daughters of Minyas, who refused to join his rites, end up devouring one of their own sons under a madness sent by the god, and are then transformed into bats.

In the Orphic tradition, the god becomes a symbol of indestructible life (zoe), in contrast with mortal life (bios). According to this myth, he was torn apart by the Titans, and humankind was born from their ashes, with a Titanic body and a divine, immortal soul of Dionysian origin.

Worship and religion

Dionysus was honoured in festivals such as the Athenian Dionysia, when everyday work was suspended, prisoners were released on bail and social roles were reversed through the use of masks. The Anthesteria was another civic celebration in Athens. His female followers, the maenads or bacchantes, danced on the mountains possessed by the god, and their trance rites included practices such as eating raw flesh and ritual dismemberment. There were also Dionysian mysteries, celebrated alongside the Eleusinian ones. Dithyrambs, choral songs in his honour, gave rise to tragedy in Athens, according to Aristotle. In Rome, the Bacchanalia were restricted by the state, which regarded them as subversive.

Legacy

The name Bacchus gave rise to the English adjective bacchic and to the word bacchanal. Modern philosophy took up the idea of the Dionysian as a force of excess and vitality. His link with the theatre makes him one of the gods most present in Western culture.

Ancient sources: Hesiod places him in Thebes as the son of Zeus and Semele; Homer and the Homeric Hymns recount his journey through Thrace and the episode of the pirates; Euripides' Bacchae is the most important source on his cult.

Trivia

A Mycenaean name. His name appears on Linear B tablets found at Pylos, dated to around the 13th century BC, linked to offerings of wine.
A foreign god. Almost all the tragedies present him as a foreigner who arrives from outside, a god of epiphany.
