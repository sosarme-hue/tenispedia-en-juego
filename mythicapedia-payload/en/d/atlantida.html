mythical island described by Plato

Atlantis

Atlantis is a mythical island that the Greek philosopher Plato describes in his dialogues Timaeus and Critias, where it appears as a powerful maritime empire defeated by Athens and swallowed by the sea in a single dreadful day and night. Although it is now regarded as fiction, it remains one of the most influential ideas about lost civilizations.

The myth

According to the story, Atlantis lay beyond the Pillars of Hercules and was larger than Libya and Asia Minor combined. Poseidon, who had received the island in the division of the world among the gods, fell in love with Cleito, daughter of Evenor, one of the first men born from the earth. To protect her, or to keep her captive, he surrounded the mountain where she lived with three rings of water. Together they had five pairs of twins. The eldest, Atlas, received the central kingdom and authority over his brothers, and the island and the surrounding sea were named after him, the Atlantic.

The Atlanteans raised a magnificent acropolis with a royal palace and a temple to Poseidon, dug canals, and built walls of red, white and black stone covered in brass, tin and orichalcum, a metal prized above gold. For generations they ruled justly, but when their divine nature weakened, arrogance and the desire for domination took hold. They tried to extend their power over the western Mediterranean until Athens, leading an alliance, stopped them.

Then, according to the tale, a catastrophe that Plato does not describe made the island and the rival armies vanish. The sea became choked with shoals and mud, and the Athenians and the Greeks forgot the event. Only in Egypt was the memory preserved, passed by the priests of Sais to Solon and later recounted by Critias. The story breaks off unfinished in the Critias, just as the author began to describe the punishment of the gods.

Legacy

Plato used Atlantis as an allegory of the arrogance of a rival state set against his ideal city, yet it later inspired Renaissance utopias such as Francis Bacon's New Atlantis. In the nineteenth century, amateur scholars took it for real history, most famously Ignatius Donnelly in Atlantis: The Antediluvian World, and since then the name has served as shorthand for any advanced, vanished civilization. Today it still appears in comic books and films. Philologists agree that the story is fictional, though debate continues over what inspired it, from the eruption of Thera to the failed Athenian expedition against Sicily.

Ancient sources: Plato's dialogues Timaeus and Critias, written around 360 BC, are the only primary sources. The logographer Hellanicus of Lesbos wrote an earlier work also titled Atlantis, of which only fragments survive.

Trivia

The name of the Atlantic. The ocean surrounding the island took its name from Atlas, the eldest son of Poseidon and Cleito, according to Plato's account.
Aristotle's view. Aristotle believed that Plato, his teacher, had invented the island in order to teach philosophy.
