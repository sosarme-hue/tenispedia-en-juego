Greek god of shepherds, flocks and the wild

Pan

Pan is the Greek god of shepherds, flocks, mountains and rustic music, with a man's body and the legs, horns and tail of a goat. His worship took root mainly in Arcadia, and his name gave rise to the word panic.

The myth

According to tradition, Pan was the son of Hermes, though ancient authors disagree about his mother. The Homeric Hymns name an unnamed daughter of Dryops, Herodotus records the version in which the mother is Penelope, wife of Odysseus, and other texts mention Callisto or Hybris. Pan was born ugly, with goatish features, and he is described as a simple, cheerful and somewhat lazy being who loved music and dancing, though he also knew the art of prophecy.

His most famous story is that of Syrinx, an Arcadian nymph who fled his advances and was turned into reeds. From them Pan made the syrinx, his pan flute. Hellenistic poets gave him many erotic adventures with nymphs and young men. Pan also punished those who disturbed his afternoon nap with a sudden shriek that made their hair stand on end. In the war against the Titans he helped Zeus with a piercing cry that scattered them in terror, and in the struggle with Typhon, Pan and Hermes recovered Zeus's stolen sinews.

Worship and religion

Pan's worship began in Arcadia, where he was honoured in caves and grottoes rather than in temples. There were sanctuaries such as the one in the gorge of the Neda River, and at Troezen he was invoked as the Deliverer after a plague. In the fourth century BC he appeared on the coins of Pantikapaion. An altar to Pan at Banias, with a Greek inscription from the second or third century AD, shows that his cult lasted into late antiquity. In the syncretic mystery cults of the Hellenistic era he was identified with Phanes, Zeus, Dionysus and Eros. Ancient sources: the Homeric Hymns, Pindar, Herodotus, Apollodorus, Hyginus and Lucian mention him.

Legacy

Pan became an important figure in the Romantic movement of Western Europe and in twentieth-century Neopagan thought. His name gave us the word panic, for a sudden, collective fear. In late antiquity the Church Fathers linked him with the goatish image of the devil to discredit him. In Rome he was identified with Faunus, a nature god.

Ancient sources: The Homeric Hymns, Pindar, Herodotus, Apollodorus, Hyginus, Philostratus and Lucian mention him.

Trivia

Pan and Pushan. The link between Pan and the Vedic god Pushan, both associated with goats, was first proposed in 1924 by the German scholar Hermann Collitz.
Capricorn. A myth recorded by Hyginus tells that when Typhon attacked Pan, he dove into the Nile: the part above water stayed a goat, while the submerged part became a fish. Zeus placed him among the stars in admiration of his cunning.
