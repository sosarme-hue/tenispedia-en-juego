water fairy of Asturian mythology

Xana

The xana is one of the best-known figures of Asturian folklore, a fairy-like being linked to fountains, rivers and waterfalls with pure water. The name covers several kinds of fairies, and it survives in many place names across Asturias and León.

The myth

According to tradition, xanas are women of extraordinary beauty with long fair hair, who comb it beside the water, though some accounts describe them as small, slender and very dark. They may steal a human baby and leave a xanín, their own child, in its place; the changeling seems to grow within months and is unmasked only by the fire and a pile of eggshells.

Another group of tales tells of the enchanted xana, who waits by a fountain on the night of Saint John's for a brave man to break her spell. In "The Four-Peaked Bun", a man receives a loaf from her and must keep it intact for a year; his wife eats one of its peaks, the bread turns into a lame horse and the attempt fails. In "The Serpent Woman", the hero must let the monstrous xana coil around him and kiss her, though fear sometimes brings punishment instead.

Legacy

The Cuban writer Daína Chaviano, of French-Asturian descent, uses the xana motif in her novel The Island of Eternal Love. Xon de la Campa modernises the figure in Über-Xana y el fugáu.
