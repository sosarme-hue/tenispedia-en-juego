Talos

Talos is a bronze automaton, or in other traditions a man of the Bronze Age, who guarded the island of Crete in Greek myth. He is best known from the episode in which he confronts the Argonauts on their return voyage.

The myth

According to tradition, Talos was a bronze guardian who protected Crete from pirates and invaders. Some versions say Hephaestus made him, while others describe him as a survivor of the Bronze Race. He circled the island three times a day, hurling boulders at approaching ships, or heated himself until he glowed and embraced the strangers who came ashore.

In Apollonius of Rhodes's Argonautica, Talos halts the Argonauts as they return with the Golden Fleece. Medea defeats him with magic: she summons the keres, spirits of death, and makes him wound his ankle, where a single vein is covered only by thin skin. His ichor drains out and he dies. Other versions say Medea drove him mad with drugs, or pulled a bronze nail from his ankle, and one account has the Argonaut Poeas kill him with an arrow.

Genealogies vary. Pausanias makes him a son of Cres, the personification of Crete, and father of Hephaestus; in another tradition he is one of the sons of Oenopion. In a story told by Simonides, Talos seizes Sardinian sailors who refuse to bring him to Minos and leaps with them into the flames.

Legacy

His image as a bronze giant reached the screen in the film Jason and the Argonauts (1963). Ancient art shows him on vases from around 400 BCE, and on coins from Phaistos he appears winged. The phrase 'sardonic laugh' is linked to his story in ancient lexicons.

Ancient sources: Apollonius of Rhodes's Argonautica, the Library of Pseudo-Apollodorus, Pausanias, Simonides, Sophocles's lost Daidalos, and the dialogue Minos, attributed to Plato.

Trivia

A single weak point. Talos was invulnerable except for a vein running down to his ankle. In Apollonius, only a thin layer of skin protected it.
A winged guardian. On three coins from Phaistos, dating to the Classical and early Hellenistic periods, he appears naked and winged, throwing a stone. Richard Buxton reads the wings as a sign of the speed needed to circle the island.
