English personification of Christmas

Father Christmas

Father Christmas is the traditional English name for the personification of Christmas, now almost synonymous with Santa Claus. He grew out of an older English folk tradition that had no connection with the American Santa Claus, and he matters because he shows how Christmas gradually acquired a human face and became the gift-bringer we know today.

The myth

Personifications of Christmas appear in England from the 15th century. In a carol attributed to Richard Smart, Rector of Plymtree from 1435 to 1477, Sir Christemas announces the birth of Christ and encourages his listeners to drink and sing. In Norwich in 1443, John Gladman rode in a pageant disguised as the 'King of Christmas', and in York the Riding of Yule and his Wife was held for years until it was suppressed in 1572.

In the 17th century the character was an old, bearded and venerable man. Ben Jonson presented him in Christmas, His Masque (1616) dressed in out-of-date fashions, and in 1638 a masque by Thomas Nabbes described him as an old gentleman in a fur gown. During the Puritan ban on Christmas, Royalist pamphleteers turned him into a symbol of 'the good old days' of feasting and good cheer.

Until Victorian times he had no connection with children, presents, chimneys or reindeer. When Christmas became a family festival centred on children, he took on the attributes of the American Santa Claus, whose myth reached England in the 1850s. By the 1880s both names were used for the same nocturnal visitor.

Legacy

Modern dictionaries treat Father Christmas and Santa Claus as synonyms, and the name remains in English use for the Christmas figure. The character was also linked to the mummers' plays, folk Christmas performances that survived through the 18th and 19th centuries.

Trivia

An old-fashioned Christmas. In 1616 Ben Jonson dressed his Old Christmas in round hose, long stockings and white shoes, styles that were already out of date.
A royalist symbol. Royalist pamphleteers used Father Christmas to defend the old Christmas customs that the Puritans had abolished in 1647.
