river of lamentation in the Greek underworld

Cocytus

Cocytus (Greek Kokytos, "lamentation") is one of the rivers of the underworld in Greek myth, a tributary of the Acheron and the river of the wailing dead. It is best known from Dante's Divine Comedy, which turned it into a frozen lake where traitors are punished.

The myth

In Greek tradition, Cocytus was one of the rivers surrounding Hades, alongside the Acheron, Styx, Phlegethon and Lethe. Some authors placed it in Thesprotia, in Epirus, while others had it flow through Arcadia, so its link to the underworld was not always fixed. Homer has it rise from the Styx and join the Acheron and Pyriphlegethon, while Plato describes its waters winding down to Tartarus.

The river was above all a place of lament and punishment. Seneca describes its stagnant waters, surrounded by ill-omened birds and gloomy plants, and some accounts say the Erinys Tisiphone gave her snakes drink from its sulphurous waters. Plato adds that impious souls condemned to Tartarus were swept along its currents after a year's wait. In Roman tradition, especially in Virgil, Cocytus became the chief river of Hades.

Legacy

Cocytus appears in Milton's Paradise Lost, Shakespeare's Titus Andronicus, a poem by Schiller and Rafael Sabatini's novel Captain Blood. Its strongest legacy, however, is in Dante's Divine Comedy, where it forms the ninth and lowest circle of Hell: a frozen lake divided into four zones, Caina, Antenora, Ptolomea and Judecca, according to the gravity of the treachery, and at whose centre Satan chews Judas, Brutus and Cassius.

Ancient sources: Homer, Plato, Virgil, Seneca, Pausanias and Apuleius mention it, among other ancient authors.

Trivia

A lake instead of a river. In Dante's Divine Comedy, Cocytus is described as a frozen lake rather than a river, although its source is said to be the tears of a statue on Mount Ida.
A name meaning lament. Kokytos means "lamentation", so the river takes its name from the wailing heard in the underworld.
