Syrian sun god, the «God of the Mountain»

El-Gabal

El-Gabal, the «God of the Mountain», was a sun deity worshipped in Emesa (modern Homs, Syria). His cult reached the heart of Rome during the reign of Emperor Elagabalus, who had served as hereditary high priest of this god before becoming emperor.

The myth

His name combines El, the chief deity of the Canaanite pantheon, and gabal, meaning «mountain» in related Semitic languages. El-Gabal was therefore the form that El took in Emesa. The sacred stone of his temple was a black conical meteorite, the symbol of the god. According to Herodian, Elagabalus danced around the altar to the sound of drums and cymbals, forcing senators to watch, and each summer solstice he paraded the stone through the city on a chariot decorated with gold and jewels.

When the emperor brought the cult to Rome, he reportedly placed his god even above Jupiter and gave him as a wife Astarte, Minerva or Urania, or a combination of the three, according to Cassius Dio. The most sacred relics of Roman religion were moved to the Elagabalium so that no other god would be worshipped. After the emperor's murder in 222, his religious edicts were reversed and the cult returned to Emesa.

Worship and religion

In Emesa, a dynasty of Arab origin served as the priests of the temple. During the second century the cult spread to other parts of the empire, and dedications have been found as far away as Woerden, in today's Netherlands. The emperor reportedly ordered that Jews, Samaritans and Christians also perform their rites in the Elagabalium.

Legacy

His name gave the emperor his nickname, Heliogabalus (Elagabalus), by which he is still known.

Ancient sources: Herodian and Cassius Dio describe his cult in Rome and the emperor's religious reforms.

Trivia

A stone fallen from the sky. The god's most revered object was a black, cone-shaped meteorite, kept in a temple on the Palatine Hill.
Free food at the solstice. The emperor's summer festivals were popular with ordinary people, since food was handed out free during the procession of the stone.
A comet as an omen. Some modern researchers have linked the stone's move to Rome with the passage of Halley's Comet in May 218, which may have been read as an omen.
