wife of Cycnus, daughter of Tragasus

Philonome

Philonome is a figure in Greek mythology, the second wife of King Cycnus of Colonae. Her story, in which she falsely accuses her stepson Tenes, is a classic example of the Potiphar's wife motif, found in many traditions.

The myth

According to the tradition, Philonome, daughter of Tragasus, married Cycnus after the death of his first wife, Proclea. She fell in love with his son Tenes and tried to seduce him, but he refused. Scorned, she accused him to his father of attacking her and brought a flute-player, called Eumolpus or Molpus depending on the version, as her false witness.

Cycnus believed her and locked Tenes and his sister Hemithea in a chest that he set adrift at sea. The chest washed up on the island of Leucophrys, which later took the name Tenedos in honour of Tenes. When Cycnus learnt the truth, he had the flute-player stoned and buried Philonome alive.

Worship and religion

According to a local tradition, the myth explained why flute-players were barred from the shrine of Tenes on Tenedos, although the detail is not consistent across authors.

Legacy

Her story is a typical example of the Potiphar's wife motif, a common theme in mythology and folklore.

Ancient sources: A fragment of the mostly lost tragedy Tennes, dubiously attributed to Euripides (5th century BC), and Lycophron (4th century BC).

Trivia

From chest to island. The island of Leucophrys, where the chest with the siblings washed up, later took the name Tenedos in honour of Tenes.
A banned flute-player. The flute-player's role in the story was used, according to one tradition, to justify excluding such musicians from the shrine of Tenes.
