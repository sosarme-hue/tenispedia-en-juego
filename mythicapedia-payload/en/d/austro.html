Roman god of the south wind

Auster

Auster is the Roman god of the south wind, linked to the warm, humid weather at the end of summer and to the sirocco. His Greek equivalent was Notos, and he was shown holding an inverted urn from which the rain he brought would fall.

The myth

According to Roman tradition, Auster is one of the four principal winds, each tied to a compass point, and he is the son of Aeolus and Aurora. He is imagined as a young man with his cloak lifted and an inverted urn, a symbol of the rains he tended to release. His moisture and storms were feared, since they could ruin the harvest.

His figure is also associated with other southern winds: the Euro-Auster, which blew from Egypt, and the Austro-Africus, sometimes called Libonotus or Notos, which came from Sidra. These names show how the Romans placed each wind in a specific region of the Mediterranean.

Trivia

A wind named after a region. The Euro-Auster was linked to Egypt, while the Austro-Africus came from Sidra, showing the geography the ancients associated with each wind.
