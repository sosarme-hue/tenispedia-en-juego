Hindu goddess of aspirations, consort of Kartikeya

Devasena

Devasena is a goddess of Hinduism, consort of the war god Kartikeya (Murugan) and, in South India, one of his two wives alongside Valli. Her name means "army of the gods", and it gives rise to her husband's title, Devasenapati, which also refers to his role as commander of the divine hosts.

The myth

According to the Mahabharata, Devasena and her sister Daityasena were daughters of Daksha. While they were relaxing by Lake Manasa, the asura Keshi abducted them intending to marry them. Devasena refused, while Daityasena agreed. The gods were losing to the demons, and Indra, searching for a suitable commander, freed Devasena, who asked him to find a husband able to protect her.

Indra and Brahma agreed that a son of Agni would be the right choice. Agni was compelled to father him, and Kartikeya was born. After proving his supremacy over the gods, he was made general of the celestial army and wed Devasena. In this version, Agni is identified with Shiva, who is proclaimed Kartikeya's father.

Tamil manuscripts of the Skanda Purana give a different lineage: Devasena is a daughter of Vishnu, born as Amritavalli, and was adopted by Indra after a penance. Indra gave her in marriage to Murugan after his victory over the asuras. The couple settled in the hills of Thiruttani or in Svarga, the abode of the gods. The Kanda Purana describes her as Indra's daughter, without mention of adoption.

Tamil tradition also records tensions between the two wives. In one version, Devayanai cursed Valli for trying to woo Murugan before her own marriage, which is why Valli was born a huntress. In the Paripatal, the rivalry ends in battle between Devayanai's royal soldiers and Valli's hunter clan, who win. In Sri Lankan folklore, Murugan stays with Valli at Kataragama, and Devayanai eventually joins them.

Worship and religion

In Tamil Nadu, Devasena does not receive independent worship; she is venerated as Murugan's consort in most of his temples. She has a prominent role at the Tirupparankunram temple, believed to be the site of their marriage. In East India she is worshipped as Shashthi, where she does receive independent veneration.

Ancient sources: The Mahabharata, the Skanda Purana (in its Tamil versions) and the Kanda Purana tell her story. The Paripatal and the Thirumurugatrupadai of Tamil Sangam literature mention Devayanai.
