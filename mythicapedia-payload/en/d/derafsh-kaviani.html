legendary royal standard of pre-Islamic Persia

Derafsh Kaviani

The Derafsh Kaviani was the legendary royal standard of Persia, linked to the blacksmith Kaveh and the mythical kings of Iranian tradition. It served as the imperial standard of the Sasanians and remains a symbol of Persian identity today.

The myth

According to Iranian tradition, the tyrant Zahhak ruled for generations with two serpents on his shoulders, which he fed with human beings every day. When his servants took the youngest son of Kaveh the blacksmith, Kaveh, enraged, raised a banner as a sign of rebellion. His leather apron, fixed to a wooden spear, was later decorated by Fereydun with gems, gold and brocade, and with tassels of red, yellow (or blue) and violet. Fereydun, the legitimate heir, eventually overthrew Zahhak.

Tradition holds that the banner passed through the Achaemenid, Seleucid, Parthian and Sasanian dynasties, and its name means "Standard of the Kings" or "Standard of Kaveh". Avestan, Achaemenid and Parthian texts do not mention it explicitly, and physical evidence before the Sasanians is scarce.

Legacy

After the Muslim conquest, the standard became an emblem of Iranian nationalism. Ya'qub ibn al-Layth, founder of the Saffarid dynasty, invoked it in his revolt against Arab rule. In Mughal India, the poet Ghalib gave one of his writings the title Derafsh-e kaviyani in 1865. Today it appears at protests against the Islamic Republic and in Persian literature, and since 2006 the president of Tajikistan has included it in his official standard.

Ancient sources: Avestan, Achaemenid and Parthian texts do not name it explicitly; Xenophon describes a golden eagle as the Achaemenid standard.

Trivia

A golden eagle. Xenophon describes a golden eagle as the standard of the Achaemenids, a different emblem from the one tradition attributes to the blacksmith Kaveh.
Shown on coins. Iranian coins from the 3rd and 2nd centuries BCE depict the standard, including those of Baydad, frataraka of Persis under the Seleucids.
