legendary Swedish king of the House of Yngling

Agne

Agne is a semi-legendary king of Sweden from the House of Yngling, best known from the Norse sagas of the Yngling kings. His story, in which a revenge plot ends with his death at a feast, is one of the best-known Scandinavian tales about the early kings of Sweden.

The myth

According to Snorri Sturluson, Agne was the son of Dag the Wise. He became a mighty and famous king, skilled in many ways. On a summer expedition to Finland he defeated a chief named Frosti, killed many Finns and subdued the whole country. He took great booty and, among his captives, Skjalf, Frosti's daughter, and a kinsman named Logi.

Agne returned to Sweden and married Skjalf. She asked him to honour her dead father with a great feast, and he agreed, inviting many guests. At the feast there was a drinking contest, and the king became very drunk. Skjalf advised him to secure the torc that had belonged to his ancestor Visbur around his neck, and he did so before falling asleep beneath a tree by the woods.

While he slept, Skjalf tied a rope to the torc, threw it over a branch and told her men to take down the tent. When they pulled the rope, the king was hanged. Skjalf, having avenged her father, fled to Finland with her followers and left behind her twin sons, Alrek and Eirík. According to the tradition, Agne was buried at a place called Agnafit, between Tauren, now Södertörn, and Stockholm.

Legacy

A burial mound at Lillhersby, called Agnehögen, is traditionally linked with the place of Agne's death as given by Snorri. It was excavated by Eric Graf Oxenstierna and dated to around 400, which some have seen as a possible hint of a historical core behind the story.

Ancient sources: Snorri Sturluson's Saga of the Ynglings, the Ynglingatal and the Historia Norwegiæ, which name him as predecessor of Alrekr and Eiríkr, and the Íslendingabók.

Trivia

A mound bearing his name. The burial mound at Lillhersby is called Agnehögen, or Agne's mound, and was dated to around 400.
A name with variants. Sources also call him Agni or Hogne, and the Historia Norwegiæ incorrectly names him Hogne.
