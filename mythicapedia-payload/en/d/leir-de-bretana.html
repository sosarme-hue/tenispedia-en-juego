legendary king of the Britons

Leir of Britain

Leir is a legendary king of the Britons, recorded by Geoffrey of Monmouth and later turned into a central figure of Elizabethan drama. His story of three daughters and a divided kingdom became the basis of Shakespeare's tragedy King Lear.

The myth

In Geoffrey of Monmouth's genealogy of the British dynasty, Leir was the son of Bladud, who died while trying to fly with artificial wings. Leir succeeded to the throne and, according to the tale, reigned for sixty years, the longest reign among Geoffrey's kings, in a period placed around the 8th century BC, the time of the founding of Rome. He is also credited with founding the city of Leicester, whose old name Kaerleir means "City of Leir".

As his death approached, Leir divided his kingdom among his three daughters: Goneril, Regan and Cordelia. Cordelia, his favourite, refused to flatter him and was left without land; she married King Aganippus of the Franks. Her sisters, married to the Dukes of Albany and Cornwall, seized the whole realm after receiving half of it, and cut his retinue until he was almost alone. Leir fled to France, where Cordelia welcomed him and, with her husband, invaded Britain and restored him. He ruled three more years before dying; Cordelia succeeded him and buried him in an underground shrine to Janus beneath the River Soar.

Worship and religion

According to tradition, Leir was buried in an underground shrine to the god Janus beneath the River Soar near Leicester, and an annual feast was held nearby in his honour.

Legacy

Leir's story was staged in the anonymous play King Leir, registered in 1594, which ended happily with the king's restoration. Shakespeare reworked it into the tragedy King Lear. The tale also appears in John Higgins's The Mirror for Magistrates and Edmund Spenser's The Faerie Queene.

Ancient sources: Geoffrey of Monmouth's 12th-century History of the Kings of Britain is the main source of the story.

Trivia

Name of the river. The names Leir, Lerion and Ligora derive from the old Brittonic name of the River Soar, reconstructed as Ligera or Ligora.
A record reign. Geoffrey gave him sixty years on the throne, the longest reign of all the kings in his history.
