war between the Olympian gods and the Titans in Greek mythology

Titanomachy

The Titanomachy is, in Greek mythology, the ten-year war in which the Olympian gods, led by Zeus, defeated the previous generation of gods, the Titans. It decided which line of divinities would rule the universe, and it marks the moment when power passed from Cronus to Zeus.

The myth

According to the fullest account, Hesiod's Theogony, the story begins with Uranus, who imprisoned his children, the Cyclopes and the Hecatoncheires, within Gaia's womb. Cronus, the youngest Titan, castrated his father with an adamantine sickle prepared by his mother and seized the throne. But Uranus had prophesied that his own children would overthrow him, so Cronus began swallowing the children Rhea bore him. Rhea saved Zeus by handing Cronus a stone wrapped in a blanket. Raised in Crete, Zeus had Metis give Cronus an emetic that made him vomit up his siblings. Freed as well, the Cyclopes and the Hecatoncheires joined Zeus in war against the Titans.

The battle pitted the Titans, based on Mount Othrys, against the Olympians, who took Mount Olympus as their home. On Zeus's side fought Hestia, Hera, Demeter, Hades and Poseidon, and Styx arrived with her children Zelus, Bia, Kratos and Nike. The Hecatoncheires hurled stones at the enemy, while the Cyclopes forged Zeus's thunderbolts, Poseidon's trident and Hades' helmet of darkness. After ten years, victory went to Zeus. The defeated Titans were chained in Tartarus with the Hecatoncheires as their guards, and Atlas was punished with holding up the sky. In the end the three brothers divided the world: Zeus took the sky, Poseidon the sea and Hades the underworld.

Legacy

The Titanomachy is above all a key case in comparative mythology. Scholars link it to other stories in which one generation of gods confronts the one that rules: the war of the Aesir against the Vanir and the Jotnar in Norse myth, the Babylonian epic Enuma Elish, the Hittite narrative of the Kingdom of Heaven, or the obscure generational conflicts in the Ugaritic fragments. In antiquity, alternative versions also circulated, such as the lost poem Titanomachy, ascribed to Eumelus of Corinth, which told the story differently.

Ancient sources: Hesiod's Theogony is the main source and the only full version that survives; the Iliad alludes to the three brothers dividing the world, and Hyginus, in his Fabulae, tells the story with variations.

Trivia

A sacred oath. Zeus made the waters of the Styx the source of his irrevocable oath, and Iris was in charge of collecting the water in a krater.
An altar among the stars. The altar where Zeus sacrificed before the war was placed among the stars as the constellation Ara, and the eagle that appeared in the sky was also turned into a constellation.
