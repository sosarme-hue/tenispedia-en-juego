Owl of Athena

The little owl of Athena is the bird that accompanies Athena, goddess of wisdom, the arts and strategic warfare, and protector of Athens. In Western culture it became a symbol of knowledge and, above all, of philosophy.

The myth

According to tradition, the owl has been linked to Athena almost from the beginning. Some modern mythographers, such as David Kinsley and Martin P. Nilsson, suggest that the goddess may descend from a Minoan palace deity associated with birds. Others offer a simpler explanation: little owls were abundant in Attica.

Ancient texts record the bond in several ways. Homer gives Athena the epithet glaukopis, related to glaux, the Greek word for little owl, usually rendered as 'bright-eyed' or 'owl-eyed'. Aristophanes noted how common these birds were in Athens, and Ovid, in his Metamorphoses, tells how the crow Corone complained that the owl Nyctimene had taken her place as the goddess's sacred bird.

Worship and religion

In Athens the owl marked belonging to the goddess. It appeared on vases, weights and the prize amphorae of the Panathenaic Games, and from around 510 BC it occupied the obverse of Athenian tetradrachms, coins known throughout the ancient world as glaux. Other Greek cities also used it in battle: according to a well-known account, owls flying over the ranks of Agathocles of Syracuse against the Carthaginians in 310 BC were read as Athena's blessing.

Legacy

The Romans linked the bird to Minerva, though sometimes they treated it simply as a sacred or favourite bird. In philosophy, Aristotle compared the human intellect to the eyes of bats, blinded by daylight, and Hegel wrote that 'the owl of Minerva spreads its wings only with the falling of the dusk': philosophy grasps an age only once it is passing away. The common European little owl bears the scientific name Athene noctua, while the names 'lechuza de Atenea' and 'búho de Atenea' are applied to it loosely.

Ancient sources: Homer (the epithet glaukopis), Aristophanes (The Birds and Lysistrata), Ovid (Metamorphoses) and Plutarch (Life of Themistocles).

Trivia

Coins called glaux. Athenian tetradrachms were known across the ancient world as glaux, meaning 'little owl', a name modern numismatics renders as 'owl'.
Ascalaphus turned into an owl. Demeter punished Ascalaphus by turning him into a screech owl after he accused Persephone of eating pomegranate seeds, which kept her in the underworld.
