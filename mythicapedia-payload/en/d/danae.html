Argive princess, mother of Perseus in Greek mythology

Danaë

Danaë was an Argive princess, daughter of King Acrisius, known as the mother of the hero Perseus, who was conceived with Zeus. Her story, shaped by a prophecy and by the confinement her father imposed to prevent it, is one of the most widely painted tales in Greek myth. According to the myth, King Acrisius, lacking male heirs, consulted an oracle that foretold his death at the hands of a grandson born to his daughter. To prevent this, he locked Danaë away in a bronze chamber or a tower, guarded day and night. Zeus nonetheless reached her in the form of a golden shower that seeped through the roof, and from that union Perseus was born. Incredulous, Acrisius set mother and child adrift in a chest. The waves carried them to the island of Seriphos, where the fisherman Dictys took them in. Years later Perseus returned to free his mother from the king Polydectes, and the prophecy was fulfilled despite his father's efforts. Ancient writers offer several versions of Danaë's parentage, her mother and the origins of the legend, and these variants are part of what makes the story compelling.

The myth

Danaë, whose Greek name is linked to the idea of 'thirsting' or 'scorched', was the daughter of Acrisius, king of Argos, and Queen Eurydice, though in two late sources her mother is called Aganippe. Having no sons, Acrisius consulted an oracle and received a grim answer: a child born to his daughter would kill him. To avert this, he confined Danaë in an underground bronze chamber, or in a tower with stone walls, watched by guard dogs that never slept.

Nothing could stop Zeus, who had become infatuated with her. He took the form of a shower of gold, slipped through the roof and made her pregnant. Pindar and Ovid use phrases that suggest this golden image, although the phrase 'golden shower' is a modern one. From this union Perseus was born.

Other accounts blame Danaë's uncle Proetus, king of Tiryns, with whom Acrisius had a long-standing hatred, which led to a quarrel between the two brothers. Ferecydes adds that her nurse was locked up with her and that Zeus, after his golden form, lay with her in his true shape. When Acrisius discovered the boy, he summoned both women before the altar of Zeus Herkeios, and, refusing to believe in the divine paternity, cast them into the sea in a chest.

The chest reached Seriphos, where Dictys rescued them and raised Perseus. According to some accounts, King Polydectes, Dictys's brother, desired Danaë and wanted to force her into marriage, so he asked Perseus for the head of Medusa. After completing the feat and rescuing Andromeda, Perseus returned to Seriphos, freed his mother and turned Polydectes to stone with the Gorgon's head. Later, at Larissa, during athletic games, Perseus accidentally threw a discus or javelin that killed his grandfather, fulfilling the prophecy.

Hecataeus claimed Danaë had a Phoenician name, an idea with little following, and a late Latin tradition places her in Italy as the founder of Ardea and mother of Daunus.

Legacy

Danaë has been one of the most frequently painted subjects in Western art. Titian produced several versions between 1540 and 1570, now held in the Prado, the Hermitage and the Kunsthistorisches Museum, among other collections. Gustav Klimt painted her in 1907, and Correggio, Rembrandt and Artemisia Gentileschi also depicted her. The golden shower scene has remained a recurring motif for exploring desire and power.

Ancient sources: Pindar and Ovid allude to the golden shower; Pherecydes adds details such as the presence of the nurse; Hecataeus of Miletus offers the version of her Phoenician origin.

Trivia

A prophecy at the origin. The oracle Acrisius feared foretold that his grandson would kill him, and that prediction guided all the king's actions.
A name with Phoenician echoes. Hecataeus of Miletus linked her name to the Phoenician 'Dana', an idea no other ancient author shared.
