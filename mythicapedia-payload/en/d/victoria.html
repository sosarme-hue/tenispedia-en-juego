Roman goddess of victory and triumph

Victoria

Victoria is the Roman goddess of victory and triumph, a personification the Romans linked with the Greek Nike. She mattered because she expressed the idea that Rome's military successes confirmed its right to rule.

The myth

Ancient texts tell almost no stories of Victoria's own, unlike Nike, so her figure was largely built by association. According to one tradition, she was the daughter of the giant Pallas and Styx, and sister of Scylla, Strength (Vis), Envy (Invidia), Power (Potestas), the Springs and the Lakes. Another version gives Styx and her own father, Acheron, as her parents.

She is shown as a winged woman offering or placing a laurel wreath or palm, symbols of triumph and of the peace that follows victory rather than of war itself. She sometimes appears at human scale driving a triumphal chariot, and in free-standing statues she stands on the palm of a much larger figure such as Jupiter, Mars or Roma. She arrived during the First Punic War, when the Romans came to know Nike through their Greek allies. She was sometimes identified with Vica Pota or with the Sabine goddess Vacuna, although Vica Pota is considered far older than any Greek influence.

Worship and religion

Her cult was public and state-sponsored. In 294 BC she received a temple on the Palatine Hill, which also stored war booty and housed the statue of the Magna Mater while her own temple was being built. Rome had other shrines to her, and the Capitol held statues that triumphant generals dedicated to her. In 29 BC Augustus had an altar set up in the Senate house to commemorate the battle of Actium, and a sacrifice was offered there before every meeting. In 382 the Christian emperor Gratian had the altar removed, despite the anxiety of many officials. No living cult of her survives today.

Legacy

Her most lasting presence is in winged Victories, pairs of figures that appear in triumphal arches and in the gaps of official Roman architecture. With the rise of Christianity, these figures gradually evolved into Christian angels. In modern times, a Victoria crowns the Berlin Victory Column, and her image also appears on ancient coins, gems and reliefs.

Ancient sources: Ancient Roman sources give her little mythology of her own; her genealogy and many traits are borrowed from Nike, the Greek goddess of victory.

Trivia
