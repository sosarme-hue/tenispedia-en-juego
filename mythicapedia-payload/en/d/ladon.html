Ladon

Ladon is a divine serpent or dragon in Greek mythology who guarded the Garden of the Hesperides and its golden apples. He is best known from the story of Heracles' eleventh labour, in which the hero killed him to seize the fruit.

The myth

The ancient sources disagree about his parentage. In Hesiod's Theogony, Ladon was the last of the offspring of Phorcys and Ceto, a pair of sea deities. Apollodorus, however, calls him the son of the monstrous Typhon and Echidna, a lineage repeated by later writers. A scholion also makes him a son of Typhon, and another source calls him the brother of the Nemean lion.

The garden where he lived lay at the western edge of the world, in Libya according to some accounts and, in Apollodorus, on Atlas among the Hyperboreans. There the golden apples had been a gift from Gaia to Zeus at his marriage to Hera. Ladon coiled around the apple tree and, according to Apollodorus, had a hundred heads and spoke with many different voices.

During his eleventh labour, Heracles killed him with arrows dipped in the poison of the Lernaean Hydra, while the Hesperides wailed at his death. In an alternative version, Ladon is never slain: Heracles persuades the Titan Atlas to fetch the apples in his place while he holds up the sky. Hera, who did not trust the Hesperides, had set Ladon to guard the tree, and after his death she raised him to the heavens, where he forms the constellation Draco.

Diodorus Siculus offers a rationalising explanation: the "golden apples" were really a flock of golden-coloured sheep, and Ladon a strong shepherd who defended them from thieves.

Legacy

Ladon gave his name to the constellation Draco, which ancient writers attribute to Zeus or Hera depending on the version. The image of the serpent coiled around the tree of golden apples was common on Greek vase painting, and Pausanias described at Olympia an ancient cedarwood image of Heracles beside the apple tree of the Hesperides with the serpent coiled around it.

Ancient sources: Hesiod (Theogony), Apollodorus (Bibliotheca), Diodorus Siculus, Hyginus and a scholion on Apollonius of Rhodes.

Trivia

Two serpents. Philostratus is the only author who describes two serpents guarding the apples instead of one.
A late name. Most ancient sources describe this serpent without giving it a proper name; only a few call it Ladon.
