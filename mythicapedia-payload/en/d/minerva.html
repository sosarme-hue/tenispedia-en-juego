Roman goddess of wisdom, war and the arts

Minerva

Minerva is the Roman goddess of wisdom, the arts, trade and strategic warfare, and a member of the Capitoline Triad alongside Jupiter and Juno. The Romans soon identified her with the Greek Athena, although she keeps distinct traits and may have Etruscan roots.

The myth

According to Roman tradition, Minerva is the daughter of Jupiter and was born from his head without a mother, an image read as the understanding that springs from the mind of the wise. Some ancient writers spoke of several goddesses with this name: Cicero, in De natura deorum, distinguishes up to five Minervas with different parentage, and Varro linked her to the Sabines.

Her deeds include the building of the ship Argo, the invention of the bone flute, the wooden horse of Epeius and the planting of the first olive tree in Attica, which won her the contest against Neptune for that land. In Ovidius she punishes those who offend her: she turned the hair of Medusa into snakes after Medusa was violated in her temple, changed the weaver Arachne into a spider after defeating her in a weaving contest, and turned Nyctimene into an owl. According to an isolated tradition, Mars fell in love with her, but was deceived by Anna Perenna, who posed as the goddess.

Worship and religion

Minerva was worshipped throughout Italy, though only in Rome did she take on a distinctly martial character. Her festival, the Quinquatria, was held from 19 to 23 March and was tied to craftsmen and intellectual life. At Lavinium, terracotta statuettes from the 4th century BC suggest her role as guardian of young women moving into marriage and motherhood, and in Rome a temple of Minerva Medica was linked to healing.

Legacy

Her image lives on in the owl that accompanies her, a traditional symbol of wisdom, and in the phrase 'Minerva's owl'. Velázquez painted the story of Arachne in Las Hilanderas, now in the Prado Museum in Madrid.

Ancient sources: Ovid's Metamorphoses recounts the episodes of Medusa, Arachne and Nyctimene, and Cicero's De natura deorum discusses her different genealogies.

Trivia

A goddess with several mothers. Cicero distinguishes up to five Minervas, each with different parents, and only one of them is born of Jupiter.
The discarded flute. According to Hyginus, Minerva threw away her flute after seeing her distorted face while playing, and cursed anyone who picked it up.
