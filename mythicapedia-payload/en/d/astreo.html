Greek god of the winds and the stars

Astraeus

Astraeus is a minor god of Greek mythology, father of the winds and the stars. Husband of Eos, the dawn, he matters mainly because his genealogy links the changing phenomena of the sky to the family of the gods.

The myth

According to Hesiod's Theogony and Apollodorus's Library, Astraeus is a son of the Titan Crius and Eurybia, and a brother of Perses and Pallas. His name is connected to the Greek word for star, astér. Hyginus, who blends several pre-Olympian figures, makes him a direct descendant of Tartarus and Gaia and counts him among the Giants, and Servius likewise has him take up arms against the gods. A late source places him among the Titans in the Titanomachy.

Astraeus married Eos, goddess of the dawn. In Hesiod, their children are Zephyrus, Boreas and Notus, followed by Eosphorus and the bright stars that crown the sky. Later authors add Eurus as his son, and Latin writers gave names to the wandering stars: Pyroeis, Stilbon, Phaenon and Phaethon, besides Eosphorus. Some sources mention a daughter, Astraea, linked to the constellation Virgo; another genealogy makes her a daughter of Zeus and Themis instead.

Later versions connect Astraeus, as father of the winds, with Aeolus, the king who commanded them at will. Boccaccio tells that Astraeus armed his sons, the Winds, and hurled them against the gods who rebelled against Jupiter. Read symbolically, Astraeus is a powerful and proud man, called father of the Winds because he ruled windy regions, and the image of sending out his sons stands for winds rising from the earth into the air.

In the Dionysiaca, a fifth-century epic by Nonnus of Panopolis, Astraeus appears as an oracular god whom Demeter consults about the future of her daughter Persephone. He warns her that the girl will be ravished by a serpent and bear a child from that union, a revelation that deeply grieves the goddess. It is the only work in which he plays a significant role outside the genealogies.

Legacy

His name is tied to the words of the star family, since it comes from the Greek for star, inherited from the Proto-Indo-European root *h₂ster-. The sources associate him with the constellation Virgo through Astraea, and Boccaccio revived him as an allegorical figure of power and pride. Beyond these learned echoes, his presence in later culture is modest.

Ancient sources: Hesiod's Theogony, Apollodorus's Library, Nonnus's Dionysiaca and, for the Giant tradition, Hyginus.

Trivia
