Phoenician god, protector of Tyre

Melqart

Melqart was the tutelary god of Tyre, the great Phoenician city-state, and one of the major deities of the Phoenician and Punic pantheons. He was linked to Heracles and to an annual cycle of death and rebirth tied to nature.

The myth

According to tradition, Melqart began as a farming god connected with spring, vegetation and fertility, and his yearly ritual included a death and a resurrection that followed the seasons. His name means "king of the city", and over time he became the patron of seafaring and colonization: wherever Tyrians founded a colony, they built a temple in his honour.

Greek writers came to identify him with Heracles, and many stories about the hero's exploits in the western Mediterranean grew around that link. He was also credited with bringing civilization to distant shores and with establishing law among people. A local legend tied his temple at Gadir (Cádiz) to the Pillars of Melqart, later known as the Pillars of Heracles.

Worship and religion

His cult is first documented in Tyre, where a sacred fire burned and his high priest ranked second only to the king. A spring festival marked his awakening, and Carthage sent a yearly tribute to his sanctuary. Herodotus described and admired his temple on an islet off Tyre. His worship declined after the Roman Emperor Theodosius banned pagan cults.

Legacy

His figure merged with Hercules, and the legend of the Pillars of Hercules at the Strait of Gibraltar grew largely out of his sanctuaries. His coins often show him riding a hippocampus, and the cippi of Melqart found on Malta, inscribed in Phoenician and Greek, helped scholars decipher the Phoenician language.

Ancient sources: Herodotus, Strabo, Livy and Josephus mention him, and a 9th-century BCE inscription from near Aleppo addresses him as lord in a dedication.

Trivia

The temple's pillars. Strabo reports that many visitors to Gadir believed the two bronze pillars in the temple were the true Pillars of Heracles. He thought the claim was fraudulent, since their inscriptions mentioned only the cost of making them.
Hannibal's vision. Livy tells how, before marching to Italy, Hannibal made a pilgrimage to Gadir and prayed at the altar of Melqart. In the story, he then had a night vision that he believed the god had sent.
