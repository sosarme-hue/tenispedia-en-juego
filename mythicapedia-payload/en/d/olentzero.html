Basque Christmas charcoal burner

Olentzero

Olentzero is a figure of Basque Christmas who, as a rural charcoal burner coming down from the mountains, brings presents to children. His character seems to combine remnants of older midwinter celebrations with Christian elements and, later, customs borrowed from Santa Claus and the Magi.

The myth

In one widely told version, Olentzero was one of the jentillak, giants who lived in the Pyrenees. One day they saw a glowing cloud in the sky that they could not understand, and only a nearly blind old man could look at it. When he examined it, he confirmed that it announced the imminent birth of Jesus. According to some accounts, the old man asked the giants to throw him off a cliff so he would not have to live through Christianisation; when they did, they fell and died, except Olentzero.

Other versions say he was abandoned as a newborn in the woods, raised by an elderly childless couple, and endowed with strength and kindness by a fairy. As an adult he became a charcoal burner who carved wooden toys for the village children. He died saving children from a burning house, and the fairy granted him eternal life so he could keep bringing joy.

Older accounts portrayed him as a fearsome figure with red eyes and a sickle, who came down the chimney to punish disobedient children.

Worship and religion

Traditionally his arrival was celebrated on Christmas Eve, though in some places, such as Ochagavía and Ermua, his visit fell on other dates. Today he is carried through the streets on a chair while people sing and collect food and sweets. In some towns, such as Lesaka, his effigy is burned at the end of the festivities.

Legacy

His figure was revived from the mid-twentieth century, especially in Gipuzkoa. The singer-songwriter Gontzal Mendibil dedicated an album to him, Olentzaro (1993), and the punk band Zarama covered his traditional song. Since 1994 he has been joined by Mari Domingi, who adds a female presence to the gift-giving.

Ancient sources: The earliest written account comes from Lope de Isasti in the seventeenth century, who recorded the name onenzaro as "the season of the good ones".

Trivia

Eyes and noses. In Álava, Navarre, La Rioja and La Bureba, a man with as many eyes as days in the year visits on New Year's Eve; in Larráun he was known as "the man of the 366 eyes".
Many names. Depending on the place he is called Olentzero, Olentzaro, Onentzaro or Orentzaro, and in parts of Navarre the name xubilaro, linked to the Yule log, is used.
