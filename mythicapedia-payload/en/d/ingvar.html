semi-legendary Swedish chieftain of the House of Yngling

Ingvar

Ingvar (Old Norse Yngvarr) was a semi-legendary Swedish chieftain of the House of Yngling, whose death on an expedition to Estonia is recounted in medieval Norse sources. He matters because the sagas present him as the ruler who won back the Swedish throne after a revolt against the Norwegian usurper Sölve Högnesson.

The myth

In Snorri Sturluson's Ynglinga saga, Ingvar is the son of King Östen, a great warrior who spent much of his time patrolling his kingdom's coasts against Danish and Estonian raiders. After a peace treaty with the Danes, he turned to the Oeselian vikings of Saaremaa and launched raids on Estonia in retaliation. One summer he reached a place called Stein, where the Estonians gathered a large army inland and gave battle. They proved too strong, Ingvar fell, and the surviving Swedes withdrew. He was buried in a mound called the Rock on the Estonian shore.

Other sources differ on the details. The Ynglingatal of Þjóðólfr úr Hvini mentions only the district of Sysla, which paid tribute, and its stanza tells how the news of the killing spread quickly. The Latin Historia Norwegiae places his death during a campaign on the island of Eysysla, that is Ösel. Thorsteins saga Víkingssonar skips Ingvar's generation altogether, making Östen the father of Anund and grandfather of Ingjald, and adds a second son, Olaf, king of Fjordane in Norway. Some accounts also name a second son, Sigvard.

Ancient sources: Snorri Sturluson's Ynglinga saga, the Ynglingatal of Þjóðólfr úr Hvini, the Latin Historia Norwegiae and Thorsteins saga Víkingssonar, all medieval Norse sources.

Trivia

The Salme ships. Between 2008 and 2010 two ship burials were found at Salme, Estonia, containing remains of at least 42 people, mostly men aged 30 to 40 who had been killed in battle. The ships date to about 650–700, and isotope analysis suggests the men came from central Sweden.
A voyage perhaps peaceful. Among the remains are dogs and falconry hawks, which suggests the journey may have been for leisure or diplomacy rather than only for war.
