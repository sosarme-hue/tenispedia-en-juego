king of Seriphos in Greek myth

Polydectes

Polydectes is, in Greek myth, the Aeolian king of the island of Seriphos in the Cyclades. His role as the antagonist of the hero Perseus makes him a key figure in the story of Danaë and her son, who washed ashore on the island in a chest.

The myth

According to the most widespread genealogy, Polydectes was a son of Magnes, a grandson of Aeolus, and the brother of the fisherman Dictys, who took in Danaë and Perseus when they reached the island. Other authors make him a son of Peristhenes and Androthoe, or of Poseidon and Cerebia, minority versions about which nothing more is known.

In time Polydectes fell in love with Danaë, but her grown son Perseus kept him from approaching her. To get rid of the young man, the king pretended he was arranging a wedding to Hippodamia and asked everyone for gifts. Perseus, who had no horses to offer, boasted that he would fetch even the head of Medusa. Polydectes demanded exactly that, threatening to marry Danaë by force if he failed. Perseus returned with the trophy and, finding his mother sheltering in a temple, strode into the palace and turned the king and his followers to stone.

Hyginus gives another ending: Polydectes became kindly, married Danaë, and raised Perseus in a temple of Athena, shielding them from Acrisius, the hero's grandfather, who came to claim them. After the king's death, a discus thrown by Perseus at the funeral games accidentally killed Acrisius.

Ancient sources: The account appears in the Bibliotheca attributed to Apollodorus, in the Fables of Hyginus and in the writings of John Tzetzes, which give differing versions of his end.
