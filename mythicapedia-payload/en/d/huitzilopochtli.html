Aztec god of the sun and war

Huitzilopochtli

Huitzilopochtli was the principal deity of the Mexica, linked to the sun, war and fire. He was the patron of the Aztecs and of their capital, Tenochtitlan, and his myths explain both their origins and the sacrifices that, in their belief, sustained the sun.

The myth

According to the myth, Huitzilopochtli was born of Coatlicue, the earth goddess, who became pregnant after a ball of feathers fell on her while she swept the temples of Coatepec. Her four hundred Centzon Huitznahua siblings and her daughter Coyolxauhqui plotted to kill him to hide what they saw as her shame.

He was born fully armed, holding the fire serpent Xiuhcoatl, and defeated his siblings. Coyolxauhqui fell from the mountain and was dismembered. He hurled her head into the sky, where it became the Moon, while he became the lord of the Sun, which chases the Moon and the stars across the heavens.

Another tradition makes him a son of the primordial couple Ometecuhtli and Omecihuatl and links him to the creation of the universe. Chronicles also say he guided the Mexica from Aztlan to the islet in Lake Texcoco where they founded Tenochtitlan, marked by an eagle on a cactus.

Worship and religion

Worship of Huitzilopochtli was central to Mexica life. His main temple, the Templo Mayor in Tenochtitlan, had a red shrine dedicated to him beside a blue one for Tlaloc. His festivals were held yearly, the most important being Panquetzaliztli, which celebrated his birth, when sacrificial victims wore his costume. The Mexica believed he needed nourishment of human blood and hearts to keep the Sun moving. After the conquest, his worship was suppressed and eventually disappeared.

Legacy

The founding myth of Tenochtitlan, with the eagle on the cactus, appears on Mexico's national coat of arms and on the current flag.

Ancient sources: Mexica codices such as the Telleriano-Remensis and the Borbonicus depict him, and the Crónica Mexicáyotl and the Anales de Tlatelolco recount his episodes.

Trivia

A hummingbird in his name. His name is usually read as hummingbird and left side or south, though some scholars propose a different translation.
The Panquetzaliztli festival. His festival was held on the last day of the fifteenth month of the Nahuatl calendar, marking his rebirth on Coatepec.
