cult epithet of the goddess Hera and of Aphrodite in Greek religion

Antheia

Antheia (Ἀνθεία, meaning roughly 'flower-goddess' in Greek) was less a goddess with a personality of her own than a cult epithet in Greek religion. It was applied to Hera and Aphrodite, and in the plural, the Antheiai were linked to the Horae, the goddesses of the seasons, probably to spring and flowering.

The myth

Antheia has almost no story of her own. The texts that mention her treat the name as a title under which a deity was honoured in a particular place. The most detailed witness is Pausanias, who describes a temple of Hera Antheia at Argos, to the right of the sanctuary of Leto. In front of it stood the tomb of women who had died in a battle against the Argives and Perseus, when they came from the Aegean islands to help Dionysus in the war.

According to the lexicographer Hesychius, Antheia was a name used for Aphrodite at Knossos. Another epigraphic source mentions the plural Antheiai (Ἄνθειαι), a divine name linked to the Horae, probably connected with spring and flowering.

Worship and religion

Hera Antheia was worshipped at Argos and also at Miletus, where the epithet is associated with the goddess. Pausanias places her temple beside that of Leto, which suggests a clearly defined sacred space. The sources do not describe specific rites or festivals, and they say nothing about whether her veneration survived antiquity.

Ancient sources: Pausanias (Description of Greece), the lexicon of Hesychius, and a lekythos by Asteas showing one of the Hesperides named Antheia.

Trivia

Other Antheias. The name also appears for other figures in Greek mythology: one of the Hesperides on a lekythos by Asteas, and one of the fifty daughters of Thespius, who each spent a night with Heracles.
