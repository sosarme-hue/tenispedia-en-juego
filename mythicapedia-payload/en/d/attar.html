Semitic god of the morning star

Attar

Attar is the god of the morning star (Venus) in West Semitic mythology, also known as Athtar, Athar, Astar or Ashtar. His name appears in Ugaritic, South Arabian, Aramaean and Ethiopian sources, and his role, gender and even form varied from region to region.

The myth

In the Canaanite tradition, Attar tried to seize the throne of the storm god Baal, but failed. In the Baal Cycle from Ugarit he appears as an astral warrior, with titles such as "Attar of the Stars" or "Attar of the Heavens".

In South Arabia, according to ancient accounts, he took the place of the old supreme god El and was regarded as lord of the whole world, always listed first among the gods. As a god of war he was called "He who is valiant in battle". His sacred animal was the gazelle or antelope, and one of his symbols was the spearhead.

When he was named Sharīqān, "the Eastern one", he was invoked as an avenger against enemies, especially in curses.

Worship and religion

In the semi-arid regions of western Asia he was sometimes worshipped as a rain god, since his storms brought the water that fed the fields. In South Arabia, rulers held ritual banquets in his honour, paid for from the tithe of the people, and ritual hunts were held as fertility rites to bring rain; the likely prey was the gazelle. Among the Aramaeans he was venerated as 'Attar-Šamayin, "the Attar of the Heavens", linked to thunder and lightning, with the goddess Hūbis as his consort. The sources do not indicate whether his cult survives today.

Ancient sources: The Baal Cycle from Ugarit and South Arabian, Aramaean and Aksumite inscriptions that name him.

Trivia

A god in women's names. Although his feminine counterpart was the goddess Astarte, among the Aramaeans his name always appeared in the masculine, even in women's names such as 'Attar-ramat.
Zeus in Aksum. In the Kingdom of Aksum, in the Horn of Africa, Attar was worshipped as the god of the sun and moon and father of other gods in the pantheon, and he was associated with Zeus.
