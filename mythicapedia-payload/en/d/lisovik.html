forest spirit of Slavic mythology

Lisovik

The lisovik (leshy in Russian) is a forest spirit of Slavic mythology who acts as master of the woodland and of all the animals that live in it. He matters because he combines the role of guardian of nature with that of a mischievous and sometimes dangerous figure for anyone who wanders into the trees.

The myth

According to tradition, the lisovik lives in the forest while the weather is warm. On 17 October he sinks beneath the earth to spend the winter, but before he goes he rages: he brings storms, splits trees and drives animals out of their burrows. He is described as a very old man or as a creature with legs and the horns of a billy goat, rather like the Roman faun or the Greek satyr. He can take the shape of an animal, and Poles believe he perches as an owl in certain trees, which is why peasants avoid felling them.

His favourite prank is to bewilder travellers. If he circles a person, that person loses their way, and the spell is broken only by wearing one's clothes inside out until reaching home. He is also lord of the hunt, since any movement of the animals is read as his command. Russian tales add that he may have a family, including a wife with thick hair whose breasts are so large she must throw them over her shoulders to walk.

Worship and religion

Hunters tried to win his favour with flattery before entering the forest, and peasants respected the trees where he was believed to dwell. The sources do not describe temples or festivals dedicated to him.

Trivia

Clothes back to front. When he wears clothing, it is put on inside out and without a belt, and some versions say his shoes are worn on the wrong feet.
His favourite saying. He repeats «I went, I found, I lost», a phrase that sums up his delight in confusing people.
A possible kinship. Some scholars link him to the Slavic god Porewit, given his role as lord of the forest and of hunting.
