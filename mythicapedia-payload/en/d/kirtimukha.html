ornamental demon face of Hindu temple art

Kirtimukha

Kirtimukha is a fierce face with horns, gaping jaws and huge fangs, used as an ornamental motif on Hindu temples in South and Southeast Asia. It comes from a legend in the Skanda Purana and the Shiva Purana, and its name means "glorious face".

The myth

According to the legend, the asura king Jalandhara, swollen with pride from his austerities, sent his messenger Rahu, whose task is to eclipse the moon, to challenge Shiva and demand that he give up his bride Parvati. Shiva answered by unleashing a burst of power from his third eye, which created a horrifying, emaciated and ravenous lion. Terrified, Rahu begged for mercy, and Shiva granted it, but he also found a way to feed the creature: it would devour human selfishness, greed and attachment.

Pleased with the outcome, Shiva named it the "glorious face" and decreed that it should always stand at the entrance of his temples. For this reason it is regarded as a symbol of Shiva himself. It often sits above the doorway of the inner sanctum as a guardian of the threshold, and in South Indian architecture it frequently crowns the top of a temple or an image of a deity.

Legacy

Some authors have compared Kirtimukha with the Greek myth of the Ouroboros, the serpent that devours its own tail. Stephen Wilk has linked its euphemistic name to the Eumenides, the gentle name given to the dreaded Erinyes.
