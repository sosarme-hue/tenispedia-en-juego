lunar god of western Anatolia

Men

Men, also written Mēn, was a lunar god worshipped in the western interior of Anatolia, especially in Phrygia and Lydia, and was very prominent under Roman rule. He is recognised by the crescent moon rising behind his shoulders and was seen as the lord of the months.

The myth

Little is known about his origins. Some scholars link him to Persian lunar deities such as Mao, or to the goddess Anahita, while others point to Mah, the Zoroastrian lunar divinity, whose feminine gender contrasts with Men's masculine one. His earliest mentions date from the 4th and 3rd centuries BC, but most of the evidence comes from the Roman period.

His iconography partly resembles that of Mithras, with a Phrygian cap and sometimes a bull. He is usually shown standing or on horseback, and occasionally riding carnelians such as rams, lions or panthers. Strabo describes him as a local god of the Phrygians.

Worship and religion

He had powerful sanctuaries. At Ameria, near Cabira, a temple dedicated to Mēn Pharnakou and the moon goddess Selene was probably founded by Pharnakes I in the 2nd century BC. At Antioch in Pisidia, the cult of Mēn Askaenos was run by a hereditary High Priest until the Roman refoundation in 25 BC.

Legacy

His symbols appear on regional coins, and under Antoninus Pius he was named on Roman coins with the formula LUS, Lunae Votum Solvit.

Ancient sources: Strabo mentions him as a local god of the Phrygians and describes the temple at Ameria.
