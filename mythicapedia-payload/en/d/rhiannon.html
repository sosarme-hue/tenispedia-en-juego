Welsh heroine of the Mabinogi, linked to horses

Rhiannon

Rhiannon is a female figure in Welsh mythology and a protagonist of the Mabinogi, the medieval collection of tales in four branches. Her figure is linked to the Gaulish horse goddess Epona and to older Celtic traditions, which makes her one of the most powerful women in Celtic literature.

The myth

In the First Branch, Rhiannon appears before Pwyll, prince of Dyfed, at the mound of Gorsedd Arberth, riding a pale horse that never goes faster than a walk, yet no rider can catch her. When Pwyll finally asks her to stop, she scolds him for not having done so sooner and explains that she wants to marry him instead of Gwawl ap Clud.

At the wedding feast, Pwyll rashly promises to grant a request from a stranger, who turns out to be Gwawl, and he asks for Rhiannon. She devises a plan: a second wedding feast where Gwawl ends up trapped in an enchanted bag that can never be filled. After two happy years, their son Pryderi is born, but the newborn vanishes the very night his nursemaids are guarding him. The frightened women smear the sleeping Rhiannon's face with the blood of a puppy and accuse her of infanticide. As penance she must tell her story to travellers and offer to carry them on her back. The boy is found by Teyrnon, a horse breeder, who raises him as his own under the name Gwri. Later Pwyll recognises him, and the child is returned to his mother, who gives him the name Pryderi.

In the Third Branch, now widowed, Rhiannon marries Manawydan and lives through further adventures shaped by enchantments.

Legacy

Her name has been linked to the reconstructed Brittonic form Rīgantonā, derived from rīgan-, meaning queen. The historian Ronald Hutton described her as one of the great female personalities in world literature. It has been suggested that the Mari Llwyd tradition, with its horse skull, may preserve something of her figure, and the Adar Rhiannon, or Birds of Rhiannon, appear in the Mabinogi and in Culhwch ac Olwen.

Ancient sources: The Mabinogi, a collection of medieval Welsh tales, and fourteenth-century Welsh poetry, where she also appears.

Trivia

The birds of Rhiannon. The Adar Rhiannon express her enchanted power: they are far yet near, and both living and dead.
A mare no one can catch. Pwyll sends his best riders after her for two days, but none can catch her, although her horse only ever walks.
