personification of death in world mythology

Death (personification)

Death is the figure who embodies the end of life in many religions and mythologies. Often shown as a cloaked skeleton with a scythe, it takes very different forms across cultures, appearing as a woman in Romance and Slavic languages, as a man in English, or simply as a guide for the soul.

The myth

In many traditions Death is an entity with its own will, but in others its role is more modest. Some beliefs describe it only as a psychopomp, a gentle figure who cuts the last ties between soul and body and guides the deceased to the afterlife, without deciding when or how the victim dies. Other stories show it as the cause of death, one that can be tricked or bribed, as with Sisyphus, who managed to escape Thanatos.

In Aztec mythology, Mictecacihuatl is the Queen of Mictlan, the underworld, ruling alongside her husband Mictlantecuhtli, and she watches over the bones of the dead. In Maya mythology, the goddess Ixtab helped those who had taken their own lives. In ancient Japan, according to the Kojiki, the goddess Izanami died giving birth to the fire god, settled in a realm of perpetual night and vowed to take a thousand lives every day. In India, the lord of death is Yama, who rides a black buffalo and carries a rope to lead souls.

Monotheistic traditions see it differently. The New Testament calls death the last enemy to be destroyed, and in Islam Azrail is the archangel who represents it, while the passing of the soul back to its Creator is regarded with serenity.

Worship and religion

The ancient Aztec festivals of the dead, blended with Spanish traditions, gave rise to the modern Mexican Day of the Dead. Devotion remains alive today in Our Lady of Holy Death, a folk saint whose following is growing in Mexico and the United States, and in San La Muerte, venerated in Paraguay, northeast Argentina and southern Brazil. Believers in both maintain their own rituals within their faith.

Legacy

The image of death has entered literature, film and popular art. In Latin America, La Parca, named after one of the three Roman Parcae, is a common term for this personification, though without a scythe. The Calavera Catrina is now an icon of the Mexican Day of the Dead, and the Grim Reaper has become a familiar figure in modern stories. Death also appears as one of the Four Horsemen of the Apocalypse.

Ancient sources: The Kojiki tells the story of Izanami, the Mahabharata mentions Yama, the Quran names Azrail, and the Bible, in the First Epistle to the Corinthians, calls death the final enemy.

Trivia

Yama in a judge's cap. In China, Yama is shown wearing a judge's cap and traditional robes. His image appears on the hell money burned in offerings to ancestors.
The list of the dead. In Korean mythology, Jeoseung Saja carries a red cloth listing the names of the dead. When he calls a name three times, the soul leaves the body and follows him.
