Greek sea god, son of Poseidon

Triton

Triton is a Greek sea god, son of Poseidon and Amphitrite, and one of the most recognizable water beings of antiquity. His human upper body and fish-tailed lower body gave rise to the generic word 'tritons' for merman-like creatures.

The myth

According to Hesiod's Theogony, Triton was born to Poseidon and Amphitrite and lived with his parents in golden palaces in the depths of the sea. Hesiod describes him as a mighty, enormous and fearsome god. Apollodorus offers a variant in which Triton's sister Rhode married Helios. Another tradition made him a son of Oceanus. Homer places Poseidon's golden palace at Aegae, on Euboea.

His best-known attribute is the conch shell, which he blew like a trumpet to calm or raise the waves. Its sound was so harsh that it sent the giants fleeing. In later literature he appears as the trumpeter of Oceanus and Poseidon, and in some texts he carries a trident. Ovid describes him as sea-blue, with shoulders covered in shells.

Triton also has a Libyan namesake, the god of Lake Tritonis, who aided the Argonauts. Apollonius of Rhodes, in his Argonautica, presents him with a fish tail and first shows him disguised as Eurypylus. He gave the heroes a clod of earth as a pledge that the Greeks would one day receive the land of Cyrene, and guided them through the marshy outlet of the lake back to the Mediterranean. According to Apollonius he married Libya, while other sources make him a son of Poseidon and Europa.

In Virgil's Aeneid, Triton drowns Misenus, son of Aeolus, after Misenus challenged the gods to play as well as he did. In Greek black-figure pottery, Heracles wrestling a sea monster is a common scene, labeled sometimes as Triton and sometimes as Nereus, the Old Man of the Sea. Apollodorus credits him with a daughter, Pallas, and presents him as the foster father of Athena, who is called Tritogeneia, 'born of Triton', although the meaning of the epithet is unclear.

Legacy

His name became a byword for the merman. In English literature he appears as the messenger or herald of Poseidon, and in Greek and Roman art 'tritons' became a generic term for these beings. Later variants such as the ichthyocentaurs, with horse forelegs, followed. Lake Tritonis and Athena's epithet keep his name alive in geography and in classical texts.

Ancient sources: Hesiod's Theogony, Homer's Iliad, Apollonius of Rhodes' Argonautica, Virgil's Aeneid, Ovid's Metamorphoses and the Pseudo-Apollodorus Bibliotheca.

Trivia

A sound that scared off. According to the sources, the sound of his conch was so discordant that, when blown loudly, it put the giants to flight, who imagined it as the roar of a dark wild beast.
Double-tailed tritons. From the late 2nd century BC, tritons with two fish tails were depicted, as on the Altar of Domitius Ahenobarbus, one of the earliest known examples.
