Greek goddess of deceit and trickery

Apate

Apate is the personification of deceit, fraud and trickery in Greek mythology. Most sources make her a daughter of Nyx, the Night, and in Roman tradition her counterpart is Fraus, Fraud.

The myth

Hesiod says that Apate was born of Night without any union, while Latin poets make her the daughter of Erebus and Nyx. In a late tradition she dwelt on the hills near the Cretan city of Amnisos, where she favoured the Cretans, who were known as liars. She liked to stay beside the false tomb of Zeus there. Hanging from her belt were all the tricks, perjuries and stratagems of humankind.

In the only myth in which she takes part, Hera, angry at Zeus's affair with the mortal Semele, finds Apate and persuades her with flattery and lies to lend her the girdle. Hera claims she wants it to bring her husband back to the marriage bed and to return Ares to Olympus. With it, Hera leads Semele to ask Zeus to appear in his true form. Zeus, who had already sworn to grant her wish, comes as a storm of lightning and burns her to death. He then rescues the unborn child, sews him into his thigh and carries him to term, so Dionysus is known as the twice-born.

Legacy

The name Apate gave rise to the dinosaur genus Apatosaurus, meaning 'deceptive lizard'. Neoplatonic philosophers such as Proclus and Plotinus used the pairing of deceit and illusion to explain desire and sensory beauty.

Ancient sources: Hesiod makes her born of Night; Cicero names Fraus and Dolus as children of Nyx and Erebus; Nonnus of Panopolis recounts her episode in the Dionysiaca.

Trivia

A dinosaur genus. The dinosaur Apatosaurus, meaning 'deceptive lizard', takes its root from this goddess's name.
Surpassing Hermes. According to Nonnus of Panopolis, Apate surpassed even Hermes, god of thieves and tricksters, in deception and persuasion.
