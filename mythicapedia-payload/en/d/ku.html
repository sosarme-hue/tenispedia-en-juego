Hawaiian god of war, politics, farming and fishing

Kū

Kū is one of the four great gods of Hawaiian religion, alongside Kanaloa, Kāne and Lono. He was regarded as the protector of war, politics, crops and fishing, and his figure is linked to the founding of the Hawaiian kingdom.

The myth

According to Hawaiian tradition, Kū is one of the primordial gods who have existed forever and who, together with Kāne and Lono, caused light to shine upon the world. He is the husband of the goddess Hina. Some have suggested a complementary dualism between them, based on the meanings of kū, "to stand", and hina, "to fall", but this idea has no support from other Polynesian languages.

Kū was invoked under many names reflecting his manifestations: guardian of forests and rain, of dry and wet farming, of the sea, or of war. He could take animal forms, such as shark, hawk or dog, and plant forms, such as the ʻieʻie vine or the ʻōhiʻa lehua flower.

Worship and religion

Kū was worshipped under many names and through feathered images (kiʻi or akua hulu manu), some of which are thought to represent him. His form Kūkāʻilimoku, "snatcher of land", was linked to war rituals that included human sacrifice, which was not part of the worship of other gods.

Legacy

Kūkāʻilimoku was the guardian of Kamehameha I, who unified the Hawaiian archipelago and established the Hawaiian kingdom, and he had monuments raised to the god in Kona. Three colossal statues of Kū were reunited for the first time in almost 200 years at the Bishop Museum in Honolulu in 2010.
