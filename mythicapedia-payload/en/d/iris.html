Greek goddess of the rainbow and messenger of the gods

Iris

Iris is the personification of the rainbow in Greek mythology and the messenger of the Olympian gods, especially Hera. Daughter of Thaumas and Electra, her role is to carry orders and messages rather than to star in stories of her own.

The myth

According to Hesiod's Theogony, Iris is the daughter of Thaumas and the Oceanid Electra, and sister of the Harpies. Because of her speed, she was sent to fetch water from the Styx in a golden jug when gods swore falsely: any god who perjured themselves in this way lost nectar and ambrosia and could not attend the assemblies of the gods.

Tradition also makes her the consort of Zephyrus, the west wind, with whom she bore Pothos. Other versions, preserved by the lyric poet Alcaeus, name her as the mother of Eros, although the god of love is usually said to be a son of Aphrodite. Some ancient writers describe her as a virgin, so her parentage varies by source.

In the Iliad she performs her messenger role: she tells Menelaus of Helen's abduction, stops Hera and Athena from joining the fighting, carries the wounded Aphrodite to Olympus and brings Achilles the request to release Patroclus's body. In the Odyssey this role passes to Hermes. The Homeric Hymns also show her serving Zeus and other goddesses, for example in her failed attempt to persuade Demeter to return to Olympus.

In Virgil's Aeneid she acts on Juno's orders: she cuts a lock of hair from Queen Dido so that she may die, and, disguised as an old woman, urges the Trojan women to burn Aeneas's ships. Ovid tells how she touched Hersilia with a single finger and made her immortal so she could join Romulus. In Aristophanes' The Birds she appears as a comic envoy of the gods, insulted and threatened by Pisthetaerus.

Worship and religion

Little trace of a cult of her own survives. There are archaic signs of worship on the island of Delos, and references to her cult in the sources are scarce. Her presence in Greek religion is mainly understood as a service performed for other gods.

Legacy

Her name survives in the word 'iris', the coloured part of the eye, and in the flower of the same name. In ancient art she appears as a winged young woman carrying the caduceus and a water jug. Over time her image was increasingly absorbed by Hermes, who came to take on much of her role as messenger.

Ancient sources: Hesiod's Theogony, Homer's Iliad, Virgil's Aeneid, Ovid, Aristophanes' The Birds and the Homeric Hymns.

Trivia

Etymology. Plato linked her name to the verb eireín, 'to speak', so that Iris would embody dialectic. Her father Thaumas is connected with thoûma, 'wonder'.
Arke's wings. According to a late source, her winged sister Arke joined the Titans during the Titanomachy.
