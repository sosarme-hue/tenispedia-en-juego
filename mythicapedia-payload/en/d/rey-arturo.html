King Arthur

King Arthur is a legendary British king and a central figure of the Matter of Britain, the medieval tradition that also gave us the Knights of the Round Table. Welsh and English sources place him among the post-Roman Britons who fought the Anglo-Saxons in the late fifth and early sixth centuries. Whether he had a real historical basis remains debated, but his cultural importance in European literature is immense.

The myth

In the legend, Arthur is the son of Uther Pendragon and Igraine, and he is born at Tintagel Castle. The magician Merlin guides his upbringing and helps him obtain the sword Excalibur. The earliest Welsh texts present him as a great warrior who defended Britain against human and supernatural enemies, and some sources credit him with twelve battles, the last at Badon Hill, where he is said to have killed 960 men with his own hands. Geoffrey of Monmouth, in his twelfth-century Historia Regum Britanniae, shaped the full story: a king who defeats the Saxons, builds a vast empire across the islands and marries Guinevere. His end comes at Camlann, where he faces Mordred, who in some versions is his son, and both fall. Afterwards Arthur is taken to the isle of Avalon. Chrétien de Troyes added Lancelot and the Holy Grail, and in the French romances the focus often shifts to other Knights of the Round Table. Versions differ widely from text to text, and there is no single canonical version. The most widely read of the older versions is Thomas Malory's Le Morte d'Arthur, written around 1470.

Legacy

The legend has inspired writers for centuries. Alfred Tennyson revived it in the Victorian era with his Idylls of the King, and in the twentieth century T. H. White gathered his novels as The Once and Future King (1958), while Thomas Berger wrote Arthur Rex (1978). White's work became the basis for the musical Camelot (1960) by Alan Jay Lerner and Frederick Loewe, and a film of the same title followed in 1967. Other notable adaptations include John Boorman's Excalibur (1981) and the satire Monty Python and the Holy Grail (1975). Today the legend remains present in theatre, film, television and comics.

Ancient sources: Y Gododdin, the Historia Brittonum and the Annales Cambriae are the earliest texts that mention him, although the last two were written centuries after the period in which he is supposed to have lived.

Trivia

A striking number. The Historia Brittonum claims that Arthur killed 960 enemies with his own hands at Badon, a figure historians regard as unreliable.
A slate from Tintagel. In 1998, Professor Christopher Morris found a slate at Tintagel inscribed with the Latinised name ARTOGNOU, corresponding to the Celtic Arthnou. It does not, however, prove that Arthur existed.
