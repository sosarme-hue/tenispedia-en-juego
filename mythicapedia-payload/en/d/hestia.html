Greek goddess of the hearth and home

Hestia

Hestia is the virgin goddess of the hearth in Greek mythology and one of the twelve Olympians. She embodies the household fire, the centre of the home, the family and communal life, and her cult was part of everyday Greek religious practice.

The myth

According to Hesiod's Theogony, Hestia was the eldest daughter of the Titans Cronus and Rhea. Her father swallowed each of his children at birth, and she was the first to be devoured. When the youngest, Zeus, escaped with his mother's help, he forced Cronus to disgorge his siblings, and Hestia joined the new generation of Olympian gods. Hesiod says that both men and women fought in the Titanomachy, though some accounts claim Hestia, with other goddesses, stayed out of the war.

The Homeric Hymn V tells how Poseidon and Apollo both sought her hand, but she refused them and swore before Zeus that she would remain a virgin forever. As a reward, Zeus gave her the central place of the hearth and the first portions and fat of every sacrifice in all the temples. She thus stood with Athena and Artemis among the few goddesses beyond Aphrodite's power. The Homeric Hymn XXIX presents her as present both in the dwellings of the immortals and in those of humans.

Tradition also gives her the protection of those who seek refuge in homes. She has few independent episodes of her own; her story is mainly that of a fixed place she never left. For this reason she is rarely shown in ancient depictions of the Gigantomachy, since someone had to keep the hearth fires burning while the other gods were busy. According to Ovid's Fasti, Priapus tried to assault Vesta as she slept, but the braying of Silenus' donkey woke her; the story closely mirrors the myth of Priapus and Lotis.

Worship and religion

At every domestic banquet or sacrifice, the first and last portions of food and the libations of sweet wine were offered to Hestia. From this came the Greek saying "to begin with Hestia", meaning to start an affair well. At the Amphidromia, a newborn was carried by its father in a run around Hestia's altar to be formally admitted into the family. Each city kept a perpetual fire in its prytaneum, the seat of the magistrates, where honoured guests were entertained and public meals were held. When founding a colony, settlers carried embers from the public altar of the mother city.

Legacy

Her name came to denote the hearth itself as a divine reality and underlies the Greek word for hospitality. In Rome her equivalent was Vesta, whose public cult was of great importance. Herodotus associated her with the Scythian goddess Tabiti, and Procopius compared her to the Zoroastrian sacred fire.

Ancient sources: Hesiod makes her the firstborn of Cronus in the Theogony; the Homeric Hymns V and XXIX devote passages to her; Ovid mentions her in the Fasti.

Trivia

Beginning with Hestia. The Greek expression for starting something well comes from the ritual of offering her the first portion at every banquet.
Mother city. Greek colonies carried embers from the public fire of the mother city to light the hearth of the new foundation.
