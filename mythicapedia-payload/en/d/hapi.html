Egyptian god of the annual flooding of the Nile

Hapi

Hapi was the god who embodied the yearly flood of the Nile in ancient Egyptian religion. Through his waters the river deposited fertile silt on its banks, making farming possible in a land surrounded by desert.

The myth

According to Egyptian tradition, the annual flood of the Nile was regarded as the arrival of Hapi. Because the flood brought fertile soil to a region that would otherwise have been desert, Hapi became associated with abundance and fertility. This is why he was shown with a prominent belly and drooping breasts, signs of a generous and nourishing harvest.

In some traditions he was regarded as the father of the gods, a caring parent who helped keep the cosmos in balance. He was believed to dwell in a cavern at the supposed source of the Nile near Aswan, from which he emerged each year to bring the flood. Hapi was not the river itself but the inundation event. He was also called a friend of Geb, the earth god, and lord of Neper, the god of grain.

Worship and religion

His cult was centred at Elephantine, at the First Cataract, where his priests performed rituals meant to ensure a steady flow of the flood. There the official nilometer was monitored to predict the level of the inundation. He was venerated along the length of the Nile and appeared on the walls of most temples, with rock chapels at Gebel el-Silsila. No living cult survives, but his figure was part of Egyptian religious life for centuries.

Legacy

Hapi was often shown binding together the stems of the plants of Upper and Lower Egypt, a gesture symbolising the unity of the country. This image was carved at the base of seated statues of the pharaohs. He also appears in modern culture, featured on the five-pound Egyptian banknote.

Ancient sources: Ancient Egyptian texts and records document his role in the Nile flood, and his name appears among the four sons of Horus, guardians of the canopic jars used in funerary practice.

Trivia

Breasts and belly. Although male, Hapi was shown with drooping breasts and a large belly, an image of the fertility the flood brought.
Two plants, one country. Depending on the region, he was shown with papyrus, the emblem of Lower Egypt, or with lotus, the emblem of Upper Egypt.
