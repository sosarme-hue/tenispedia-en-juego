Aztec god of the Moon

Tecciztecatl

Tecciztecatl is the Aztec lunar deity, linked to the image of the Man in the Moon. His story explains how the Sun and the Moon came to be in the Aztec cosmos, which makes him a key figure in their creation accounts.

The myth

In Aztec tradition, the world had passed through several ages ruled by sun gods, and the fourth sun was destroyed by a flood. The gods then gathered to choose who would become the next sun, and the chosen one had to throw himself into a bonfire. Two gods offered themselves: Nanahuatzin, a poor and humble god who could be spared, and Tecciztecatl, a rich and proud god who insisted on the honor.

At the last moment Tecciztecatl hesitated, while Nanahuatzin leapt bravely into the flames. Tecciztecatl then gathered his courage and followed him into the fire, so that two suns appeared in the sky. The gods, angered by his hesitation, threw a rabbit at his face, leaving its shape marked on his surface, and dimmed his brightness until he could only be seen at night.

In some depictions he carries a large white seashell on his back, the tecciztli or tēucciztli, which represents the Moon itself; in others he has butterfly wings. He is considered a son of Tlaloc and Chalchiuhtlicue. In some myths he is associated with Tezcatlipoca, the night sky and antagonist of Huitzilopochtli, and he is described as the personification of pride.

Trivia

The rabbit mark. According to the story, the gods threw a rabbit at Tecciztecatl, and its shape was left on the Moon. This is why some depictions show a rabbit-shaped mark on its surface.
A seashell as symbol. The tecciztli, the large white seashell he sometimes carries on his back, is the symbol that represents the Moon itself in images of this god.
