ship of Jason and the Argonauts in Greek mythology

Argo

The Argo was the ship in which Jason and the Argonauts sailed from Iolcos in search of the Golden Fleece. In Greek tradition it was built with divine help and was said to be the first ship to cross the sea, and its prow carried a talking piece of oak. It also became a constellation.

The myth

According to the myth, the ship was built by the shipwright Argus, with Athena's help, and Hera watched over its crew. Athena placed in the prow a piece of oak from the sacred grove of Dodona, which could speak with a human voice and deliver oracles. Jason consulted the oracle before setting sail, and the god gave him leave to gather the nobles of Greece.

The Argo had fifty oars, all pulled by the Argonauts themselves, and its steersman, Tiphys, learned from Athena how to fix the sails to the mast. According to Herodotus, the bronze tripod they carried was given to Triton after the crew got lost in Lake Tritonis, and the Nereids and Triton helped guide the ship through dangerous moments. After returning with the Fleece, the ship was dedicated to Poseidon at the Isthmus of Corinth.

The story ends tragically: years later a beam fell from the top of the ship and killed Jason as he slept on the ground. Athena or Minerva then placed the ship among the stars as the constellation Argo Navis.

Worship and religion

Because the ship had been built with divine help, it was treated as a sacred object. After the voyage it was dedicated to Poseidon at the Isthmus of Corinth and made into a monument to the gods.

Legacy

The Argo has appeared as a motif in plays, films, books and philosophical writing. Aristotle used it as an example when discussing ostracism in Athens, noting that the ship left Heracles behind because he was too heavy. In the 1963 film Jason and the Argonauts, the figurehead is a woman's head modeled on Hera that speaks to Jason. In 2008 a replica of a Mycenaean-era penteconter, named Argo, was launched at Volos and sailed to the coast of present-day Georgia.

Ancient sources: Apollonius of Rhodes' Argonautica is the best source; it is also mentioned by Diodorus Siculus, Apollodorus, Euripides in Medea, Catullus, Propertius, Herodotus and Aristotle.
