ritual personification of the harvest-wealth branch in ancient Greece

Eiresione

Eiresione, also spelled Iresione, was the personification in Greek religion of a branch of olive or laurel decked with wool and fruit, a symbol of the wealth and abundance that children carried during festivals for Apollo.

The myth

According to an ancient account, the custom began with Theseus's return to Athens after burying his father Aegeus. Theseus then fulfilled the vow he had made to Apollo on the seventh day of Pyanepsion, the day he reached the city safely. To celebrate, his companions mixed the leftovers of their meals into one common pot and shared the table, which is why boiling pulses became part of the festival. The branch carried in procession recalled the suppliant's branch, but it was now heavy with fruit because barrenness had ended.

Worship and religion

Only children who had both parents still living could carry the branch. It was carried during the Pyanopsia and Thargelia festivals, offered to Apollo, and afterwards hung at the house door. The song the children sang during the ritual was also called eiresione.

Ancient sources: An ancient account of Theseus's return to Athens describes the procession and the song that accompanied it.

Trivia

Only for children with living parents. Only children whose two parents were still alive could carry the branch, a requirement that set this ritual role apart for young participants.
One name for the object and the song. The same name was used both for the branch and for the song the children sang during the procession.
