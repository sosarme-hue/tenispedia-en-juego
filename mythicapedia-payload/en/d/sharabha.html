part-lion, part-bird creature and deity of Hindu mythology

Sharabha

Sharabha is a hybrid creature of Hindu mythology, part lion and part bird, who appears in Shaiva tradition as a form of Shiva. He matters because he illustrates the rivalry, and also the reconciliation, between the devotees of Shiva and those of Vishnu.

The myth

In early Sanskrit literature, Sharabha is a savage beast that roars and frightens other animals in hills and forests. He has eight legs, is stronger than a lion or an elephant, and can clear a valley in a single leap. Later texts describe him as an eight-legged deer, so his appearance varies between traditions.

The best-known version comes from the Shaiva scriptures: Shiva takes the form of Sharabha to subdue Narasimha, the man-lion avatar of Vishnu, and return him to his original state, a symbol of harmony. This is the origin of his name Sarabheshwara, meaning Lord Sharabha. Vaishnavas, however, deny that Narasimha was defeated and hold that Sharabha is a name of Vishnu. Another story tells that Vishnu took the form of the two-headed bird Gandaberunda to confront Sharabha. In Buddhism he appears in a Jataka tale as an earlier birth of the Buddha.

Worship and religion

In Tamil Nadu, the Chola dynasty, which strongly favoured the Shaiva sect, commissioned four images of Sharabha, one of them in a separate shrine at the Darasuram temple. According to this tradition, his representation can bring good luck, cure illnesses and destroy enemies.

Legacy

His image was used on the emblem of the Indian state of Karnataka, on the crest of Mysore University and in the logo of the company Karnataka Soaps and Detergents Limited.

Ancient sources: The Mahabharata, the Shiva Purana, the Sharabha Upanishad and the Kalika Purana describe him, and the Ramayana mentions him as the name of a monkey king.

Trivia

A chain of destruction. At the Chennakeshava temple in Belur, Sharabha is shown devouring a lion, and in turn is destroyed by Gandaberunda in the final scene of a sequence of animals devouring one another.
Images in their own shrines. The Chola dynasty commissioned four images of Sharabha, and one of them, at Darasuram, stands in a small shrine of its own, an unusual arrangement for the period.
