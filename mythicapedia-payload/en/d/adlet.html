half-human, half-dog creature of Inuit mythology

Adlet

The adlet are monstrous, dog-legged humanoids in Inuit lore from Greenland and the coasts of Labrador and Hudson Bay. Their name also referred to inland Native American peoples, showing how stories mixed with real contacts between groups.

The myth

According to the account Franz Boas recorded on Baffin Land, the young woman Niviarsiang rejected all her suitors and ended up marrying a dog, Ijirqang. They had ten children: five dogs and five adlet, with a dog's body from the waist down and a man's from the waist up. Since the dog did not hunt and the family went hungry, her father tricked him with stones in his boots and drowned him.

Niviarsiang took revenge by sending her dog sons to gnaw at her father's feet and hands; he cut off her fingers, and when they fell into the sea they became whales and seals. The adlet were sent inland, where they founded a numerous people, while the dogs crossed the ocean to become the ancestors of the Scandinavians.

Ancient sources: The stories were recorded in the late nineteenth century by Franz Boas and Hinrich Rink, and in the early twentieth century by A. L. Kroeber, who published several tales about the adlet.

Trivia

The tornit. The tornit are another fabulous race in Inuit lore. In a tale recorded by Kroeber, two tornit escape from cannibalistic adlet by cutting the thongs that held their sledges together.
A parallel myth. The theme of a woman who marries a dog also appears among the Dakelh of British Columbia and among the Chukchi, on the other side of the Bering Strait.
