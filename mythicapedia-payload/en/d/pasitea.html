one of the Charites, wife of Hypnos

Pasithea

Pasithea is one of the Charites (Graces) of Greek mythology and, according to Homer, the woman whom Hera promised to Hypnos, the god of sleep. She appears mainly in the Iliad and later epic, where her story serves as the pretext for a plot of deceptions among the gods.

The myth

In the Iliad, Hera wants to distract Zeus so that the Achaeans can gain ground against the Trojans. To do so she approaches Hypnos, sleep, and swears by the river Styx that she will give him Pasithea in marriage, something he had long desired, according to Homer. Hypnos agrees, and Zeus falls asleep.

The sources do not agree on her parentage. Homer does not name her parents. Quintus of Smyrna suggests that Hera may have been her mother through Hera's marriage to Hypnos. In Nonnus's Dionysiaca, her father is Dionysus and her mother is Hera, although the two are enemies throughout the poem. Hesiod's Theogony includes a Pasithea among the Nereids, and in a late version she is a daughter of Zeus and a woman named Autonoe. Ovidio mentions the children of Sleep, among them Phantasos, without naming their mother.

In the Dionysiaca she has a larger role. Hera sends Iris to Hypnos to repeat the deception and again offers him Pasithea. In book 33, Pasithea sees the madness of Dionysus and returns saddened to Aphrodite, confessing that her grief comes from her father and not from Hypnos. In another episode she helps Aphrodite spin thread while the goddess tries to use Athena's loom, until the mockery of the gods forces her to give up.

Ancient sources: Homer's Iliad (book 14), Quintus of Smyrna's Posthomerica, Nonnus's Dionysiaca, Hesiod's Theogony, Ovid's Metamorphoses, and brief mentions by Catullus and Statius.

Trivia

An uncertain name. The meaning of her name is obscure, and the explanations proposed since the eighteenth century are not secure.
A sacred oath. Hera swears by the Styx that she will give Pasithea to Hypnos, an oath that not even the gods could break.
