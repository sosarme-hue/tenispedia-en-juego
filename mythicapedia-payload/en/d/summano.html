Roman god of nocturnal thunder

Summanus

Summanus was the Roman god of nocturnal thunder, as distinct from the daytime thunder hurled by Jupiter. Even Ovid was unsure of his exact nature, and he is often linked with Jupiter under the title Jupiter Summanus.

The myth

According to tradition, the Romans kept only two of the nine gods who had once controlled lightning: Jupiter Fulgurator for daytime bolts, and Summanus for those at night. Varro counts him among the Sabine gods honoured by the king Titus Tatius, and Pliny believed he was of Etruscan origin. His name was explained as Summus Manium, "the highest of the Manes", or as sub ("under") plus mane ("morning"), that is, the night.

A bolt knocked his statue from the roof of the temple of Jupiter Capitolinus, and the head was found in the Tiber. His own temple, near the Circus Maximus, was dedicated around 278 BCE during the war against Pyrrhus, and lightning struck it again in 197 BCE. Saint Augustine notes that Summanus was once more revered than Jupiter, until a more magnificent temple to Jupiter drew greater honour.

Worship and religion

His festival fell on 20 June, the day before the summer solstice. Two black oxen or wethers were sacrificed, animals usually offered to chthonic deities, and round cakes called summanalia, perhaps a solar symbol, were offered to the statue. Mount Summano, in the Alps near Vicenza, is traditionally regarded as a site of the cults of Pluto, Jupiter Summanus and the Manes, with a sanctuary in use from the early Iron Age into late antiquity.

Legacy

Georges Dumézil linked him with the violent, nocturnal side of heavenly sovereignty, paired with Varuna and Mitra in Vedic religion. Camões placed him in Pluto's gloomy realm, and Milton used him as a simile for Satan visiting Rome. An asteroid discovered in 1990, (11885) Summanus, was named in his memory.

Ancient sources: Varro, Pliny, Ovid, Paulus Diaconus, Martianus Capella and Saint Augustine mention him.

Trivia

An asteroid named after him. The asteroid (11885) Summanus, discovered in 1990, commemorates this Roman god.
A mountain often struck by lightning. Mount Summano, at 1291 metres, rises in the Alps near Vicenza and is frequently struck by lightning.
