Andean fat-stealing boogeyman

Pishtaco

The pishtaco is a folkloric boogeyman of the Andes, especially in Peru and Bolivia, who supposedly kills travellers to extract their fat. The figure reflects Indigenous fears of exploitation by outsiders.

The myth

According to the tradition, the pishtaco is a solitary bandit who ambushes lone travellers with a curved knife hidden in his clothes. He beheads them to extract their fat, which was said to lubricate machinery or make soap and ointments.

The legend has colonial roots: Indigenous Andeans believed Spanish conquistadors sought their fat to heal wounds, and over time the figure became a pale foreigner or a plantation owner.

Legacy

Ricardo Palma recorded it in his Tradiciones peruanas. Rumours about the pishtaco have even disrupted food aid programmes and the work of geologists on the Altiplano. Later rumours gave rise to the sacaojos, a figure said to steal children's eyes.

Trivia

Wayruru amulets. Red and black wayruru beans are used as amulets against the kharisiri. The traditional cure, besides killing the attacker, is to buy human fat and burn it with these beans and a white egg.
Geologists mistaken for pishtacos. Indigenous people have attacked geologists working on the Altiplano of Peru and Bolivia, mistaking them for pishtacos.
