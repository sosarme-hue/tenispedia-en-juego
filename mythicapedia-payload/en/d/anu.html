Mesopotamian god of the sky and king of the gods

Anu

Anu (in Sumerian, An, 'sky') is the god of the sky and king of the gods in Mesopotamian religion, and the ancestor of many deities. He opens the lists of gods, although in practice he played a secondary role to Enlil and, later, to Marduk and Ashur.

The myth

According to Mesopotamian tradition, Anu was the son of Anshar and Kishar, in the genealogy recorded in the Enuma Elish. With Ki, the earth, he fathered Enlil, who separated sky from earth. God lists give him several spouses, among them Ki, Urash and Antu, all associated with earth, and in a more sparsely attested tradition Nammu.

Anu appears briefly in the Epic of Gilgamesh: his daughter Ishtar asks him for the Bull of Heaven to attack the hero, and after the bull dies a leg is flung at the goddess's head. In the myth of Adapa, Anu summons the hero for breaking the wing of the south wind and offers him the food of immortality, which Adapa refuses after Enki warns him. In the Hurrian myths of Kumarbi, known mainly from Hittite versions, Anu is a former ruler of the gods overthrown by Kumarbi, who bites off his genitals and gives birth to the weather god Teshub.

Worship and religion

Anu was rarely worshipped. His main link was with the Eanna temple in Uruk, where the chief deity was Inanna. After a period of decline, under Seleucid rule he was reinterpreted as an active god and was worshipped in Uruk in the final centuries of Mesopotamian history. Rulers invoked him as the source of their legitimacy. His symbol was a horned crown on a pedestal.

Legacy

It has been proposed that the castration of Ouranos in Hesiod's Theogony was inspired by the Hurrian myth of Kumarbi, and that in the Hellenistic period Anu may have been identified with Zeus, though this is uncertain. His name simply means 'sky', and the same cuneiform sign could also be read as dingir, 'god'. In Uruk, two circumpolar stars were named the 'Great Anu and Antu of Heaven'.

Ancient sources: The Enuma Elish, the Epic of Gilgamesh, the god list An = Anum, the myth of Adapa, and the Hurrian Kumarbi texts known from Hittite translations.

In art

Trivia

A sacred number. Anu was associated with the number 60, a sacred figure for the Sumerians, and the numeral could stand for his name.
A god without an image. Anu almost never appears in Mesopotamian art; he is known mainly from texts and from symbols such as the horned crown.
