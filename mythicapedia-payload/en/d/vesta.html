Roman goddess of the hearth and sacred fire

Vesta

Vesta is the Roman goddess of the hearth, home and family, whose sacred fire burned in her temple in the Forum Romanum. The counterpart of Hestia in Greek mythology, she held a greater public role in Rome, closely tied to the survival of the Roman people.

The myth

Myths about Vesta are few, and most revolve around her fire and her priestesses. According to a tradition preserved by Dionysius of Halicarnassus and Plutarch, a phallus emerged from her hearth and impregnated a young woman. In some versions the child was Romulus and Remus, in others Servius Tullius.

Her genealogy is more straightforward: daughter of Saturn and Ops, she was the sister of Jupiter, Neptune, Pluto, Juno and Ceres. Unlike her sisters, she refused to marry. Ovid compares her to the earth, since both hold a watchful fire beneath them. Another story tells how Priapus tried to approach Vesta while she slept during a festival of Cybele, though the episode is unclear in the sources.

Worship and religion

Her temple in the Forum was entered only by her priestesses, the Vestal Virgins, who were chosen at around ten years old and kept her flame alight. The fire was never allowed to go out and was renewed every year on 1 March. Their virginity was considered essential to Rome's survival. The Vestalia, held from 7 to 15 June, was one of the most important festivals, when respectable matrons walked barefoot to the temple carrying food offerings.

The cult lasted remarkably long. As Christianity spread, it was one of the last non-Christian cults still active, until Theodosius I closed the temple in 391 AD and extinguished the flame.

Legacy

Her image became linked to purity, loyalty and domestic life. In the twentieth century, some psychoanalysts read her as a "Phallic Mother", combining virginity with the fertility ascribed to her in the stories. Her equivalence with Hestia also made her a useful point of comparison between Greek and Roman religion.

Ancient sources: Ovid, Dionysius of Halicarnassus, Plutarch and Cicero mention Vesta in their works on Roman religion and history.

Trivia

A goddess with few myths. Ancient mythographers noted that Vesta had hardly any myths of her own. Her importance came mainly from being one of the oldest gods, entitled to precedence in worship and offerings.
A hearth in the emperor's house. After becoming pontifex maximus in 12 BC, Augustus gave part of his private house to the Vestals and built a new shrine of Vesta there, linking the state's public hearth with his own residence.
