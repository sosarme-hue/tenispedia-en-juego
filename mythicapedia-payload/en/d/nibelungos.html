Nibelungs

The Nibelungs are a mythical people and a family line in Germanic and Norse legend, tied to a great hoard sunk in the Rhine and a cursed ring. The name refers both to a race of underground dwarfs and to a Burgundian royal house, a mix of traditions that shows how these stories travelled and changed.

The myth

According to the tradition, the Nibelungs were dark beings who lived underground and worked precious metals, above all gold. Their great hoard, lying at the bottom of the Rhine, had been taken from the nymphs who guarded it. Their king owned a magical ring that brought misfortune to whoever wore it.

In one version, the knight Siegfried kills the Nibelung princes Nibelung and Schilbung after quarrelling with them over how to divide the treasure. In other sources the name belongs to a royal family instead: the Gibichungs of Burgundy, led by King Gunther (Gunnar in Old Norse), who are responsible for Siegfried's death and are destroyed at the court of Attila. In Norse tradition the term is a clan name for Gunnar and Högni, and in the Thidrekssaga and Faroese legends brothers, sons and revenges multiply.

Legacy

The Nibelung story was almost forgotten for centuries until Romanticism revived it in the nineteenth century. In 1829 Wilhelm Grimm published a study of German historical legends, and Richard Wagner turned the treasure and the ring into his four-opera cycle Der Ring des Nibelungen: Das Rheingold, Die Walküre, Siegfried and Götterdämmerung.

Ancient sources: The medieval German Nibelungenlied, the Atlakviða and Atlamál poems of the Poetic Edda, the Old Norse Thidrekssaga, and the Latin poem Waltharius, composed around 920.

Trivia

A Frankish name. In Waltharius, a Latin poem from around 920, Gunther's warriors are called "Nivilones", perhaps the earliest surviving form of the name.
Dwarfs with another name. In the late medieval Lied vom Hürnen Seyfrid, the name appears as Nybling or Nibling and is given to a dwarf who in turn gives his name to the treasure.
