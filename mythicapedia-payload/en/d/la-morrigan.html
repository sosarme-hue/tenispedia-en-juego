Celtic goddess of war, death and sovereignty

The Morrígan

The Morrígan is a figure from Irish mythology linked to war, fate, death and destruction, often appearing as a crow or raven. Her role also includes renewal, love and guardianship of the land and its people, which makes her one of the most complex deities of the Celtic world.

The myth

According to tradition, the Morrígan belongs to the Tuatha Dé Danann, the magical people said to have lived in Ireland before the Irish. She is described as maiden, lover and mother, and as a shape-shifter who could pass from a young woman to an old one, or from a woman to a crow. She stirs warriors to brave deeds and strikes fear into their enemies, and she can help bring victory. She also foretells death: she is seen washing the bloodstained clothes of those fated to fall.

She is often grouped with her sisters Badb and Macha, sometimes with Nemain, although some sources present them as manifestations of a single triple goddess. The Lebor Gabála Érenn identifies the Morrígan with Anu, the "nurturer of the gods". She is also described as the envious wife of the Dagda.

Legacy

Her figure survived in Irish folklore, where she is associated with the banshee. Some modern writers have tried to link her with Morgan le Fay of the Matter of Britain, although linguists place the two names in different branches of the Celtic family. She has also been compared with the Hebrew figure Lilith.

Ancient sources: The Lebor Gabála Érenn and the Glossary of Cormac, which connects her with Anu.

Trivia

A word from nightmares. Part of her name, mor, may come from a root meaning terror, the same root behind Old English maere, the source of the word nightmare.
The Two Breasts of Anu. Two twin hills in County Kerry, in Munster, are called the Two Breasts of Anu, after the goddess with whom the Morrígan is identified.
