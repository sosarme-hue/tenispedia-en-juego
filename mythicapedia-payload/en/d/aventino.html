Aventinus

Aventinus is a hero of Latin tradition, son of Hercules and the priestess Rhea in Virgil's Aeneid. He fights as an ally of Mezentius against Aeneas, and his name is linked to Rome's Aventine Hill.

The myth

In the Aeneid, Virgil introduces him in Book VII among the allies of Mezentius, facing Aeneas in battle. He rides a war chariot in ceremonial dress and wears a lion's skin, like his father, and a Hydra appears on his shield. The poem presents him as a slayer of monsters and a winner of chariot races, but he never appears again.

The ancient commentator Servius explains that the Aventine Hill took its name from birds or from an Aboriginal king named Aventinus, who was buried there, and that the son of Hercules may have taken his name from the hill rather than the other way round. Modern scholars generally believe Virgil invented the character, perhaps from a local name.

Legacy

His name is associated with the Aventine Hill in Rome, one of the city's seven hills. Outside the Aeneid, he is barely mentioned in classical literature.

Ancient sources: Virgil, Aeneid, Book VII, and Servius's commentary on that passage.

Trivia

A Hydra on the shield. A Hydra appears on his shield, the monster Hercules defeated. Some scholars see in this a hint that Aventinus himself represents a monster destined to fall before Aeneas.
A possible editorial error. The philologist W. Warde Fowler suggested that some lines of the Aeneid refer to the Sabine hero Ufens, perhaps because of a confusion introduced by the poem's early editors.
