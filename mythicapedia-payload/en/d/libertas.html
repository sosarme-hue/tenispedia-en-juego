Roman goddess of liberty

Libertas

Libertas is the Roman goddess and personification of liberty, equivalent to the Greek Eleutheria. She gained political weight in the late Republic and became a symbol that still inspires modern emblems, such as the Statue of Liberty in New York.

The myth

Libertas has almost no mythology of her own. Unlike many Roman deities, she does not appear in stories about gods and heroes; her role is closer to a political abstraction than to a narrative character. The only author who includes her in a mythological text is Hyginus, who makes her a daughter of Jupiter (Zeus) and Juno (Hera) and a sister of Juventas, Youth.

Her image was shaped mainly by her attributes. She was shown with the rod and the pileus, the cap freed slaves received when they gained their liberty. The rod was linked to the ceremonial manumission known as manumissio vindicta, in which a slave became free through a formal gesture before a magistrate.

Worship and religion

Libertas received temples in Rome. Tradition holds that Tiberius Sempronius Gracchus founded her first sanctuary on the Aventine Hill in 238 BC, where census tables were kept. Later, Publius Clodius Pulcher built another on the Palatine in 58-57 BC, on the site of Cicero's former house. In 46 BC the Senate voted a new shrine in honour of Julius Caesar, but it was never built, and a small statue of the goddess stood in the Forum.

Legacy

Libertas has served as a model for many modern personifications. Her image has appeared on coins throughout history, and the Statue of Liberty in New York takes its most recognisable feature, the Roman robe, from her. Bartholdi replaced the pileus with a seven-rayed crown but kept the broken chains at her feet.

Ancient sources: Hyginus is the only ancient author who includes her in a mythological text.

Trivia

A freed slave's cap. The pileus was a felt cap that slaves received on becoming free, after their heads had been shaved. That is why it became an emblem of liberty.
