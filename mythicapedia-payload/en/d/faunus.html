Roman god of forests and fields

Faunus

Faunus is the Roman god of forests, plains and fields, and protector of farming and livestock. He is one of Rome's oldest deities and was later identified with the Greek god Pan.

The myth

According to tradition, Faunus was an ancient king of Latium, grandson of Saturn and son of Picus, and father of Latinus by the nymph Marica, though some accounts make Marica his mother instead.

After his death he was raised to the status of a tutelary deity of the land, thanks to his services to agriculture and cattle breeding. He was linked to a goddess of similar attributes, Fauna or Fatua, regarded as both his sister and his wife.

Many fauni, spirits of the untamed woodland, were thought to accompany him, just as the Paniskoi accompanied Pan. Educated Romans connected the fauns with the Greek satyrs, wild followers of Dionysus.

Worship and religion

Roman farmers worshipped Faunus, and two festivals, the Faunalia, were held in his honour: one on 13 February at his temple on the island in the Tiber, and one on 5 December, when peasants brought rustic offerings and danced. His priests, the Luperci, took part in the Lupercalia on 15 February, wearing goatskins.

He was also consulted as a prophetic god in the sacred grove of Tibur, by the Albunea spring, and on the Aventine Hill. Worshippers slept on fleeces of sacrificed lambs to receive prophetic dreams and voices.

Legacy

In Gaul he was identified with the Celtic god Dusios. In 1979, thirty-two fourth-century spoons engraved with his name were found near Thetford in England, showing that he was still worshipped even as Christianity spread.

Ancient sources: Virgil, in the Aeneid, mentions him alongside Pan; Varro describes his oracles given in Saturnian verse, and Nonnos places him with Dionysus on his campaign in India.

Trivia

Other names. When he made cattle fertile he was called Inuus. Some scholars have also identified him with Favonius, one of the Roman wind gods.
