mythical creature of North American folklore

Jackalope

The jackalope is a mythical animal of North American folklore, described as a jackrabbit with antelope horns. Born from tall tales of the American West, it is today one of the most recognisable creatures in the country's popular culture.

The myth

Accounts describe the jackalope as a jackrabbit with antelope horns. The name blends jackrabbit and antelope, although jackrabbits are hares rather than rabbits and the pronghorn is not a true antelope. Folklorists place it among the so-called fearsome critters of North American tradition, alongside the hodag, the giant turtle and Bigfoot, beasts that appear in tall tales and comic hoaxes.

The best-known version places its origin in Douglas, Wyoming. According to the usual account, in the 1930s the Herrick brothers, who had learned taxidermy, grafted deer antlers onto a jackrabbit hide and sold the piece to a local hotel. They went on to make many more, and other taxidermists still make them.

Tradition adds other traits: the jackalope is said to be shy unless approached, to imitate any sound, including the human voice, to throw off pursuers, to be lured with a bottle of whiskey, and to breed only during hailstorms. Some stories claim its meat tastes like lobster and that females can be milked, though The New York Times doubts the doe milk sold in Douglas.

Legacy

The jackalope appears in bars and souvenir shops across the United States, on postcards and as a company logo. It has turned up in shows such as Frasier and Avatar: The Last Airbender, and in Pixar's short film Boundin', which was nominated for an Oscar. The Wyoming Legislature has considered making it the state's official mythological creature.

Trivia

Licensed hunting. Douglas issues jackalope hunting licenses to tourists. The official season lasts a single day, June 31, a date that does not exist.
A medical origin. The Shope papilloma virus causes horn-like tumors in rabbits. Researchers believe it may have inspired some tales of horned hares.
