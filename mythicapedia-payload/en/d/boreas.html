Greek god of the north wind

Boreas

Boreas is the Greek god of the cold north wind, storms and winter. Son of Eos and Astraeus, he is brother to Zephyrus, Notus and the other winds, and his abduction of the Athenian princess Oreithyia made him a beloved figure in Athens.

The myth

According to Hesiod's Theogony, Eos bore Boreas, Zephyrus and Notus to Astraeus, and the tradition describes him as a winged old man with a bearded face and shaggy hair, with a violent temper that could stir seas and lands, freeze water and bring snow and hail. The Greeks placed his home in Thrace, and beyond his breath they imagined Hyperborea, a land where people lived in complete happiness to extraordinary old age.

The best-known episode is the abduction of Oreithyia, an Athenian princess, on the banks of the Ilissus. He first pleaded for her favour, but when that failed he seized her while she danced and carried her off to Thrace in a cloud of wind. There she bore him two sons, the Boreads Zetes and Calais, and two daughters, Chione and Cleopatra. From then on the Athenians regarded him as a relative by marriage.

Boreas is also closely linked to horses. In one tradition he took the form of a dark stallion and fathered twelve colts with the three thousand mares of Erichthonius, swift enough to run over a field of grain without trampling the ears. Other accounts give him different unions and offspring, such as the horses of Erechtheus or those of Ares, and love affairs with Pitys and Hyacinthus.

Worship and religion

When Xerxes threatened Athens, according to Herodotus, the Athenians prayed to Boreas and credited his winds with sinking four hundred Persian ships. In gratitude they built him a shrine by the Ilissus, and a similar event is said to have happened twelve years earlier. Other Greek cities also honoured him for similar help, such as Megalopolis and Thurii, where he was granted citizenship and a plot of land.

Legacy

The abduction of Oreithyia was a popular subject on Attic vase painting, where Boreas appears as a bearded man in a tunic with shaggy hair. Aeschylus dramatised it in his lost play Oreithyia, and Jean-Philippe Rameau wrote his last opera, Les Boréades (1755), inspired by him. His name gave English the word 'boreal', and the Latin Septentrio, linked to the seven stars of the Big Dipper, lies behind 'septentrional'.

Ancient sources: Hesiod names him in the Theogony as a son of Eos and Astraeus, and Ovid credits him with stirring up storms and churning the seas.

Trivia

A wind that helped Athens. Herodotus says the Athenians believed Boreas sank the Persian fleet, and so they built him a shrine by the Ilissus.
A contest with the Sun. In an Aesop fable, Boreas and the Sun bet on who can make a traveller remove his cloak. The Sun wins with heat, since persuasion beats force.
