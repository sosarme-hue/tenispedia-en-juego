protector of the Gelug school of Tibetan Buddhism

Dorje Shugden

Dorje Shugden (rdo rje shugs ldan) is a protective entity linked to the Gelug school, the newest school of Tibetan Buddhism, and the centre of a bitter controversy since the 1930s. Once a vengeful spirit of southern Tibet, he was adopted as a minor protector of the school.

The myth

According to early histories, the Fifth Dalai Lama destroyed Shugden through fire-ritual exorcism and tantric rites. Shugden's followers later claimed that the attempt had failed. As an angry spirit, he is shown with a blood-dripping sword, a skull-cup filled with organs and a crown of five skulls, standing on a carpet of human skins.

According to Dreyfus, Shugden was a minor Gelug protector until Pabongkhapa began promoting him as the chief protector in the 1930s. Pabongkhapa presented him as a violent guardian who acted against other traditions, replacing older protectors such as Pehar, Nechung, Palden Lhamo and, above all, Kalarupa, whom Tsongkhapa himself was believed to have appointed.

Worship and religion

His worship included oracles, such as the one at the Trode Khangsar shrine in Lhasa and the Panglung oracle, which Joseph Rock documented in Kham in 1928. Images of him are rare in the Gelug tradition before the late nineteenth century. His worship continues today, though it remains contested within Gelug circles.

Legacy

His cult remains a source of dispute inside and outside Tibetan Buddhism. In 1996, Kelsang Gyatso, founder of the New Kadampa Tradition, was expelled from Sera Je Monastery for supporting Shugden and criticising the Dalai Lama.

Trivia

An oracle and a sword. In 1928, the Panglung oracle twisted a sword of Mongolian steel into many loops during a public invocation witnessed by Joseph Rock.
A protector without early art. Very little artwork depicting Shugden exists in the Gelug school before the end of the nineteenth century, reflecting how recent his central role is.
