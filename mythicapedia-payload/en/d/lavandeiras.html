washerwoman spirits of Celtic and Iberian folklore

Lavandeiras

The lavandeiras are female spirits from the folklore of northern Spain, Portugal and the wider Celtic world. They appear washing bloodstained sheets beside rivers and springs, and their presence is linked to coming deaths.

The myth

According to the tales, they appear as old women with white hair or as young, beautiful women who sing in a seductive voice. They ask travellers to help them wring the laundry; if the helper twists in the opposite direction to theirs, he may die, and anyone who refuses is also at risk.

In Celtic tradition, a druid warns the hero Cu Chulainn that a washerwoman at the river foretells his death. The hero replies that he does not fear a ghost washing bloodstained clothes, and soon after he falls in battle. In northern Iberia, the washerwomen are linked to women who died in childbirth or to unbaptised children, and in Galicia and Asturias the legend has merged with that of the meninos, small beings who live in caves.

Legacy

The figure crossed to America with the Spanish conquistadors and gave rise to the siguanaba, a woman who washes clothes by rivers and springs and seduces men. In the nineteenth century, French Romantic writers such as Victor Hugo, Guy de Maupassant and George Sand wrote about the lavandières of Brittany and other regions.

Ancient sources: Medieval Irish literature shows female figures washing bloodstained shrouds as a sign of future battles and deaths, and the legends of Cu Chulainn include the druid's warning.

Trivia

The road of springs. According to some stories, to meet a lavandeira you must walk past 7 or 9 springs that do not dry up in summer. The encounter comes at the seventh or ninth.
From the Otherworld to the war goddess. Some specialists link these washerwomen to the Celtic Otherworld and to the goddess Morrigan, who foretells the death of Cu Chulainn by washing his bloodstained clothes.
