heroine of Greek mythology, wife of Protesilaus

Laodamia

Laodamia is a figure of Greek mythology, the daughter of King Acastus of Iolcus and the wife of the Thessalian prince Protesilaus. Her story of love and grief is one of the best-known Greek tales about death, separation and a brief return from the world of the dead.

The myth

Protesilaus was the first Greek to fall in the Trojan War, shortly after he had married Laodamia. According to the myth, the gods allowed him to return to the world of the living for three hours to be with his wife. When that time was up, he had to go back to the underworld. Laodamia, unable to bear the loss, is said in the most widespread version to have stabbed herself.

Hyginus, in his Fabulae, tells a different version. After her husband left, Laodamia had a bronze likeness of him made and kept it in her room, where she performed rites before it and embraced and kissed it. A servant caught her and told her father, Acastus, who burst into the room, saw the statue and had it burned on a pyre together with the offerings. Laodamia could not endure the loss and threw herself into the flames, either out of despair or to join her husband in the land of the dead.

Legacy

The myth inspired the tragedy Protesilaus and Laodamia by the Polish author Stanisław Wyspiański. Her name also appears among the inhabitants of the Mourning Fields, a region of the underworld that Aeneas visits in Book VI of Virgil's Aeneid, where she is remembered as one of the souls who suffer for love.

Ancient sources: Hyginus' Fabulae, which contains the bronze statue version, and Book VI of Virgil's Aeneid, which places her among the inhabitants of the Mourning Fields.

Trivia

The Mourning Fields. Virgil places Laodamia in the Mourning Fields, a region of the underworld that Aeneas crosses in Book VI of the Aeneid.
A bronze statue. In Hyginus' version, Laodamia did not die at once. Instead she had a bronze likeness of her husband made and devoted herself to it in her room.
