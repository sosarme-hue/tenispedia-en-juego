Greek god of mockery and fault-finding

Momus

Momus is the Greek god of mockery and fault-finding, the personification of satire and reproach. First a poetic figure, he later became in humanist tradition a symbol of pointed, useful criticism.

The myth

Hesiod made Momus a son of Night, who in his account had no partner, while Cicero names both Night and Erebus as his parents. His best-known episode comes from Aesop's fables. Three gods, Zeus, Prometheus and Athena, made a bull, a man and a house, and chose Momus as judge. He found fault with everything: he told Zeus that the bull had no eyes in its horns to see where it charged, Prometheus that the man's thoughts were hidden, and Athena that the house had no wheels to escape bad neighbours. Angered by his spite, Zeus threw him out of Olympus.

Lucian tells a similar story with Poseidon, Athena and Hephaestus as makers, and a Momus who criticises the man for having no windows in his chest. Philostratus recounts how, looking over Aphrodite, he could find only one flaw: her sandals squeaked. In the lost epic Cypria, attributed to Stasinus, Momus advised Zeus to ease the overcrowded earth with the Trojan War rather than destroy it with thunderbolts or floods.

Worship and religion

Little cult is documented in antiquity. Celebrations of Momus survive in the Momoeria, New Year festivals in northern Greece.

Legacy

In the Renaissance he became a mouthpiece for criticism of power, as in Leon Battista Alberti's Momus, and Erasmus presented him as a champion of legitimate criticism. Baltasar Gracian gave him a chapter in El Criticon, and Francis Bacon cited him in an essay on building. On the European stage he softened into a harmless figure of fun, akin to Harlequin. Today his name lives on in the carnivals of Cadiz, Malaga, Barranquilla, Buenos Aires and Montevideo.

Ancient sources: Hesiod, the Cypria attributed to Stasinus, Aesop's fables, Plato, Lucian and Philostratus mention him.

Trivia

A squeaky sandal. According to Philostratus, the only fault Momus found in Aphrodite was that her sandals squeaked, a joke about her chattiness.
An envious judge. His name comes from a Greek word meaning blame or reproach, which explains his reputation as a tireless critic.
