fire-breathing giant of Roman mythology

Cacus

Cacus is a fire-breathing giant in Roman mythology, son of Vulcan, who lived in a cave on the Aventine Hill, on the site of future Rome. His story is tied to that of Hercules, and his name survives in Spanish as a word for thief.

The myth

According to the tradition, Cacus lived in a cave where he nailed the heads of his victims to the doors. In the Aeneid, King Evander tells Aeneas that Hercules stopped to pasture the cattle he had taken from Geryon near the cave. While the hero slept, Cacus stole four pairs of oxen, dragging them by the tails so that their tracks pointed the wrong way.

When Hercules woke, he heard the rest of the herd lowing, and a cow answered from inside the cave. Cacus had blocked the entrance with a boulder held by chains forged by Vulcan, so the hero had to tear off the mountaintop. Cacus spat flames and smoke, but Hercules answered with branches and millstone-sized rocks until he lost patience, leapt into the cave and strangled him. Other versions say he was beaten to death with a club, or that his sister Caca revealed his hiding place.

Worship and religion

In the Aeneid, Hercules founded an altar to commemorate his victory, the Ara Maxima, beside which the Forum Boarium, Rome's cattle market, later developed. Each year rites were performed in his honour, and the hero had temples in the area, including the Temple of Hercules Victor, which still stands.

Legacy

His name passed into Spanish as a word for thief, and in the Golden Age he was almost regarded as a patron of thieves; the phrase la ciencia de Caco, the science of Caco, appears in La Palomilla. Cervantes cites him in Don Quixote as a model thief, Dante places him in Hell guarding the thieves, and Ursula K. Le Guin and Rick Riordan have reimagined him in their works.

Ancient sources: Virgil's Aeneid (Book VIII), which gives the version told by Evander; Dionysius of Halicarnassus, Plutarch and Solinus also mention him.

Trivia

A Greek-rooted name. His name is linked to the Greek kakos, meaning bad, which fits his reputation as a thief and a man-eater.
Oxen walking backwards. In a letter to a friend, Thomas Browne compared old people reluctant to go to the grave with Cacus' oxen, which moved backwards.
