Chinese stories about the origins of the world

Chinese creation myths

A set of narratives in Chinese cosmology explaining the origin of the universe, the earth and life. Unlike other traditions, they do not form a single story but complementary and often contradictory versions.

The myth

Ancient texts offer several versions. In one, the Huainanzi describes a primordial energy, qi, which separates into the pure and bright, forming heaven, and the heavy and turbid, forming earth; from their essences come yin and yang, the seasons, the stars and living beings.

Another version, in the Songs of Chu, begins with a formless chaos and poses questions about how heaven, earth and yin and yang came to be, without answering them. In the Tao Te Ching, the Way gives birth to unity, duality, trinity and the myriad creatures.

In other accounts, two creator spirits shape heaven and earth, and depending on whether their energy is confused or refined, creatures or human beings appear.

Legacy

The concepts of qi, or vital energy, and of yin and yang have passed into traditional Chinese medicine, philosophy and everyday life across East Asia.

Ancient sources: Tao Te Ching, Songs of Chu (Tianwen), Huainanzi, Daoyuan and Taiyi Shengshui.

Trivia

A creative New Year. According to a tradition recorded in the sources, the world was created at Chinese New Year, and animals, people and many deities came into being during its fifteen days.
No single creator. Scholars note that, unlike the Bible, the Chinese tradition preserves numerous accounts with no official version and no central creator god.
