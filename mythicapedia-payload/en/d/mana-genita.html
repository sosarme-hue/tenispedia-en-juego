Roman goddess of newborns

Mana Genita

Mana Genita, also known as Genita Mana, was an obscure Roman goddess associated with newborn children and, according to some writers, with childbirth. She is barely attested in ancient sources, so many details remain uncertain.

The myth

According to Plutarch and Pliny, a dog or a bitch was sacrificed to the goddess while a prayer was recited asking that no member of the household become "good". Plutarch offered several readings of this formula: perhaps it referred to dogs, which must be fierce to guard the house, or perhaps it was a veiled request that no one should die.

Plutarch also linked the goddess to Hecate and to Eileithyia, and suggested that her name came from the Latin verb fluere, "to flow".

Worship and religion

Her cult was tied to the household, since the sacrifice and prayer were offered on behalf of the members of the home. The sources say nothing about whether it survived later.

Ancient sources: Pliny, Plutarch (in his Roman Questions) and Horace, who alludes to her in the Carmen saeculare.

Trivia

A disputed name. Plutarch proposed that Mana came from fluere, "to flow", an explanation the grammarian Verrius Flaccus also connected with the manes, the souls of the dead.
A Greek parallel. Plutarch compares her with Hecate and with Eileithyia, the Greek goddess of childbirth, since the Greeks also sacrificed bitches in their rites.
