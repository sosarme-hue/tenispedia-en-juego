series of feats of the Greek hero Heracles

The Labours of Heracles

A series of twelve feats that Greek myth attributes to Heracles while he served King Eurystheus. They are the hero's most famous exploits, and their standard version owes much to the compilation of Apollodorus.

The myth

According to the tradition, Hera, Heracles's enemy since his birth, drove him mad, and in his frenzy he killed his children by Megara. To atone, he consulted the oracle at Delphi, which ordered him to serve Eurystheus, king of Mycenae, and perform the tasks he set. Once they were completed, immortality awaited him.

The labours are told in different ways. According to Apollodorus, whose list is the most widely accepted, the first was slaying the Nemean lion, an invulnerable beast that Heracles strangled in its cave. Then came the Lernaean Hydra, the Erymanthian Boar, the Ceryneian Hind, the stables of Augeas, the Stymphalian Birds, the Cretan Bull, the Mares of Diomedes, the belt of Hippolyta, the cattle of Geryon, the golden apples of the Hesperides and, finally, the capture of Cerberus in the Underworld. Diodorus and other authors change the order of some feats, and according to Apollodorus Eurystheus did not count two of them, so the hero had to perform two more.

Worship and religion

In the story, Heracles made a sacrifice to Zeus Soter ('Savior') at Cleonae after killing the lion, before carrying the pelt to Mycenae. The sources do not describe a specific cult devoted to the labours as such.

Legacy

The labours appear on the metopes of the Temple of Zeus at Olympia, dating to around 460 BC. Hellenistic tradition later linked them to the signs of the zodiac, a continuation that reached into astrology.

Ancient sources: The Iliad is the oldest literary reference; a now-lost poem by Peisander fixed the cycle of twelve labours, and the standard version comes from Apollodorus's Library, alongside Diodorus Siculus and Euripides.

Trivia

The origin of the Milky Way. According to the story, Hera pushed the infant Heracles away from her breast, and her milk sprayed across the sky, forming the Milky Way.
A labour out of order. Diodorus places the golden apples of the Hesperides as the twelfth labour, and says Heracles expected to gain immortality by bringing them to Eurystheus.
