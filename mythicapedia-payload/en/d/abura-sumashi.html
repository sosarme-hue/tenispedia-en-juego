yōkai from the folklore of Amakusa, Japan

Abura-sumashi

The abura-sumashi is a yōkai, a spirit from the folklore of Amakusa in Kumamoto Prefecture, Japan. Its name means "oil squeezer," and its stories revolve around theft in an era when oil was a precious commodity.

The myth

According to tradition, the abura-sumashi is the ghost of a person who stole oil and fled into the woods. Before electricity, oil lit and heated homes, so stealing it was a serious crime. In some versions, stealing oil from temples and shrines could be punished by reincarnation as a yōkai.

The spirit mainly appears on mountain passes, such as Kusazumigoe, where it startles travelers. In many tales, an old grandmother walking a mountain pass with her grandchildren tells them that long ago an abura-sumashi lived there, and a mysterious voice replies, "I still do!" On rare occasions, the creature appears to travelers, materializing out of thin air.

Legacy

In modern popular culture it is depicted as a squat creature covered by a straw coat, with a head resembling a stone or a potato. This look is inspired by the artwork of Shigeru Mizuki.

Trivia

The name. "Abura" means oil and "sumashi" means to press or squeeze. Literally, the name could be translated as "oil squeezer."
The voice on the pass. In the tales, an old grandmother walking with her grandchildren mentions the abura-sumashi, and a voice answers from somewhere that it is still there.
