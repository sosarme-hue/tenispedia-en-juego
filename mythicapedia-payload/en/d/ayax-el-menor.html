Greek hero, leader of the Locrians

Ajax the Lesser

Ajax the Lesser, son of King Oileus, led the Locrian contingent in the Trojan War and is told apart from Ajax the Great by his smaller stature and his tragic fate. His story links Homer's epic with later accounts of the sacrilege against Cassandra and the gods' punishment. The myth presents him as a skilled fighter with the spear and a swift runner who wore a linen cuirass. According to Homer, he led forty ships from Locris to Troy and took part in the main battles of the Iliad. During the funeral games for Patroclus, Athena made him stumble in the footrace and he lost the first prize, a sign of his troubled relationship with the goddess. After the fall of Troy, according to some accounts, he dragged Cassandra away from Athena's temple, where she had taken refuge; in some versions he raped her there. The Greeks wanted to stone him, but he saved himself by swearing innocence while clinging to the goddess's statue. Even so, Athena stirred up a storm that scattered the fleet on the voyage home. There are two endings. In the most widespread, his ship broke against the Gyrean Rocks near Euboea, and Poseidon, with his trident, killed him after Ajax boasted that he had escaped the sea despite the gods. In another version, the storm threw him onto a rock while he was unconscious, and he died struck by a thunderbolt or a spear from Athena. According to tradition, his body and the trident turned to stone.

The myth

Worship and religion

The Locrians of Opus venerated him as their national hero. It was said that they always left a gap in their battle formation, convinced that Ajax, unseen, was fighting with them.

Ancient sources: Homer mentions him in the Iliad and the Odyssey, Virgil in the Aeneid, and Euripides in The Trojan Women.
