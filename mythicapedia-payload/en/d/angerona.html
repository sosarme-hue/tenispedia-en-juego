Roman goddess of silence

Angerona

Angerona, also spelled Angeronia, was a Roman goddess linked to silence, anguish and the relief from them. Her figure is hard to pin down because ancient sources contradict one another, and some scholars connect her with older Celtic traditions and with the turning point of the year at the winter solstice.

The myth

According to one group of passages, Angerona was the goddess who brought on anguish and fear, but also the one who freed people from them. Other authors presented her as the protector of Rome and the keeper of its secret name, which was never to be spoken so that enemies could not learn it. In this version the goddess demanded silence by pressing a finger to her lips.

Julius Modestus and Festus recount a more concrete episode: a disease called angina struck men and animals and vanished as soon as sacrifices were offered to Angerona. A late source even suggests the secret name of the city was Amor, Roma spelled backwards, although this idea is uncertain.

Worship and religion

Her statue stood in the shrine of Volupia, near the Porta Romanula by the Roman Forum. She was shown with her mouth bandaged and sealed, a gesture that, according to Masurius Sabinus, meant that those who bore their worries patiently could reach the greatest happiness. Every 21 December the Angeronalia, also called Divalia, were celebrated, and the pontiffs offered sacrifices at the temple of Volupia and in the curia Acculeia. Her cult does not survive today.

Ancient sources: The Latin writers Festus, Julius Modestus and Masurius Sabinus, who mention her in connection with angina, silence and her statue.

Trivia

Ancharia at Faesulae. At Faesulae, today Fiesole, she was worshipped under the name Ancharia, and an altar belonging to her was discovered there in the late nineteenth century.
A bronze statuette. A small bronze statuette of a nude woman with a finger pressed to her lips may represent her.
