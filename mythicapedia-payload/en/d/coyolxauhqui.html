Aztec goddess of the Moon

Coyolxauhqui

Coyolxauhqui is a goddess of Mexica (Aztec) mythology associated with the Moon, daughter of the earth mother Coatlicue and sister of Huitzilopochtli. Her defeat at his hands is one of the most important myths of Mexica religion, and her great monolith, found in 1978, is one of the best-known Aztec monuments.

The myth

According to Mexica tradition, Coatlicue was sweeping a shrine on the summit of Coatepec when a ball of hummingbird feathers fell from the sky. She picked it up and tucked it at her waist, and became pregnant with Huitzilopochtli.

When her other children, the Centzon Huitznahua, learned of the pregnancy, they felt dishonored. Coyolxauhqui led them in an attack on their mother. Before they could carry it out, Huitzilopochtli was born fully armed, faced his siblings, beheaded Coyolxauhqui and threw her body down the mountainside, where it broke apart. He then scattered the Centzon Huitznahua, who fled south.

The story is read as an astronomical allegory: Huitzilopochtli stands for the Sun, Coyolxauhqui for the Moon and her brothers for the stars. The myth expresses the Sun's daily dominance over the Moon and stars. Some authors say her head was tossed into the sky and became the Moon, though such variants are hard to verify against sixteenth-century sources.

Worship and religion

The Coyolxauhqui monolith, about 3.25 meters across and shaped like a shield, lay at the foot of the stairs of the Templo Mayor in Tenochtitlan, on the side dedicated to Huitzilopochtli, recreating the myth of Coatepec. Its position presented the goddess's defeat as a warning to the city's enemies.

Scholars believe the ritual sacrifice of warriors echoed this story during the feast of Panquetzaliztli, dedicated to Huitzilopochtli. Captives had their hearts removed and their bodies thrown down the temple stairs to the stone, where they were beheaded and dismembered.

Legacy

The monolith's discovery in February 1978 by electric company workers spurred the archaeological excavation of the Templo Mayor, which continues today. The stone is one of the few great Aztec monuments found fully in situ.

Trivia

An accidental find. The monolith turned up in February 1978 while electric company workers were digging to lay underground cables in central Mexico City.
The four hundred southerners. Centzon Huitznahua means four hundred southerners and is the name of Coyolxauhqui's siblings who joined her attack on Coatlicue.
