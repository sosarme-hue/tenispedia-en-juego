Egyptian goddess of night and mourning

Nephthys

Nephthys is a goddess of ancient Egypt, daughter of Nut and Geb and a member of the Heliopolitan Ennead. Sister of Isis and Osiris, she is associated with mourning, night, the dead and funerary protection, and was paired with her sister in funerary rites.

The myth

According to Egyptian tradition, Nephthys was the daughter of Nut and Geb. Her name, Nebet-het, means "Lady of the House" or "Lady of the Temple", an epithet that probably linked her to a particular temple or to an aspect of temple ritual. She was traditionally regarded as the wife of Set and the mother of Anubis, although recent Egyptology has questioned this. Levai argues that her marriage to Set belonged to Egyptian mythology but not to the myth of Osiris's murder, and that it linked her to the benevolent aspect of Set, the one who killed Apophis.

In the Osiris myth, Nephthys helps Isis mourn their brother and gather his dismembered body after Set murders him. She also cared for the child Horus: the Pyramid Texts call Isis the "birth-mother" and Nephthys the "nursing-mother". For this reason she was considered the nurse of the reigning pharaoh. She also appears as one of the four "Great Chiefs" of Busiris and as a protector of Hapi, the canopic jar guarding the embalmed lungs.

Worship and religion

Nephthys was worshipped in many parts of Egypt, most often within temple groups of deities, frequently in triads with Isis and Osiris, or with Isis and Horus. At Memphis she bore the title "Queen of the Embalmer's Shop" and was linked to Anubis. At Abydos she joined Isis as a mourner in the "Lamentations of Isis and Nephthys", performed each year during the Osirian festivals by two priestesses. She was also associated with beer and festive rites. As a goddess of the ancient Egyptian religion, she has no living cult today.

Legacy

Modern Egyptology has studied her family relations and her role in ritual. She appears in scholarship on Egyptian funerary religion and in the iconography of amulets and temple reliefs.

Ancient sources: She appears in the Pyramid Texts, the Papyrus of Ani and the Book of the Dead, and Plutarch mentions her marriage to Set in De Iside et Osiride.

Trivia

Beer without a hangover. In reliefs at Edfu, Dendera and Behbeit, Nephthys receives beer offerings from the pharaoh, which she returns so that he may enjoy it without a hangover.
The Westcar Papyrus. An ancient Egyptian tale tells how Isis, Nephthys, Meskhenet and Heqet, disguised as dancers, attend the birth of the children of a priest.
