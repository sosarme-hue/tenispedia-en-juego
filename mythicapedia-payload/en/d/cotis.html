Thracian and Greek goddess of nocturnal rites

Kotys

Kotys, also called Kotytto, was a goddess worshipped in Thrace and in parts of Greece, with a cult reported in Corinth and, according to some sources, in Italy and Dorian Sicily. Her festivals were linked to night-time rites, purification by bathing, and riotous, licentious behaviour.

The myth

Ancient writers describe her as an ambiguous deity, sometimes presented as androgynous and combining attributes of both sexes. Literary sources compared her with the Anatolian Cybele, the Great Mother of the Gods, while later relief sculptures from Thrace show her as a huntress similar to Artemis.

Her worshippers were called baptes, "bathers", because the rites began with an elaborate bathing ritual. Some Greeks regarded her as an aspect of Persephone, and her cult closely resembled that of the goddess Bendis.

Worship and religion

Her festival, the Cotyttia, was held on hills and featured riotous processions and orgiastic rites, especially at night. Her worshippers were known as baptai, and purification in water was central to the rite. Her worship seems to have been adopted publicly in Corinth around 425 BC, and perhaps privately in Athens at about the same time.

Legacy

Eupolis's comedy Baptae portrayed famous men of his day as devotees of the goddess. John Milton briefly alludes to her in Comus as a dark-veiled goddess, and Algernon Charles Swinburne refers to her orgiastic nature in his poem "Prelude". Aleister Crowley used the name Cotys for a character in his short story "The Stone of Cybele".

Ancient sources: She appears in Eupolis's comedy and in the Suda, which mentions her worship among the Corinthians.

Trivia
