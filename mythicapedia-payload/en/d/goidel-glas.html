legendary ancestor of the Gaels, creator of the Goidelic languages

Goídel Glas

In medieval Irish and Scottish tradition, Goídel Glas is the creator of the Goidelic languages and the eponymous ancestor of the Gaels. His story, rooted in Celtic tradition, links the Gaels to the Old Testament and the wider Mediterranean world.

The myth

In the Lebor Gabála Érenn, composed around the eleventh century, Goídel Glas is the son of Nel, a descendant of Fénius Farsaid, and of Scota, daughter of a pharaoh. A snakebite left a green ring on him, supposedly giving him the nickname Glas, "the green"; Moses healed him with his staff.

John of Fordun's Scottish version makes him the son of a Greek king, Neolus, who was exiled to Egypt, where he served the pharaoh and married Scota. After the plagues or the drowning of the Egyptian army in the Red Sea, they fled to Hispania. There they settled at Brigantia, today A Coruña, and his descendant Breogán built a tower from which his son Íth glimpsed Ireland. The ninth-century Historia Brittonum describes him instead as a Scythian nobleman.

Legacy

Some early authors considered him the founder of the Portuguese city of Porto, which he supposedly named Portus Gaythelos, and the city's name was said to have given rise to Portugal. Modern scholars believe the tale was largely invented by medieval Irish Christian writers.

Ancient sources: The Lebor Gabála Érenn, the Auraicept na n-Éces, the ninth-century Historia Brittonum, Walter Bower's fifteenth-century Scotichronicon, and John of Fordun's Scottish version.

Trivia

The green nickname. According to Michael O'Clery, a snakebite left a green ring on him, which gave him the nickname Glas. Geoffrey Keating offers a different explanation for the same name.
A Roman tower. Breogán's tower is linked to the Tower of Hercules, which the Romans built at A Coruña.
