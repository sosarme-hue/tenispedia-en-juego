snake god of Roman Anatolia

Glycon

Glycon was a snake god who had an oracle in the city of Abonoteichos, in Paphlagonia (northern Anatolia), during the 2nd century. We know of him mainly through the satirist Lucian of Samosata, who presents him as a hoax staged by the prophet Alexander.

The myth

According to Lucian, Alexander of Abonoteichos announced that the god Asclepius was about to be reborn in his town. When the crowd gathered in the marketplace, the prophet produced a goose egg, cut it open and revealed a small snake inside, as if it were a newborn god. Within days the animal had grown enormously and had human features on its face. Lucian claimed it was really a puppet that appeared in the temple, and some accounts describe Glycon as a trained snake with a puppet head.

The cult drew on Macedonian beliefs about snakes and fertility, and on the story of Olympias, impregnated by Zeus disguised as a serpent. Barren women brought offerings in the hope of conceiving, and the god was also believed to offer protection against plague.

Worship and religion

The cult centred on the temple at Abonoteichos, where Glycon's oracle answered questions. By around 160 its fame had spread across the East, and Roman governors and even the emperor Marcus Aurelius sought its prophecies. After Alexander's death around 170 it lost followers, but it lasted for about a hundred years more, and Alexander came to be counted in its mythology as a grandson of Asclepius.

Legacy

His image appears on Roman coins and, centuries later, on a marble statue found in Constanța, Romania, now displayed in the city's history and archaeology museum. Romania honoured him on a postage stamp in 1974 and on a 10,000 lei banknote in 1994. The comic book writer Alan Moore describes himself as a devotee of Glycon.

Ancient sources: Lucian of Samosata, in his satirical work about Alexander of Abonoteichos, is the only known ancient literary reference to this god.

Trivia

A statue in Romania. A marble statue of Glycon was found during excavations under the former Pallas railway station in Constanța.
Stamp and banknote. Romania commemorated this sculpture on a postage stamp in 1974 and on a 10,000 lei banknote in 1994.
Coins with the serpent. Under Antoninus Pius, coins minted at Abonoteichos began to depict the snake Glycon specifically.
