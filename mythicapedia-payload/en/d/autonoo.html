Autonous

Autonous is a character of Greek mythology, son of Melaneus and husband of Hippodamia, who owned a large herd of horses. His story survives only in one account, in the Metamorphoses of Antoninus Liberalis.

The myth

According to the tradition, Autonous neglected the farming of his land, which produced only rushes and thistles, so his children were named after those plants. Erodius, who loved the horses most, led them to grassy meadows, but one day Anthus drove them out of their familiar pastures. Hungry, the horses attacked Anthus and ate him. Autonous, stricken with panic, could not save him, nor could Anthus' servant, and Hippodamia, weakened by her frailty, failed when she tried to drive the horses away.

Moved by pity for the grieving family, Zeus and Apollo turned them into birds. Autonous became a stone curlew, whose Greek name óknos recalls that he "was not in time" to save his son; Hippodamia became a lark, whose crest symbolizes her courage; and Acanthis became a goldfinch. Erodius and the servant became herons, one black and one white, which, according to the story, are never seen together.

Ancient sources: He appears only in the Metamorphoses of Antoninus Liberalis.

Trivia

A name with a pun. The Greek name of the stone curlew, óknos, is linked to the idea that Autonous did not arrive in time to save his son.
Why thistles and rushes. His children were named after plants because their father neglected the fields, which produced only rushes and thistles.
