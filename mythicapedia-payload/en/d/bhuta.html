ghost or wandering spirit of Indian folklore

Bhoota

The bhoota is a supernatural creature of the Indian subcontinent, usually the ghost of someone who died violently, prematurely or without a proper funeral. Its presence in popular culture, literature and some ancient texts of the region makes it one of the best-known figures of Indian folklore.

The myth

According to tradition, a bhoota is a restless soul that cannot move on, whether to rebirth, paradise or hell, to non-existence or to nirvana. The causes vary by region: a violent death, an early death or the lack of a funeral performed by a son. It can take the shape of various animals, but usually appears in human form, though its feet, turned backwards, give it away. It casts no shadow, speaks with a nasal voice, often wears white and floats above the ground it avoids because of its sacred character.

Popular stories tell of travellers who unknowingly give a lift to a bhoota, such as a woman waiting by the roadside at night, until they notice the detail that betrays her. It is said to love milk, and a person who drinks contaminated milk may become possessed. A particular kind is the churel, the spirit of a woman who died in pregnancy or childbirth, who lures young men at crossroads and can cause the death of anyone who falls in love with her, although there are tales of men who live with her or even marry her.

Protection comes from iron and steel, the smell of burnt turmeric, invoking the names of saints and gods, or sprinkling earth on oneself. Exorcists do not destroy the soul; they perform the atma-shanti rite, which uses a prayer from the Atharva Veda and is a modified śrāddha ceremony, in which the afflicted promise to fulfil the spirit's wishes or complete what it left unfinished.

Worship and religion

Hindus can pacify a bhoota through the worship known as bhuta aradhana, which involves offerings of food, fire and incense. According to some beliefs, a bhoota may become a protective and benevolent spirit, such as that of a hero or a good person who died in unfavourable circumstances.

Legacy

The term has entered idioms in Hindustani, Punjabi, Bengali and other northern Indian languages, such as bhut sawaar hona (to be obsessed with something) or bhut bangla (a neglected or abandoned house). In Java, buta denotes a malevolent giant in wayang theatre, and in Pakistan the word bhoot is also used for jinn.

Ancient sources: The ancient texts cited are the Atharva Veda, which provides the prayer for the atma-shanti rite, and the Garuda Purana, with its section on preta spirits.
