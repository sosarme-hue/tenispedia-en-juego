sea monster of the medieval bestiaries

Aspidochelone

The aspidochelone is a sea monster from medieval European tradition, sometimes described as a giant whale or sea turtle and at other times as a beast with huge spines along its back. It matters because it comes from the Greek Physiologus and became a moral allegory about deception.

The myth

According to the Physiologus, written in the 2nd century AD, the aspidochelone is a vast sea creature whose Greek name seems to combine "asp" or "shield" with "turtle". It rises from the depths with its back covered in sand and plants, so it looks like an island. Sailors land on it, drive in stakes and light fires to cook, and when the heat reaches its back the creature dives, dragging the ship and its crew down with it.

In the bestiary tradition the tale becomes an allegory: the aspidochelone stands for Satan, who deceives those he means to devour. Other versions link it to the qorha of the Talmud, the Arabic saratan and Jasconius in the Legend of Saint Brendan, where the monster is a living island that wakes when a campfire is lit.

Legacy

The story of a monster mistaken for an island spread through medieval and Arabic literature. Jorge Luis Borges recorded it in The Book of Imaginary Beings under the name "zaratan". J. R. R. Tolkien included a version of the fastitocalon, a name found in the Old English poem "The Whale", in The Adventures of Tom Bombadil.

Ancient sources: The Physiologus (2nd century AD), medieval bestiaries, the Babylonian Talmud, Basil of Caesarea's Hexameron and the Old English poem "The Whale" in the Exeter Book.

Trivia

A living island. In the Legend of Saint Brendan, the monks celebrate Easter on the back of Jasconius, and the creature wakes when they light a fire.
A name with two possible roots. The Greek name may combine "asp" or "shield" with "turtle", which explains the two ways the animal is described.
