Aztec dual deity

Ometeotl

Ometeotl is the name given to the paired creator deities of Mexica (Aztec) mythology, Ometecuhtli and Omecihuatl, also known as Tonacatecuhtli and Tonacacihuatl. Its name is usually rendered as "Two-God" or "Dual God," and scholars still argue hotly about what it really means.

The myth

According to Nahuatl and colonial sources, the pair dwelt in the highest heaven, Omeyocan, the "place of duality," above the nine levels of heaven. The History of the Mexicans as Told by Their Paintings calls them Lord and Lady of Abundance, and says they had always existed in the thirteenth heaven, with no known beginning, only their dwelling and their act of creation.

Some texts suggest the two were aspects of a single being. A singer in the Cantares mexicanos says that "they, God, stand double." Other chronicles speak of a god named Ometecuhtli, meaning "two gods," one of whom was a goddess.

Legacy

Miguel León Portilla popularized the reading of Ometeotl as "Dual God" or "Lord of Duality" and presented it as the primordial entity of the Mexica. The figure remains a matter of scholarly debate: Richard Haly (1992) argues the names derive from omitl ("bone") rather than ome ("two"), and James Maffie maintains that Mexica religion was pantheistic and centered on the concept of Teotl.

Ancient sources: The Cantares mexicanos, the History of the Mexicans as Told by Their Paintings, the Florentine Codex, the Historia Tolteca-Chichimeca, and the Codex Ríos, among other Nahuatl and early colonial chronicles.

Trivia

A disputed name. Richard Haly proposes that the name derives from omitl, "bone," rather than ome, "two," so the pair would be linked to the creation of humans from bones.
Hometeule. The Codex Vaticanus A labels this deity "Hometeule," a Spanish phonetic rendering of the Nahuatl Ometeotl.
