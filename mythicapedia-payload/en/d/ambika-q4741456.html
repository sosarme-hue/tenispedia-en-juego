Jain protector goddess, yakshi of Neminatha

Ambika

Ambika is the protector goddess of the twenty-second Tirthankara, Neminatha, in Jainism. She is also revered as a mother goddess associated with prosperity and childbirth, and her name, meaning "mother" in Sanskrit, is a common epithet of the Hindu goddess Parvati.

The myth

According to Jain texts, Ambika was once an ordinary woman named Agnila, who lived in the city of Girinagar with her husband and two children. The sources differ on the names: the Śvetāmbara tradition calls the husband Soma and the children Siddha and Buddha, while the Digambara tradition gives Somasarman, Shubhanakar and Prabhankara.

One day Soma invited Brahmins to a śrāddha, the funeral ceremony, and left Agnila at home. Varadatta, the chief disciple of Neminatha, arrived at the end of a month-long fast and asked her for food. She gave him some, but Soma and the Brahmins were furious, since they now considered the food impure. Soma drove her from the house with her children, and she climbed a hill. Because of her virtue, the tree beneath which she sat became a kalpavriksha, a wish-granting tree, and a dry water tank filled with water.

The demigods, angered by her treatment, decided to drown the whole village, except her house. Soma and the Brahmins, seeing this, begged her forgiveness. As Soma approached, fearing punishment, Agnila threw herself off a cliff, but was instantly reborn as the goddess Ambika. Her husband was reborn as a lion, came to her, licked her feet and became her mount. Later Neminatha initiated her two sons, and Ambika became his yakshi.

Worship and religion

Ambika is depicted in many images and temples across India, and Jains worship her alongside the Tirthankaras, together with Padmavati and Chakreshvari. Ambika and Padmavati also feature in tantric rituals involving yantras and mantras. As kuladevi, a family and lineage guardian, she is especially revered by the Porwad Jain community, and her worship remains alive; in Shravanabelagola it is an integral part of Jain rituals.

Legacy

Her figure appears in countless sculptures, from early reliefs to medieval statues, and in tantric texts and hymns such as the 13th-century Ambika-stavana. At Vimal Vasahi she is carved as the kalpalata, the wish-fulfilling creeper.

Ancient sources: Tantric texts and medieval Jain hymns, such as the Ambika-Kalpa and the Ambika-stavana, celebrate her as a goddess who fulfils wishes.

Trivia

Paired image. In many representations she appears paired with the yaksha Sarvanubhuti on her right, with a small Tirthankara image above them.
A mango in hand. Her usual iconography shows a mango, a symbol associated with her, held in one of her right hands.
