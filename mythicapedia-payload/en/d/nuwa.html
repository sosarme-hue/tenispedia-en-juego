Chinese goddess of creation and the repair of the sky

Nüwa

Nüwa is a goddess of Chinese mythology, often presented as the creator of humankind and the mender of the sky. She is sometimes counted among the Three Sovereigns, and she remains one of China's most venerated goddesses.

The myth

According to tradition, Nüwa appears in an age when heaven and earth were not yet in order. Texts often show her with a human body and a snake or dragon tail, a form in which she was said to have carved the world's rivers and dried the land after the floods. The sources disagree about her role: she is variously creator, goddess, empress, wife or tribal leader, and some scholars think « Nüwa » may be a title rather than a name.

The best-known episode tells of a quarrel between two powerful gods. Gonggong, god of water, lost a contest and, in his rage, struck the Buzhou mountain with his head. One of the pillars holding up the sky broke, the heavens tilted toward the northwest, the earth shifted toward the southeast, and great floods followed. Nüwa cut off the legs of a giant tortoise to replace the pillar and mended the sky with stones of five colours, though she could not fully level it. According to tradition, this is why the sun, moon and stars travel toward the northwest and China's rivers flow east. Another version says she plugged the hole in the sky with her own body.

Nüwa is also the creator of humans. In one account she first made animals: the rooster, the dog, the sheep, the pig, the cow and the horse. On the seventh day she shaped people from yellow clay, but tiring of modelling each one by hand, she dipped a rope into the mud and swung it, so that the drops falling to the ground became people. The figures shaped by hand became the nobles, and those made with the rope the common people. Other variants say rain deformed some figures, explaining illness and physical disability.

Worship and religion

In some regions of southwestern China, among ethnic minorities, she is worshipped as a goddess, and certain festivals are held as tribute to her sacrifice. According to one account, she asked the gods to make her goddess of marital affairs, which links her with matchmaking and marriage. She belongs to Chinese folk religion and also appears in Buddhism, Confucianism and Taoism. She remains one of China's most revered goddesses, alongside Guanyin and Mazu.

Legacy

Her figure has inspired literature and art across China, from tomb murals to creation tales. She is often shown holding a compass, a traditional symbol of the domed sky, and beside her husband Fuxi she appears with intertwined serpent bodies. Fuxi and Nüwa were also linked with silk, and Fuxi is sometimes credited with the origins of divination.

Ancient sources: Liezi, Chuci, Huainanzi, Shiji, Shuowen Jiezi, Duyizhi and Taiping Yulan.

Trivia

An ancient question. In the Chuci, Qu Yuan asks who shaped Nüwa's body. It is one of the earliest known mentions of the goddess and has been read as a philosophical question about who created the creator.
The origin of the fan. The Duyizhi tells how Nüwa hid her face behind a fan of grass, and says this is the origin of the custom of women carrying fans.
