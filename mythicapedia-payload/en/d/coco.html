bogeyman of Iberian and Latin American folklore

Coco

The Coco is a shapeless, ghost-like bogeyman of Iberian and Latin American folklore, used by adults to frighten children into obedience and bedtime. Known as Cuco, Cucuy or Cuca depending on the region, it remains alive in lullabies and family customs.

The myth

According to tradition, the Coco watches children from the rooftops and, if they misbehave or resist sleep, carries them off to devour them or make them vanish without a trace. It is rarely described in detail: what frightens is its behaviour, since it can take the shape of any dark shadow. That is why it is compared to the devil and is, in a sense, the opposite of the guardian angel. Some also see it as an image of the community's dead.

Its origins lie in northern Portugal and Galicia, and colonization carried it to Mexico, Argentina, Uruguay and Chile. In Brazil it became the Cuca, an alligator-faced woman who appears in Monteiro Lobato's children's books as a powerful witch.

Worship and religion

The Coco has no cult of its own. Its clearest ritual link is in Monção, Portugal, where the dragon Coca takes part in Corpus Christi festivities, facing Saint George. The tradition is still alive and draws many visitors.

Legacy

It appears constantly in lullabies, which mix soothing and threat, and in painting, most famously Francisco de Goya's Que viene el Coco. In Spanish, the word coco also colloquially means head.

Ancient sources: The earliest Castilian mention appears in Antón de Montoro's Cancionero (1445), and a Portuguese reference appears in Gil Vicente's Auto da Barca do Purgatório (1518).

Trivia

From television to alligator. Brazil's alligator-shaped Cuca came from the 1952 TV show Sítio do Picapau Amarelo. In Lobato's novel she was described only as having an alligator face and clawed fingers.
A fight with consequences. According to Monção's tradition, if the Coca defeats Saint George by frightening the horse, the harvest will fail and famine will follow. If the horse and Saint George win, the crops will be fertile.
Origin of the coconut's name. According to Joan Coromines, Vasco da Gama's sailors may have named the fruit coco because its three holes resemble a face with eyes and a mouth.
