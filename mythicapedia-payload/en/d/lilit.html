female demon of Mesopotamian and Jewish folklore

Lilith

Lilith is a female figure of Mesopotamian origin whom medieval Jewish folklore recast as Adam's first wife and later as a demon. Her evolution, from a wind spirit to a modern symbol of female independence, makes her one of the most enduring demonic figures in Western tradition.

The myth

In Mesopotamian religion, Lilith belonged to a family of spirits linked to wind, storm and air, whose name contains the root lil, meaning "wind", "air" or "spirit". According to cuneiform texts, the lilû and ardat-lilî were demonic beings associated with adults and adolescents who died before marriage or childbearing. They were believed to bring misfortune, illness and death, and to threaten children in particular.

Later Jewish tradition, probably influenced by Babylon, took up the figure. In medieval folklore, especially in the satirical Alphabet of Ben Sira, Lilith appears as Adam's first wife, created at the same time and from the same clay. She refused to submit to Adam and left the Garden of Eden. In some versions she joined the archangel Samael and bore him the lilim, her demonic children. Eve, formed later from Adam's rib, took her place.

This story grew out of a rabbinic reading of Genesis 1:27, which speaks of the creation of "male and female", and was developed by the kabbalists in the Middle Ages. Many rabbinic authorities, such as Maimonides, nevertheless rejected the existence of Lilith.

Legacy

Lilith has inspired literature, fantasy, horror and the occult. In astrology she is linked to the "Black Moon". Western art portrayed her as a femme fatale, and in today's popular culture she persists in advertising and as an image of a woman who refuses submission. Since the late twentieth century she has been reinterpreted as a symbol of female emancipation.

Ancient sources: She appears in the Babylonian Talmud, the Zohar and the Alphabet of Ben Sira, and in the Mesopotamian Gilgamesh poem her name occurs in a later tablet.
