Yoruba orisha of trickery, crossroads and language

Eshu

Eshu, also written Èṣù or Exu, is an orisha in the Yoruba religion, the messenger of the other divinities and a figure linked to chance, crossroads, speech and order. His worship has spread across the Americas and the Caribbean, where it survives under names such as Exu or Echú.

The myth

In Yoruba tradition, Eshu is a primordial divinity, an Irúnmọlẹ̀ sent by Olodumare from Ìkọ̀lé Ọ̀run, the home of the deities. Some accounts describe him as the first particle of life created by Olorun, while his genealogy makes him a son of Obatalá and of Yembo or Ochanla. He is charged with enforcing natural and divine laws and keeping order.

Eshu acts as the intermediary between humans and the orishas: he carries human sacrifices to the divinities and oversees their distribution. He lives alone in dark, remote places, in the bush or on the savannah, and is credited with the power to free people from chains and prisons. He is an inseparable friend of Ogún and Ochosi.

Worship and religion

Many Candomblé temples set a small mound of red clay dedicated to Exu near the main entrance. His offerings include palm oil, corn, beans, cassava flour and animals such as roosters and chickens. Since 1996 Havana has held a seventeen-day festival that ends on January 6, with sacrifices, songs and gifts to police officers and street vendors; it is also celebrated in Santiago de Cuba, Holguín and Matanzas. His worship remains alive.

Legacy

His figure has reached well beyond religion. He is the main antagonist of K. A. Applegate's fantasy series Everworld and appears in the film Scooby-Doo! Ghastly Goals. In the Brazilian film Cidade de Deus, a drug lord is renamed during a Candomblé ritual devoted to Exu. The missionary mistranslation of his name as "devil", widespread since the mid-nineteenth century, was challenged by activists and by the Nigerian linguist Kola Tubosun, who changed the translation on Google.

Trivia

A mistranslated name. For decades, missionaries and dictionaries rendered Èṣù as "devil" or "Satan". The term was corrected after online campaigns and academic work.
A festival in Havana. Since 1996, Havana has held a seventeen-day festival in honour of Eshu, ending on January 6, the day enslaved people once openly practised their rites.
