legendary sixth king of Rome

Servius Tullius

Servius Tullius is, according to tradition, the sixth king of Rome, of servile birth and a remarkable rise to power. He is remembered as a reforming monarch credited with the census, the reorganisation of the army and the first city walls, which made him seen as a second founder of Rome.

The myth

According to Roman tradition, Servius was the son of Ocrisia, a slave taken when Tarquinius Priscus captured the Latin town of Corniculum. Raised in the royal palace, he is said to have had a flame surround his head as he slept without harming him, a sign of divine favour that won him the regard of the king and his wife Tanaquil.

Other versions make Ocrisia the widow of a Corniculan nobleman, and some legends attribute his conception to a god, a household lar or Vulcan. After marrying the king's daughter Tarquinia, called Gegania in some sources, Servius gained influence in government.

When Tarquinius was killed by two hired assassins sent by the sons of Ancus Marcius, Tanaquil hid the death and announced from a window that the king had charged Servius with ruling in his name. Once the situation was under control, the death was made official and Servius was proclaimed king, without a popular vote. His forty-four-year reign included victories over Veii and the Etruscans, and according to Livy it ended when he was murdered by his daughter Tullia and her husband Tarquinius Superbus.

Legacy

His figure is linked to the Servian Walls, which ancient tradition attributed to him. Over the centuries his reign became a reference point for the development of Roman citizenship, a recurring theme in the political history of Rome.

Ancient sources: Livy, in his Ab urbe condita, and Dionysius of Halicarnassus, in his Roman Antiquities, record the main versions of his life, while Cicero mentions him in De re publica.

Trivia

A flame over his head. A fiery portent above the sleeping boy's head was, according to tradition, what revealed his destiny as king.
A king without a popular vote. His accession was, according to Livy, the first in which the Roman people took no part in choosing the king.
