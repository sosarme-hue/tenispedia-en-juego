Brahmā

Brahmā is a celestial deva who, in Buddhism, holds the highest place in the realm of form and who, according to tradition, urged the Buddha to teach the Dharma after his enlightenment. The name comes from Vedic tradition, but in Buddhism he is not a creator god as he is in Hinduism.

The myth

In early Buddhist cosmology there are many Brahmās, one in each world, living in Brahmaloka, a heavenly realm of pleasure without pain that is not eternal, since all planes of samsara decay and are regenerated. Several of them claim to be all-powerful creators, but the Buddha corrects them. The most prominent is the Great Brahmā, called Brahma-sahampati, who appeared before Siddhartha after his enlightenment and asked him to teach the path to liberation.

In Hindu tradition, by contrast, Brahmā is a four-headed god who created our universe. According to the Bhagavata Purana, when he visited Krishna's palace he discovered countless Brahmās, principal gods of other universes, and realised he was just one among many.

Worship and religion

In Buddhism, Brahmā is regarded as a protector of the teaching (dharmapala). In Thailand, where he is called Phra Phrom, he is venerated as the god of order, government, law, education and the physical world, and his worship remains alive.

Legacy

His name is used in Hinduism and Buddhism with different meanings, and Buddhist texts employ the concept of the brahmaviharas, the four meditative virtues that Buddhism acknowledges predate its own tradition.

Ancient sources: Early Buddhist texts such as the Tevijja Sutta and the Janavasabha Sutta, and the Hindu Bhagavata Purana, mention Brahmā.
