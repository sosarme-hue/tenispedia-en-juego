Mares of Diomedes

In Greek mythology the Mares of Diomedes were man-eating horses kept by the Thracian king Diomedes, and capturing them was the eighth of the labours of Heracles. Their story also links Heracles to the Bistones and to his companion Abderus.

The myth

According to tradition, Diomedes was a king of Thrace, ruler of the Bistones, who kept a herd of savage mares. They were chained to bronze mangers and fed on the unsuspecting guests who came to his palace, whom they tore apart. Eurystheus ordered Heracles to bring them to Mycenae as one of his twelve labours.

The ancient sources do not entirely agree. In the earliest version, preserved through Pindar, Heracles enters the palace alone at night, throws a stableman into the manger to distract the animals and breaks the chain while they devour him. Diomedes probably dies, perhaps defending his property. In Apollodorus' version, Heracles travels with volunteers, overpowers the grooms and drives the mares towards the sea; while he fights the Bistones and kills Diomedes, his lover Abderus is left in charge of the animals and is dragged to death by them. Diodorus Siculus tells that Heracles threw Diomedes himself to the horses, which devoured their master and were then tamed. Heracles afterwards handed the animals to Eurystheus, who dedicated them to Hera.

Legacy

Lucretius, in his poem on nature, alludes to these mares, describing them as animals that breathed fire from their nostrils. Some scholars think he was referring to a different tradition or playing with Euripides' tragedy.

Ancient sources: Pindar, Apollodorus, Diodorus Siculus, Euripides in his Alcestis and Lucretius in De rerum natura.

Trivia

Ancestors of Bucephalus. According to some sources, these mares were ancestors of Bucephalus, the horse of Alexander the Great.
Fire-breathing mares. Lucretius describes them breathing fire from their nostrils, a trait not found in the accounts of Pindar or Apollodorus.
