legendary red cow of Aizu, Japan

Akabeko

Akabeko is a legendary red cow from the Aizu region of Japan, which gave its name to a traditional papier-mâché toy. It is a symbol of the area and of Fukushima Prefecture, and its image is linked to Buddhist devotion and protection against illness.

The myth

According to the Aizu legend, recorded by Thomas Madden, the akabeko was a red ox that lived in 807 CE. It worked on the construction of the Enzō-ji temple in Yanaizu, under the supervision of the monk Tokuichi. When the building was finished, the animal gave its spirit to the Buddha, and its flesh turned to stone.

Another version says the cow refused to leave the temple grounds after construction and became a permanent fixture there. In the Aizu dialect, beko means cow, hence the name, and the animal became a symbol of faithful devotion to the Buddha.

Worship and religion

The toy is tied to a folk belief: during a smallpox outbreak in the 17th century, people noticed that children who owned akabeko seemed not to catch the disease, perhaps because of the red colour, associated with protective amulets. It thus became a charm against illness, a belief that persists today.

Legacy

The akabeko is one of Japan's best-known crafts and one of the few from Fukushima with nationwide fame. It is made in about a dozen family workshops that have passed down the technique for generations, in a process that takes around ten days.

Trivia

Separate pieces. The toy consists of two papier-mâché pieces: a hollow body, and a head and neck that hang from a string and bob when moved.
Workshop markings. Workshops decorate the toys differently. The Igarashi family paints the kanji kotobuki (longevity and luck) on the cow's back.
