Greek goddesses of fate

Moirai

The Moirai are the personifications of destiny in Greek mythology. In classical tradition they often appear as three sisters who spin, measure and cut the thread of each person's life. Their name means portion or lot, since every being receives a moira, a share of life.

The myth

According to tradition, the Moirai began as an impersonal, abstract force with no fixed face or number. In Homer's Iliad they appear in the singular, as Moira, who spins the thread of life at birth, and the Odyssey mentions the Klothes, the spinners, in the plural. Over time their number settled at three, and Hesiod was the first to give them individual names. Clotho spins the thread with her distaff and presides over births, Lachesis measures its length with a rod, and Atropos, the inflexible one, cuts it when the hour comes.

Their power even reached the gods. During the Gigantomachy they killed Agrios and Toas with bronze clubs, and in the Typhonomachy they tricked Typhon into eating short-lived fruits. Since they know the future, they are also prophetic: at Calydon they foretold the fate of the infant Meleager, linked to a burning brand from the hearth. According to Plato, they sang in harmony, Lachesis of the past, Clotho of the present and Atropos of the future. They are also associated with death, alongside the Keres and the Erinyes, and they escort Persephone from the underworld. Zeus appears as their overseer, but for Aeschylus, Herodotus and Plato he was not the final decider of fate.

Worship and religion

Pausanias mentions a sanctuary of the Moirai beside that of Zeus at the gates of Thebes, without a statue, and at Sicyon they received the same annual sacrifices as the Erinyes. In Athens, Aphrodite Urania was considered the greatest of them, and at Delphi they were sometimes counted as two. The sources do not say whether their worship survives today.

Legacy

The Romans identified them with the Parcae, and Dante names them by their Greek names in the Divine Comedy. Shakespeare drew on the Norns and the Moirai when creating the Weird Sisters of Macbeth, figures who foretell the protagonists' fate.

Ancient sources: Homer (Iliad and Odyssey), Hesiod, Aeschylus, Herodotus, Plato and Pausanias mention them in their works.

Trivia

Zeus Moiragetes. Pausanias records the supposed epithet Zeus Moiragetes, meaning leader of the Moirai, from an inscription at Olympia.
Macbeth's witches. Shakespeare called his witches the Weird Sisters, a term related to Old English wyrd, meaning fate.
