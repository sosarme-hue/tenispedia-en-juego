primordial Greek goddess of the Earth

Gaia

Gaia (also Gaea) is the personification of the Earth in Greek mythology, one of the primordial deities that arose from Chaos. She is the mother of Uranus, the Titans, the Cyclopes and the Hecatonchires, and her story is told above all in Hesiod's Theogony, where she is both the physical stage of creation and the origin of powers that later fight for control of the cosmos.

The myth

According to Hesiod, at the beginning there was Chaos, and then Gaia emerged, broad-breasted, the eternal seat of the Olympian gods. Without union with anyone, she bore Uranus, the starry sky, equal to herself in size; then the Oreae, the mountains, and finally Pontus, the barren and restless sea. With Uranus, her first husband, she had the Titans, the one-eyed Cyclopes and the Hecatonchires, who had a hundred hands and fifty heads. Uranus hid his children inside Gaia, who suffered and devised a plan with them.

Gaia forged a sickle of grey flint or adamant and asked her sons to avenge their father's outrage. Only Cronus agreed. When Uranus came to Gaia, Cronus castrated him with the sickle. From the blood that fell upon the Earth came the Erinyes, the Giants and the Meliae. Gaia later united with Pontus, and bore Nereus, Thaumas, Phorcys, Ceto and Eurybia.

Fearing to be overthrown, Cronus swallowed his children, as Uranus and Gaia had warned him he would be. When Zeus was born, Gaia hid him on Crete and raised him, and later advised him in the struggle against the Titans. In other versions, Gaia united with Tartarus and bore Typhon, the last challenge to Zeus's authority. Tradition presents her as a primitive force opposed to the order of the gods, rather than a gentle mother.

Worship and religion

Gaia received little worship of her own. In classical Greece she had no major festivals or principal temples, and she was usually honoured together with other gods and goddesses. Black sheep were sacrificed to her, and she was invoked in oaths by striking the ground with the hands, as the Homeric poems show. Local cults of the earth seem older than the Olympian religion, though her worship did not survive as such.

Legacy

Her name gave rise to scientific terms such as "geology" and "geography". The Gaia hypothesis, proposed by James Lovelock and Lynn Margulis, takes her name to describe the Earth as a self-regulating system. The Greek name has also inspired the names of institutions and artworks.

Ancient sources: Hesiod's Theogony is the main text on her cosmogonic role; the Homeric poems mention her in oaths and sacrifices, and Hyginus's Fabulae and Apollodorus's Library record versions of her offspring.

Trivia

Gaia and the sickle. According to Hesiod, Gaia herself forged the sickle with which Cronus castrated Uranus, making her the instigator of her husband's overthrow.
Olive and rosemary. Some lesser-known myths say that Gaia turned the athlete Elaea into an olive tree and the young Libanus into rosemary after their unjust deaths.
