prophetic sea god of Greek mythology

Proteus

Proteus is an early prophetic sea god of Greek mythology, best known for his ability to change shape at will. Homer calls him one of the "Old Man of the Sea", and he serves Poseidon as herdsman of the sea beasts. His name gave English the adjective "protean", meaning versatile or ever-changing.

The myth

In the oldest traditions, Proteus was a servant of Poseidon who could see through the depths of the sea. Each midday he came out of the water to sleep in the shade of rocks, surrounded by sea monsters. Anyone who wanted to force a prophecy had to seize him during his sleep, because Proteus would try to escape by taking every possible form. When he saw that his efforts were in vain, he resumed his usual shape, told the truth, and returned to the sea.

Homer places his home on the sandy island of Pharos, near the Nile Delta, which is why he was also called the Egyptian. Virgil names instead the island of Carpathos, between Crete and Rhodes, and says Proteus was born in Thessaly. In the Odyssey, Menelaus is becalmed at Pharos on his way home from Troy and, guided by Eidothea, daughter of Proteus, holds him down. Proteus turns into a lion, a serpent, a leopard, a pig, water and a tree, then reveals that Agamemnon was murdered, that Ajax the Lesser was shipwrecked, and that Odysseus is stranded on Ogygia. In Virgil's Georgics, Aristaeus must capture him to learn how to save his bees.

Another tradition makes him a king of Egypt, son of Poseidon, married to the Nereid Psamathe or to Torone. His sons Telegonus and Polygonus were killed by Heracles, and he also had Theoclymenus and Theonoe. According to Diodorus, the Egyptians called him Cetes. In Euripides, Proteus welcomed Dionysus, and Hermes brought Helen to his court.

Legacy

The name survives in English as the adjective "protean", used for things that are versatile or ever-changing. Alchemist Heinrich Khunrath linked him to mercury and the unconscious, and Carl Jung took him as a symbol of the unconscious. Milton, Shakespeare and Wordsworth mention him in their works, and the Uruguayan writer José Enrique Rodó titled one of his major essays Motivos de Proteo.

Ancient sources: Homer (Odyssey), Virgil (Georgics) and Euripides (Helen), among other ancient authors.

Trivia

A name with an Egyptian echo. Some sources suggest his name derives from the Egyptian Prouti, and Diodorus notes that the Egyptians called him Cetes.
An oracle that must be caught. To obtain his prophecy one had to hold him while he slept, without letting go even as he changed shape.
