Shiva as Lord of the Dance

Nataraja

Nataraja is a depiction of the Hindu god Shiva as the divine cosmic dancer, Lord of the Dance. It is one of the most recognisable images in Hindu art and a popular symbol of Indian culture.

The myth

According to Hindu tradition, Shiva performs the cosmic dance called the Ananda Tandava, the dance of bliss. As he dances, the universe is set in motion; when he stops, the universe ends, only to be recreated when the dance resumes. The ring of flames around him stands for the cosmic fire that dissolves the universe at the end of each cycle of creation and destruction.

His four arms carry attributes with their own meaning. In the upper right hand he holds the damaru, the drum whose sound gave rise to creation, and in the upper left, agni, the fire. The lower right hand makes the abhaya mudra, which dispels fear, and the lower left points to the raised foot, a sign of liberation from ignorance. Shiva treads on the dwarf Apasmara, who embodies the ignorance and desire that bind humans to the illusion of Maya. His vertical third eye burned Kama, desire, to ashes.

Worship and religion

The Nataraja temple at Chidambaram, in Tamil Nadu, is one of the most important shrines of Shiva and one of five temples dedicated to the elements, here space or akasha. Unlike the other four, it contains no stone lingam: the god is worshipped through the bronze image and in an empty chamber, the Chit Sabha, where the akasha lingam is said to be. Worship follows Hindu agamas and, since the Chola era, the image of Nataraja has been carried in processions and festivals. The worship continues today.

Legacy

After Ananda Coomaraswamy published The Dance of Siva in 1918, the image spread in the West. Philosophers such as Aldous Huxley, physicists such as Fritjof Capra and the cosmologist Carl Sagan referred to it in their writings. In 2004 a two-metre statue of the dancing Shiva was unveiled at CERN in Geneva. Bharatanatyam celebrates 108 poses associated with Nataraja.

Ancient sources: Tamil texts such as the Tevaram and Manikkavacakar's Thiruvasagam, the Natya Shastra, the Koyil Puranam, and the Uttarakamika and Amshumadagama agamas.

Trivia

Statue at CERN. In 2004 a two-metre statue of the dancing Shiva was unveiled at CERN, the European centre for particle physics research in Geneva.
An empty chamber. At Chidambaram, the bronze image is complemented by the Chit Sabha, an empty chamber about 1.5 metres high, hung with curtains and roofed with a golden dome.
Five-pointed flames. Although the flames around Shiva first had three points, the five-pointed form became typical in the 11th and 12th centuries.
