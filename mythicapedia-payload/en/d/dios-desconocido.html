nameless deity of ancient Greek religion

Unknown God

The Unknown God (in Greek, Agnostos Theos) is the title given to a nameless deity worshipped in ancient Greece, especially in Athens, alongside the twelve major gods and countless lesser ones. It matters because Paul's visit to Athens made it a point of contact between Greek religion and Christianity.

The myth

According to a story recorded by Diogenes Laertius, Athens was struck by a plague, and its people sought to appease the gods with the right sacrifices. The sage Epimenides gathered a flock of sheep on the Areopagus and released them. Wherever a sheep lay down, an altar was raised to the local god of that place. But at least one sheep led the people to a spot that no god claimed, and there an altar was built with no name inscribed on it.

Christian tradition gives another version. According to the Acts of the Apostles, Paul saw an altar to this god in Athens and, speaking to the Athenian elite at the Areopagus, used it to announce the God of the Bible. To Athenians of his time, a nameless god could be seen as the unknown god par excellence.

Worship and religion

Athens had a temple dedicated specifically to this god, and Athenians swore "in the name of the Unknown God". Philostratus the Younger and Pausanias mention altars in the city. In the modern interpretation of Eduard Norden, it was not a specific deity but a way of honouring a power whose name and nature were unknown.

Legacy

The altar on the Palatine Hill in Rome, unearthed in 1820, is dedicated to an unnamed deity and is displayed in the Palatine Museum. In philosophy, Hegel drew on Paul's episode to reflect on whether God can be known, and in Plotinus' theology the One is likewise unknowable.

Ancient sources: Diogenes Laertius recounts the origin of Epimenides' altars, Philostratus the Younger and Pausanias mention altars in Athens, and the Acts of the Apostles in the New Testament records Paul's speech.

Trivia

Athenian oath. Athenians swore by the formula "in the name of the Unknown God", a sign of how common this cult was in everyday life.
Altar on the Palatine. An altar with a Latin inscription dedicated to a nameless deity was found in 1820 on the Palatine Hill in Rome.
