monstrous primordial creature of Greek mythology

Typhon

Typhon was a monstrous, serpentine giant and one of the deadliest creatures in Greek mythology. According to Hesiod he was the son of Gaia and Tartarus, and he challenged Zeus for control of the cosmos; his offspring with Echidna included many of the best-known monsters of the Greek world.

The myth

According to Hesiod's Theogony, Typhon was born to Gaia and Tartarus after Zeus drove the Titans from heaven. Other accounts make him the son of Gaia alone, or of Hera alone: in the Homeric Hymn to Apollo, Hera, angry that Zeus had given birth to Athena without a woman's help, prayed to Gaia, Uranus and the Titans, struck the ground and conceived a son stronger than Zeus. She gave the infant to the serpent Python to raise. Tradition places his birth and lair in Cilicia, near the Corycian cave, or in the land of the Arimoi.

His appearance was terrifying. He had countless heads, arms reaching east and west, and wings. His head brushed the stars, and from his shoulders and thighs sprang dragon heads and coils of hissing vipers. Fire flashed from his eyes, and his voice could be that of a bull, a lion or a serpent. Nonnus of Panopolis later made him even more venomous, with leopard, lion, bull and boar heads.

Typhon challenged Zeus for the rule of the world. The battle, the Typhonomachy, was cataclysmic: the sea boiled, the earth groaned and Olympus trembled. Zeus won with his thunderbolts. Depending on the version, Typhon was cast into Tartarus or imprisoned beneath Mount Etna, whose eruptions were blamed on his breath and movements. Other sources place him under the island of Ischia.

Legacy

The name Typhon gave rise to the English word "typhoon", through the Latin typhon, meaning a whirlwind or cyclone. The Greeks linked him with the winds of storms and with volcanic activity. His story is connected with that of the serpent Python, killed by Apollo, and with Zeus's succession to power. He was also identified with the Egyptian god of destruction, Set.

Ancient sources: Hesiod's Theogony describes his battle with Zeus; the Homeric Hymn to Apollo tells of his birth; Pindar, Aeschylus, Apollodorus and Nonnus describe him in detail.
