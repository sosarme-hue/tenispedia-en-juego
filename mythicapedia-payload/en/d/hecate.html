Greek goddess of crossroads, night and magic

Hecate

Hecate is a Greek goddess of crossroads, night, magic and the Moon, a deity who stands at the edges of the Greek pantheon. Her origins may lie outside the Greek world, and her protective role in the household makes her a complex figure whose image as a witch has lasted into modern times.

The myth

Hecate has no myth of her own; she is understood mainly through the functions she performs in the stories where she appears. Hesiod's Theogony presents her as an ancient goddess with power over earth, sea and sky, and the Homeric Hymn to Demeter also mentions her. According to Hesiod she is the daughter of Perses and Asteria, though later authors make her a Titan and the Neoplatonists count her among the Olympians.

She also appears on the frieze of the Lagina sanctuary, where she helps protect the newborn Zeus from his father Cronus, and on the great Pergamon frieze, fighting the Titans. Her retinue includes dogs, the empusae and the Lampades nymphs, and Roman writers portrayed her as nocturnal and infernal. She was said to send the ghosts of the dead, and she was called Brimo after the roar she gave when someone tried to violate her.

Worship and religion

Hecate was especially worshipped in Boeotia and Aegina, and her most famous sanctuary stood at Lagina in Caria. The oldest archaeological evidence comes from Selinunte in Sicily, where a temple, perhaps hers, was built around 570 BCE. At Miletus her inscriptions present her as a guardian of doorways, and in the rites of the Milesian molpoi a decorated stone was bathed in libations before her statue. In Athens she was one of the minor goddesses of the household. Offerings were made at crossroads, where victims were slaughtered every new moon, and dogs were sacrificed to her in Thrace, Samothrace, Colophon and Athens. Her three-formed statues, called hekataia, stood at crossroads and at the entrances of temples and homes.

Legacy

Her figure reached Western culture largely through the Mythologiae of Natalis Comes, a Renaissance compilation that reinterpreted her traditions and identified her with Proserpina and Luna. John Skelton evoked her in a verse naming Diana and Persephone alongside her. In English her name was spelled Hecat after Arthur Golding's 1567 translation of Ovid, and it was often pronounced in two syllables into the nineteenth century. Lucan had already linked her with the witches of Thessaly, a connection that lingered in later imagination.

Ancient sources: Her earliest surviving references are in Hesiod's Theogony and the Homeric Hymn to Demeter; Lucan and Ovid also mention her.

Trivia

English spelling. The spelling Hecat comes from Arthur Golding's 1567 translation of Ovid, and for centuries the name was pronounced in two syllables.
Sacred dogs. A fragment of Euripides describes dogs as her sacred animal, and she was said to have tamed them with magic arts.
