bodhisattva of compassion in Mahayana Buddhism

Avalokiteśvara

Avalokiteśvara, "the Lord who looks down", is the bodhisattva associated with great compassion and one of the most widely venerated figures in Mahayana Buddhism. In East Asia he became Guanyin, often a female figure, and in Tibet he is Chenrezig, whose manifestations are linked to the Dalai Lama.

The myth

According to Mahayana doctrine, Avalokiteśvara vowed to hear the pleas of all beings in times of trouble and to postpone his own buddhahood until every being on earth had reached nirvana. The texts present him as a bodhisattva who hears the cries of the world and comes to the aid of those who call on him with devotion. Chapter 25 of the Lotus Sutra is devoted to him and describes thirty-three manifestations suited to the needs of different beings.

A well-known legend tells that, exhausted from trying to free all beings from samsara, his head split into eleven parts. Amitābha gave him eleven heads to hear the cries of the suffering, and when his arms shattered in his effort to reach everyone, he was given a thousand arms. Many Himalayan versions give him eight arms that hold the dharma.

The name admits different readings. Sanskrit tradition understands it as "the lord who looks down", but recent research suggests the original form was Avalokitasvara, "the one who perceives sound", meaning the cries of beings, a reading preserved in the Chinese name Guanyin.

Worship and religion

In Tibetan Buddhism he is a central deity and is regarded as a buddha. The Gelug school identifies the Dalai Lama as his primary earthly manifestation, and the Karma Kagyu school identifies the Karmapa in this way. According to a Tibetan source, Amitābha entrusted him with the care of Tibet, so he also appears as kings and ministers. In China, the cult of the eighteen-armed Cundī was popular in later Buddhist communities, and the female form of Guanyin is widely venerated in Taoism and Chinese folk religion. His worship remains alive across much of Asia.

Legacy

His mantra, "Om mani padme hum", is the most popular mantra in Tibetan Buddhism. In East Asia his female form, Guanyin, is one of the most beloved divinities and appears in temples, paintings and sculptures. In Sri Lanka, where he is called Natha-deva, he is honoured in shrine rooms beside the Buddha image, and in Thailand and Myanmar he is known as Lokesvara or Lokanat.

Ancient sources: He appears in the Lotus Sutra (chapter 25, known as the Avalokiteśvara Sūtra) and in the Heart Sutra, as well as in texts such as the Nīlakaṇṭha Dhāraṇī Sūtra.

Trivia

Two readings of the name. The Sanskrit name is usually translated as "the lord who looks down", but some scholars believe the original form, Avalokitasvara, meant "the one who perceives sound".
A buddha who appears as a bodhisattva. According to the Nīlakaṇṭha Dhāraṇī Sūtra, Avalokiteśvara had already become a buddha countless eons ago, but appears as a bodhisattva out of compassion for others.
