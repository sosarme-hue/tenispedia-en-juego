Roman goddess of hope

Spes

Spes is the Roman goddess who personifies Hope, shown as a young woman holding a cornucopia or a flower. She corresponded to the Greek Elpis and received both private and state worship, with several temples documented in Rome and other cities.

The myth

Ancient texts preserve few stories specific to Spes, since her power lay more in the idea than in narratives. She was understood as a power that comes from the gods, unlike qualities that reside within a person, such as Virtue or Faith.

Tradition calls her the last goddess, following the Latin saying Spes Ultima Dea, which expresses the idea that hope is the last thing to be lost, being the final resource available to human beings.

Worship and religion

During the Republic, a temple to ancient Hope (Spes vetus) was said to stand near the Praenestine Gate, although its existence as anything beyond a private shrine has been doubted. A well-documented temple to Spes and Fides was built by Aulus Atilius Calatinus after vows made during the First Punic War. At Capua, around 110 BC, another was built for the triad of Spes, Fides and Fortuna. At Minturnae, from 70 BC, ceremonies at her shrine were performed by enslaved and freeborn magistri and by ingenui.

Legacy

In the imperial era, Spes was among the personifications of the Virtues in the imperial cult, and she was associated with the emperor Augustus's capacity to secure blessings. Her Greek counterpart, Elpis, appears above all in the myth of Pandora.

Ancient sources: Roman tradition mentions her mainly through inscriptions and records of temples and vows rather than through major literary narratives.

Trivia

A temple by the market. The temple of Spes and Fides built by Calatinus stood beside the vegetable market, just outside the Carmental Gate. It was burned down and restored twice.
Hope in Pandora's jar. The Greek counterpart Elpis is remembered mainly for remaining in Pandora's jar after the other evils escaped. The Greeks held ambivalent views about hope.
