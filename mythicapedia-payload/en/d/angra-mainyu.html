Angra Mainyu

Angra Mainyu, also known as Ahriman, is in Zoroastrianism the destructive evil spirit and the source of every evil in the world. A figure of Persian tradition, he is central to the good-versus-evil dualism that runs through much of Zoroastrian belief.

The myth

In the oldest version of the myth, preserved in the Avestan texts, Angra Mainyu is the twin brother of Spenta Mainyu, the bounteous and creative spirit, and both are sons of Ahura Mazda, the Wise Lord and supreme deity. His name means "destructive spirit". In the Gathas, traditionally attributed to Zoroaster, it is not yet a proper name but a destructive mind set against the bountiful one. The two spirits face a primordial choice that shapes human life and the fate of the world.

In the younger Avesta, Angra Mainyu leads the daevas, whom he deceives and who embody greed, anger and envy. For each of the sixteen lands created by Ahura Mazda, he responds with a scourge or vice. He tries to tempt Zoroaster with sovereignty over the world, but is refused, and the prophet repels his legions of demons. According to the story, creation was good until he corrupted it, above all by staining the pure fire with smoke.

The tradition holds that at the end of time he will be defeated by Ahura Mazda and destroyed by Spenta Mainyu, and his daevas will devour one another until they fade out. In later Zurvanite versions, the two are twins born of Zurvan, and Angra Mainyu tears open the womb to be born first, though his rule is limited to nine thousand years.

Legacy

Persian texts depicted him holding a diamond, a symbol of power over the world that tradition said he lost when Ohrmazd triumphed over him. In spiritualism, Rudolf Steiner, co-founder of anthroposophy, linked him with the urge to achieve things through one's own thinking. In fiction he appears as the main villain of the video game Prince of Persia (2008) and the Mexican film Santo contra el Hacha Diabólica, and as a sorcerer in the Warhammer 40,000 franchise.

Ancient sources: The Gathas, the Yasna, the Vendidad and the Yashts of the Avesta, along with Pahlavi texts such as the Bundahishn.
