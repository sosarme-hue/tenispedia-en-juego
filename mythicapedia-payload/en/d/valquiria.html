Norse maiden who chooses the slain in battle

Valkyrie

Valkyries are female figures in Norse mythology who choose the warriors slain in battle and guide them to Valhalla, the hall of Odin. Their name means 'chooser of the slain', which describes their role well.

The myth

According to the stories, valkyries served Odin and rode over battlefields deciding who would die and who would survive. The chosen dead became einherjar, warriors who would fight beside the gods at Ragnarök, the final battle of the world. When they were not preparing for that day, valkyries served mead and ale in Valhalla. In the Prose Edda, Gunnr, Róta and the younger norn Skuld are said to ride constantly to choose who must die.

Sources disagree on their number and origin. The Völuspá names six valkyries, while the Grímnismál lists eleven more, among them Skögul, Hildr and Reginleif. The Poetic Edda describes them as supernatural beings of unknown ancestry, but heroic lays present them as bands of warrior women led by a chief, almost always a great king's daughter. Sigrún, who marries the hero Helgi Hundingsbane, is one example; Brynhildr, the princess of the Volsunga saga, is another. Valkyries also appear as lovers of heroes and mortals, and are sometimes linked to the norns, with whom they share a connection to fate.

Their animal companions were not always winged horses, as modern art often suggests. A Scandinavian kenning called the wolf the 'valkyrie's horse', and valkyries are often compared to ravens circling the battlefield.

Worship and religion

Modern scholars think the earliest valkyries may have been priestesses of Odin who carried out ritual sacrifices of prisoners, described as being 'sent to Odin'. Over time these rites gave rise to legends of supernatural maidens. The sources describe no temples or festivals dedicated to them, and their worship did not survive the Christianization of Scandinavia.

Legacy

Richard Wagner made them famous in his opera Die Walküre (The Valkyrie), the second part of Der Ring des Nibelungen. Thomas Bulfinch, in Bulfinch's Mythology (1855), claimed their armour caused the aurora borealis, an idea no other source supports. Today valkyries appear in art, music, comic books, video games and poetry. The German Walküre and the Old English wælcyrige share the same roots as the Norse name.

Ancient sources: The Poetic Edda (Völuspá, Grímnismál, Helgakviða Hundingsbana I), the Prose Edda of Snorri Sturluson and the Volsunga saga, compiled in the 13th century from older traditions.

Trivia

Valkyrie's horse. A Scandinavian kenning used the phrase 'valkyrie's horse' as a poetic way to refer to the wolf, suggesting these figures were linked to wolves rather than horses.
Thrud, daughter of Thor. Among the valkyries named in the sources is Thrud, who is the daughter of Thor. Her name means 'power'.
