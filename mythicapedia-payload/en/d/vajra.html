Vajra

The vajra is a mythical weapon in Hinduism, most closely associated with Indra, king of the gods, and a ritual symbol shared by Hinduism, Buddhism and Jainism. Its name means both 'diamond' and 'thunderbolt', embodying indestructibility and irresistible force.

The myth

In the Vedic tradition, the vajra was the weapon Indra used to slay the serpent-like asura Vritra, who had held back the waters. The Rigveda describes it as a disc-shaped weapon crafted for Indra by Tvaṣṭṛ.

Later Puranas expand the story. Vritra had received a boon that protected him from any known weapon, so only one made from the bones of the sage Dadhichi could defeat him. Dadhichi gave up his life, and the gods fashioned the vajra from his spine. In another version, the sage had guarded the gods' weapons, dissolved them in sacred water and drank it, so his bones became the weapon. In the Ramayana, Indra strikes the young Hanuman's chin with the vajra after the boy tries to swallow the sun.

Worship and religion

In Hinduism the vajra is a ritual weapon and symbol. In Tibetan Buddhism it is used together with a bell in recitation rituals. In Tibet, Bhutan and Nepal it is known as the dorje and takes the form of a bell-like object. It still appears in the iconography of many Buddhist deities, and its use in practice continues today.

Legacy

The term gave its name to Vajrayana, or the 'Thunderbolt Way' or 'Diamond Way', one of the three major branches of Buddhism, most associated with Tibet. In tantric tradition the vajra stands for skillful means, while the bell represents wisdom.

Ancient sources: The vajra appears in the Rigveda, the oldest Vedic text, and in the Puranas and the Ramayana.

Trivia

Double meaning. In Sanskrit, vajra means both 'diamond' and 'thunderbolt', which explains its double symbolism of hardness and force.
Lightning from the sky. Lightning bolts falling from the sky were also called vajra, as they were believed to come from Indra's hurled weapon.
