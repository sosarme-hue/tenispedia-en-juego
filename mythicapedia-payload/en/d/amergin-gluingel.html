Milesian bard and druid of Irish mythology

Amergin Glúingel

Amergin Glúingel is a bard, druid and judge of the Milesians in the Irish Mythological Cycle, to whom several poems are attributed. He matters because, in the tradition, he took part in the conquest of Ireland, and his invocation song has become one of the most quoted texts in Irish poetry.

The myth

According to the legend, Amergin was one of the seven sons of Míl Espáine. The Milesians came to Ireland to avenge their great-uncle Íth, treacherously killed by the three kings of the Tuatha Dé Danann. At the estuary of Inber Scéne, named after his wife Scéne, who had died at sea, the three queens Banba, Ériu and Fódla granted permission to settle, and each asked that the island bear her name. Ériu gave rise to the name Éire, while Banba and Fódla became poetic names for Ireland.

Before the battle, Amergin acted as an impartial judge and set the rules of engagement. When the druids of the Tuatha Dé Danann raised a storm to stop the landing, Amergin sang an invocation to the spirit of Ireland, known as The Song of Amergin, and the storm parted. The three kings were killed in single combat by three sons of Míl, Amergin among them. He then divided the island with his brothers, but Érimón defeated Éber and ruled all of Ireland, and soon after killed Amergin in battle. Local tradition in Drogheda places his burial under Millmount.

Legacy

The Song of Amergin, translated by Lady Augusta Gregory in 1904 in Gods and Fighting Men, is one of the best-known pieces of Irish mythological poetry. Some early medieval Welsh poems attributed to Taliesin share themes with those attributed to Amergin.

Trivia

Name of the island. According to the legend, the three queens asked that the island bear their names. Ériu is the origin of Éire, while Banba and Fódla remain poetic names for Ireland, much as Albion does for Great Britain.
A magical boundary. The Milesians agreed to withdraw beyond the ninth wave, a magical boundary of the ocean.
