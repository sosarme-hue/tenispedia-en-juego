presence that frightened horses at Greek hippodromes

Taraxippos

In Greek mythology, the taraxippos was an unseen presence blamed for panicking horses at hippodromes throughout Greece. Rather than a single being, it may have been an epithet or a dangerous place, depending on the ancient author consulted.

The myth

Tradition attributed the danger of the racetrack to ghosts, daimones or ill-omened sites. The most famous was the Taraxippos Olympios at Olympia. Pausanias, who records more explanations than any other ancient author, regards it as an epithet rather than a single entity.

According to some accounts, the terror at Olympia was the ghost of Oenomaus, who had killed the suitors of Hippodamia. Others spoke of the ghost of Myrtilus, the charioteer who caused Oenomaus's death, or of the tomb of the earth-born giant Ischenus. At the Isthmian Games it was said to be the ghost of Glaucus of Potnia, torn apart by his own horses.

Worship and religion

Some taraxippoi were linked to hero cults or to Poseidon Hippios, god of horses, who brought about the death of Hippolytus. A widely held modern explanation is that racecourses often stood over tombs or altars, and the sharp turns of the track were the most likely place for crashes.

Legacy

The name survives in language and art as a symbol of horses' fear. The comic playwright Aristophanes joked in The Knights, calling Cleon Taraxippostratus, "disturber of the horse troops".

Ancient sources: Pausanias is the ancient source offering the greatest number of explanations for the taraxippoi.

Trivia

An epithet, not a creature. Pausanias regarded the taraxippos as an epithet rather than a single entity, which explains the variety of explanations.
A political insult. Aristophanes called Cleon Taraxippostratus in The Knights, a joke aimed at the Athenian politician.
