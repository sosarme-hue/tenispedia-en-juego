lord of the underworld of Bolivian mines

El Tío

El Tío is the lord of the underworld and protector of the mines in Bolivian mining folklore, above all at Cerro Rico in Potosí. Blending Andean beliefs with traits of the Christian devil, he remains a living presence for many miners.

The myth

In mining tradition, El Tío rules the sótano, the underground world, and offers miners protection or ruin depending on how he is treated. He is usually pictured as a horned devil with bulging eyes, sometimes in the form of a goat. His roots lie in Andean reverence for the huacas and nature deities, and colonial-era influences gave him traits of the Catholic devil.

Some accounts hold that the Spaniards gave him devilish features to discourage Indigenous worship. Another explanation says his name comes from Spanish «Dios», which came out as «Tío» because Quechua and Aymara have no D sound. According to researcher Víctor Montoya, his wife is Chinasupay, the she-devil.

Worship and religion

Miners leave him cigarettes, coca leaves and liquor, and in more recent times white rum on Tuesdays and Fridays. On Carnival Tuesday they pour chicha on the ground and adorn his statue with confetti. Llamas or alpacas were once sacrificed and their blood splashed at mine entrances; today visitors perform the challa ritual with materials bought in witches' markets. Many miners are Catholic yet keep venerating him, believing God and El Tío rule separate realms.

Legacy

El Tío appears in the documentary The Devil's Miner and in the diablada dance of the Oruro Carnival, where the dancers playing Lucifer are understood to represent him.

Ancient sources: He is mentioned in colonial-era texts, though the sources name no specific works.

Trivia

A name with history. The name «Tío» may derive from Spanish «Dios», since Quechua and Aymara have no D sound.
A hellish couple. Víctor Montoya compares El Tío to Hades and his wife Chinasupay to Persephone.
