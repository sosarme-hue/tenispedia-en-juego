Roman personification of eternity

Aeternitas

Aeternitas was the divine personification of eternity in Roman religion. Her cult was closely tied to the deified emperor and to the idea of a rule without end.

The myth

Ancient texts hardly present her as the protagonist of any story, so her figure is known mainly through her iconography. Martianus Capella counts her among the most honoured daughters of Jupiter and mentions her diadem, whose circular shape represents eternity.

On coins her image grew richer. Under Vespasian (75–79 AD) she holds a head of the Sun and another of the Moon. On the coins of Titus (80–81 AD) she carries a cornucopia, leans on a sceptre and rests one foot on a globe, linking eternity, prosperity and world dominion. From the 2nd to the mid-3rd century her iconography also includes the phoenix, a symbol of cyclical time, which was reborn from its flames every 500 years.

Worship and religion

Like other abstract deities, she was honoured with sacrifices and temples both in Rome and in the provinces. At Tarraco in Roman Spain there was a temple of Aeternitas Augusta, which appears on a coin. As Aeternitas Imperii, she received sacrifices from the Arval Brethren in thanksgiving when Nero survived a conspiracy and an assassination attempt. Her cult formed part of official Roman worship from the Julio-Claudians to the Severans.

Legacy

Her idea survives in language through imperium, the Latin word from which the English word empire derives. Her male counterpart, Aion, god of unbound time, the celestial spheres and the Zodiac, belongs to the same family of ideas. Boccaccio read her as an absolute principle containing all time and governing the course of the stars, and borrowed from Claudian the image of a dragon biting its own tail to describe the circular flow of time.

Ancient sources: Martianus Capella, in The Marriage of Philology and Mercury, and Claudian, in his heroic poem on Stilicho.

Trivia

The phoenix as symbol. The phoenix appears in Aeternitas's iconography because, according to tradition, it was reborn from its flames every 500 years, which links it to cyclical time.
A temple in Hispania. A temple dedicated to Aeternitas Augusta stood in Tarraco, in Roman Spain, and is known because it is shown on a coin.
