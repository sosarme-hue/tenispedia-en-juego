hybrid creature from Persian mythology

Manticore

The manticore is a hybrid creature from Persian mythology, with a human head, a lion's body and a scorpion's tail or a tail covered in venomous spines. Its name means "man-eater", and its fame crossed centuries and borders until it became a familiar figure in medieval European bestiaries. According to the stories, the manticore lives in India and feeds on people, devouring them whole and leaving no bones behind. Some versions give it three rows of teeth and a tail that can launch spines like arrows. Its size varies from one author to another: from that of a lion to that of an elephant, and sometimes it has wings or armour. Its literary origin is usually traced to Ctesias, a Greek physician at the court of the Persian king Artaxerxes II, who described it in his lost book Indica, known today only from fragments. According to Ctesias, his Persian-speaking informants said that the Indians captured the cubs and crushed their tails with a stone before the spines could grow. Aelian added that the beast preferred to hunt humans and could attack two or three at once. Pliny the Elder included it in his Naturalis Historia as a real animal and also placed it in Ethiopia, mixing it with other beasts. Pausanias, by contrast, suspected that the account exaggerated the description of an Indian tiger. Over time, Christian tradition and the bestiaries turned it into a symbol of tyranny, oppression and envy and, finally, into an embodiment of evil.

The myth

Legacy

The manticore appears often in medieval bestiaries, where illustrators drew it with a thick mane, a beard, a cat's tail and sometimes a blue body. In 1607 Edward Topsell described it in his Historie of Four-Footed Beastes and repeated the story of the crushed tail, with some changes. The name also entered heraldry, where related terms such as manticore, mantygre and satyral were used from the late Middle Ages into modern times.

Ancient sources: Ctesias in his Indica (5th century BCE, preserved in fragments), Aelian in De Natura Animalium and Pliny the Elder in his Naturalis Historia.

Trivia

A misspelt name. In some copies of Aristotle the name appears as "mantichoras", a mistaken spelling that ended up becoming standard in Europe.
Blue or blonde. Some medieval illustrators painted the manticore blue or gave it blonde hair, moving away from the original description.
