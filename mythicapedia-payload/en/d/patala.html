subterranean realms in Indian religions

Patala

Patala is, in Indian religions, the set of subterranean realms lying beneath the earth, often rendered as the underworld. Its name means «that which is below the feet», and it is described as more beautiful than Svarga, the heavens.

The myth

Texts portray Patala as a place full of splendid jewels, groves, lakes and soil of many colours, including gold. The Vishnu Purana tells how the wandering sage Narada visited it and was struck by its beauty. There is no sunlight, yet the jewels worn by its residents light the darkness, and there is no old age, sweat or disease.

According to the Vishnu Purana, seven realms lie one above another, seventy thousand yojanas below the earth's surface, and their names vary between texts: Atala, Vitala, Sutala, Talatala, Mahatala, Rasatala and Patala. Each has its ruler. Bala, a son of Maya, reigns in Atala; Hara-Bhava, with his consort Bhavani, in Vitala; the asura king Bali in Sutala, whom Vishnu, in his dwarf form Vamana, sent there after taking the three worlds, though he later granted Bali the role of guardian of his palace. Maya dwells in Talatala, and the Nagas, headed by Vasuki, occupy the lowest region. Beneath them lies Naraka, the Hindu hell. Tradition adds that the earth and the seven realms rest on Shesha, the thousand-headed serpent.

Worship and religion

In early Vajrayana Buddhism, Patala was understood as a set of underground paradises inhabited by nagas and asuras. Caves inhabited by asuras were its entrances, and masters such as Padmasambhava «tamed» these beings, especially the females, converting them into dharmapalas or dakinis. It was also linked to water magic and to attaining the status of vidyadhara, practices that lost prominence after the early period of Tibetan Buddhism.

Ancient sources: Vishnu Purana, Bhagavata Purana, Padma Purana, Shiva Purana, Vayu Purana and Devi-Bhagavata Purana.

Trivia

Seven names, many lists. The Puranas do not agree on the names of the seven realms. The Shiva Purana replaces Mahatala with Tala, and the Vayu Purana uses different names such as Gabhastala or Sritala.
An unexpected guardian. According to the Vishnu Purana, Vishnu promised Bali he would stand as watchman of his palace in Sutala, turning a defeated king into a devotee.
