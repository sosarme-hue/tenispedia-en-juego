giant of Greek mythology

Mimas

Mimas was one of the Giants of Greek mythology, a child of Gaia who, in some accounts, was born from the blood of the castrated Uranus. He belongs to the Gigantomachy, the cosmic battle between the Giants and the Olympian gods, and his name later went to one of Saturn's moons.

The myth

According to the myth, Mimas was born of Gaia after Uranus was castrated, and he fought in the Gigantomachy against the Twelve Olympians. Accounts of his death differ. Apollodorus says Hephaestus struck him down with red-hot missiles from his forge, and that he was buried under a mass of molten iron.

Other writers have him killed by Ares: Apollonius of Rhodes in the Argonautica and Claudian in his Gigantomachia, where the god is Mars, his Roman counterpart. In Euripides's Ion, the chorus describes a scene on the Temple of Apollo at Delphi in which Zeus reduces Mimas to ashes with his thunderbolt.

Tradition placed his body beneath Prochyte, one of the Phlegraean Islands off the coast of Naples. Horace and Seneca mention him alongside other Giants, and Claudian recalls his weapons hanging in a wood near Mount Etna as spoils of war.

Legacy

In 1847 the Giant gave his name to the moon of Saturn closest to the planet, Mimas. He appears on Attic vase paintings of the Gigantomachy, sometimes under a name that may be a misspelling, and a Giant on the frieze of the Siphnian Treasury at Delphi is possibly the same figure.

Ancient sources: Apollodorus, Euripides (Ion), Apollonius of Rhodes (Argonautica), Claudian, Horace and Seneca.

Trivia

A moon of Saturn. In 1847 his name was chosen for the moon of Saturn closest to the planet, one of the best-known bodies in the Solar System.
A volcanic island. Tradition placed his body under Prochyte, one of the Phlegraean Islands near Naples, an area of intense volcanic activity.
