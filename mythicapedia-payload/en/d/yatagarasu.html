three-legged guiding crow of Shinto mythology

Yatagarasu

Yatagarasu is a mythical three-legged crow and a guiding god in Japanese Shinto mythology. His image has been passed down since ancient times as a symbol of guidance and of divine intervention in human affairs.

The myth

According to the story, the crow was sent from heaven to guide Emperor Jimmu on his expedition from the Kumano region to Yamato, where he established his court at Kashihara. The Kojiki says Takamimusubi sent the bird, while the Nihon Shoki credits Amaterasu. Steep mountains and difficult paths forced the army to change course, and the crow showed the way inland.

After guiding the emperor, the crow served as his messenger to local enemies and tried to persuade rebellious brothers to submit. The two chronicles tell this episode differently, and the outcome varies between them. Tradition also links the bird to the Kamo family, whose members were the first chief priests of the Kamigamo shrine.

The three legs do not appear in the Kojiki or the Nihon Shoki. The earliest reference is the Wamyō Ruijushō, from around 930, when the Japanese crow was merged with the Chinese sun crow, a three-legged bird that lived in the sun.

Worship and religion

Yatagarasu is venerated mainly at the Kumano shrines, where he is regarded as a servant of Susanoo and a symbol of the region. Every year on 7 January, the Hoin Shinji ceremony at Kumano Hongu Taisha distributes seals printed with his face. Popular belief holds that whoever burns one of these seals and swallows the ashes must tell the truth, or else suffer serious harm. These celebrations continue today.

Legacy

His image appears in many works of Japanese and global popular culture, including the manga and anime Gintama, the Shin Megami Tensei franchise and its spin-off Persona, and the manga One Piece, where Zoro uses an attack with that name. The Digimon Crowmon also derives from him. He appears in the Yu-Gi-Oh! card game and the video game Geometry Dash.

Ancient sources: He is recorded in the Kojiki, the Nihon Shoki, and the Shinsen Shōjiroku, as well as in early artworks.

Trivia

Late three legs. Neither the Kojiki nor the Nihon Shoki says he has three legs. The first reference to that figure appears in the Wamyō Ruijushō, written around 930.
A name with two readings. It has been read as “eight-span crow,” but here “yata” simply means “large.”
