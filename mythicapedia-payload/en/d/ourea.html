mountain personifications in Greek mythology

Ourea

The Ourea are, in Greek mythology, the personifications of mountains, offspring of Gaia and siblings of Uranus and Pontus. They appear mainly in Hesiod as a primordial collective and, in other authors, as a backdrop or even as judges and rivals.

The myth

According to Hesiod's Theogony, the Ourea were born from Gaia, Earth, alongside Uranus, Sky, and Pontus, Sea, in the earliest generations of the cosmos. Hesiod gives them no individual names: they are great mountains, home to goddesses and the forest nymphs.

Apollonius of Rhodes includes them in his account of the separation of earth, sky and sea, as inert elements. Ovid, by contrast, tells how Typhon hurled fire over famous peaks such as Athos, Etna and the Caucasus, which burned along with the forests. Medea, in her incantation, also calls on the mountains among the primordial forces.

Some mountains have their own voice. According to Ovid, Tmolus judged a musical contest between Pan and Apollo before King Midas and gave the victory to the god of the lyre. Corinna tells how Cithaeron defeated Helicon in a singing contest before the Muses, and how the defeated Helicon tore off a rock and shattered it into ten thousand pieces.

Legacy

In Imperial Roman art, some bearded male figures have been identified as mountains, although the LIMC warns that these attributions are hypothetical and lack a stable iconographic canon.

Ancient sources: Hesiod's Theogony, Apollonius of Rhodes, Ovid, Corinna and Theocritus, among other Greek and Latin authors.

Trivia

A defeated mountain. In Corinna's account, Helicon, defeated in the singing contest, tears off a rock and smashes it into ten thousand stones.
Mountains in a spell. Medea calls upon the Earth, the Night and the mountains, among other forces, to empower her magic.
A smiling Olympus. Theocritus describes how Olympus rejoices at the birth of Hermes and seems to smile like a human.
