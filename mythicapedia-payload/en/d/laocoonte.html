Trojan priest of Greek mythology

Laocoön

Laocoön was a Trojan priest in Greek and Roman mythology, best known for his death alongside his sons at the hands of two sea serpents. His story, above all Virgil's version in the Aeneid, is one of the most famous episodes of the Trojan War and has inspired sculpture, painting and aesthetic debate for centuries.

The myth

According to tradition, Laocoön was the son of Capys, though other authors name Acoetes or Antenor as his father. He was a brother of Anchises and therefore an uncle of Aeneas. He served as a priest of Apollo Thymbraeus, but Hyginus tells that the god had forbidden him to marry and have children. Laocoön defied the god, married Antiope and had two sons. Euphorion of Chalcis adds that he had intercourse with his wife inside the sanctuary, in front of the cult statue, and was punished for it.

In the account of Arctinus of Miletus, the oldest surviving version, two serpents rise from the sea during a celebration and kill Laocoön and one of his sons. Aeneas reads the event as a dire omen and leaves the city. Sophocles wrote a lost tragedy on the subject, in which the serpents kill both sons and leave the father alive to suffer.

The best-known version is Virgil's. While Laocoön sacrifices a bull to Neptune on the shore, he warns the Trojans about the trap with the words Timeo Danaos et dona ferentes and hurls his spear at the wooden horse. Soon after, two serpents arriving from Tenedos kill his sons and then him. The Trojans take his death as punishment for attacking a gift to the goddess, and they bring the horse into the city. Quintus Smyrnaeus gives a different version: Athena blinds him with earthquakes and then sends the serpents.

Worship and religion

According to the sources, Laocoön served as a priest of Apollo Thymbraeus or, in other versions, of Poseidon. In some accounts he offers sacrifices to the sea god on the coast, and in others he is chosen by lot for that role. The sources do not describe a cult devoted to him.

Legacy

The Hellenistic sculptural group Laocoön and His Sons, attributed to Agesander, Polydorus and Athanadoros, was excavated in Rome in 1506 and acquired for Pope Julius II. It is now in the Vatican Museums. The right arm was missing and was restored in an outstretched position, but when the original arm was found in 1957 it was bent, as Michelangelo had proposed. The work became a reference point in eighteenth-century aesthetic theory, especially for Lessing. Around 1609 El Greco painted his own version, now in the National Gallery of Art in Washington. The asteroid 3240 is named Laocoon after him.

Ancient sources: Arctinus of Miletus in the Iliupersis (7th century BC), Bacchylides, Sophocles in his lost tragedy Laocoön, Virgil in the Aeneid, and Quintus Smyrnaeus in the Posthomerica.

Trivia

A famous phrase. The warning Timeo Danaos et dona ferentes comes from Virgil's Aeneid and is still quoted as an example of distrust toward gifts.
