Japanese god of wealth and longevity

Fukurokuju

Fukurokuju is one of the Seven Lucky Gods of Japanese folklore, associated with wisdom and long life. His figure probably derives from an old Chinese Taoist sage, and his origins blend Chinese and Japanese traditions.

The myth

According to tradition, before reaching divinity he was a Chinese hermit of the Northern Song period (960-1127), renowned for his miracles. Some accounts consider him a reincarnation of the Taoist deity Xuantian Shangdi, and say that during his human life he was a sennin, an immortal who could live without eating.

His image is linked to that of the Chinese longevity god Shou, and in China he was thought to embody the celestial powers of the southern polar star. He is usually shown bald, with long whiskers and an elongated forehead. Some scholars propose that Japan merged the older Chinese Three Star Gods into a single deity.

Fukurokuju was not always among the Seven. In the earliest Japanese depictions he was replaced by Kisshōten, goddess of fortune, beauty and merit, but he later became a settled member of the group. He is often confused with Jurōjin, another god of fortune, who in some accounts is his grandson and in others shares his body. He is also credited with the power to resurrect the dead, a distinction no other of the Seven holds.

Legacy

He frequently appears in Japanese popular art, such as depictions of the Seven Lucky Gods. His usual symbols, the staff with a sacred book tied to it, the crane, the turtle and the black deer, are good-luck motifs linked to longevity.
