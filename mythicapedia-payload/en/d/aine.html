Irish goddess of summer, wealth and sovereignty

Áine

Áine is an Irish goddess linked to summer, fertility, wealth and sovereignty, and one of the best-known figures in the folklore of County Limerick and Munster. She is associated with the moon, with midsummer and the sun, and with the feast of Saint John's Eve.

The myth

Accounts of her parentage disagree. Some tales make her the daughter of the sea god Manannán mac Lir, others name Egobail as her father, and some claim she was a granddaughter of Donn of Uisneach. Her best-known story tells how Ailill Aulom raped her, and she took revenge by cutting off one of his ears. Having lost an ear, he could no longer hold the lordship of Munster, and he killed her with his sword. She was buried at Cnoc Áine, near Lough Gur. Tradition holds that the Eóganachta, the royal dynasties of Munster, descend from her.

In Limerick folklore she appears as a woman who lives beneath the waters of a lake, combing her long golden hair with a golden comb, and she is connected with the Sidhe. In the legend of Seán Ó hAodh, a piper is taken to her mansion beneath Lough Gur, and she gives him a gold purse that never runs empty.

Worship and religion

On Saint John's Eve, men at Cnoc Áine in County Limerick lit clíars, bunches of straw and hay tied to poles, which were carried in procession to the hilltop. They then ran with them through the fields and between the cattle to bring good luck. Rites of fire and blessing of the land at the site were recorded as recently as 1879. There is no reliable evidence that this cult survives today.

Legacy

The FitzGerald family, of Norman origin, claimed a connection with Áine, and in their stories Gerald FitzGerald is born of her rape and transforms into a goose at Lough Gur. The goddess's name, meaning brightness or joy in Irish, gave its name to the hill of Knockainey, and meadowsweet is said to be her plant.

Trivia

A plant of her own. Meadowsweet, also called queen-of-the-meadow, is said to be her plant. It is one of the few concrete associations the popular tradition records for her.
Confusion with other goddesses. She is sometimes confused with Morrigan or Dana, and her name has been mistakenly equated with Danu because of a superficial resemblance.
