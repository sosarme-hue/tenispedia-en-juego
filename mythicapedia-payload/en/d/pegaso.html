winged horse of Greek mythology

Pegasus

Pegasus is the winged horse of Greek mythology, usually described as the offspring of Medusa and, in the most common account, of Poseidon. His story, tied to the heroes Perseus and Bellerophon, made him one of the most recognisable figures of ancient myth and of later art.

The myth

According to the tradition, Pegasus and his brother Chrysaor were born from the blood of Medusa when Perseus beheaded her. Hesiod places their birth by the springs of Oceanus, at the edge of the world, and links the horse's name to the Greek word for spring. Other versions have him sprung from the earth wet with the Gorgon's blood, or formed from blood, pain and sea foam, which points to Poseidon as his father. From the start he is associated with thunder and lightning: Hesiod says he carried Zeus's thunderbolts, and some accounts say the god brought him to Olympus, where he was stabled with other horses and served alongside the attendants Astrape and Bronte.

His best-known episode involves Bellerophon. In the most common version, the hero received a golden bridle from Athena in a dream while sleeping in her temple, and at dawn he found Pegasus drinking at the Pierian spring and tamed him. Riding him, Bellerophon defeated the Chimera, a monster that ravaged Lycia, and won further victories. Overcome by ambition, he tried to reach Olympus, but Zeus sent a gadfly that stung the horse, throwing the hero down. Bellerophon was left lame, while Pegasus reached the sky and was placed among the stars. One tradition holds that a feather fell to earth near Tarsus on the day of his transformation.

Hesiod also tells that when the horse struck the ground with his hoof, a spring burst forth: the Hippocrene on Mount Helicon, home of the Muses. For this reason the Muses were also called Pegasides.

Legacy

Pegasus became a common element in British heraldry, usually as a supporter or crest, and appears on the arms of the Inner Temple. During the Second World War, British airborne troops adopted the image of Bellerophon riding Pegasus as their sleeve insignia in 1941. The Pegasus Bridge in Normandy, captured by British airborne forces on D-Day, takes its name from the same emblem. The selection process for the Parachute Regiment is known as Pegasus Company. Tuscany has used the Pegasus as a symbol since the sixteenth century. Pegasus is also a constellation, and he is among the most famous horses in literature.

Ancient sources: Hesiod's Theogony is the earliest source that mentions him, and Pindar and Ovid also recount episodes of his story.

Trivia

A feather at Tarsus. According to one tradition, when Zeus turned Pegasus into a constellation, a single feather fell to earth near the city of Tarsus.
A bridge named after him. Pegasus Bridge, over the Caen Canal, took its name from the emblem of the British airborne troops who captured it on the night of D-Day.
