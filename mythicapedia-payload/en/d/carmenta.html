Roman goddess of childbirth and prophecy

Carmenta

Carmenta, also known as Carmentis, was a Roman goddess of childbirth and prophecy, linked to the invention of the Latin alphabet. Her story is tied to the Arcadian legend of Evander, whose settlement was regarded as a precursor of Rome.

The myth

According to tradition, Carmenta was originally a nymph of Arcadia. Ancient writers disagree on her Greek name: Dionysius of Halicarnassus calls her Themis, while Boccaccio calls her Nicostrata. Both describe a woman inspired by a divine breath who foretold the future in verse. At Rome she took the name Carmenta from her link with the carmina, songs and spells.

Her son Evander is given different parentage: Pausanias and Dionysius make Hermes his father, while Boccaccio makes him a son of Palante. After a violent event in Arcadia, Evander left for Italy with his mother, who had foretold trouble and a great destiny for him. They reached the Palatine and founded Pallantium, later regarded as one of the origins of Rome.

Tradition credits Carmenta with adapting Greek letters for Latin. Hyginus says she altered fifteen letters and that Apollo added the rest. Plutarch also notes that she was regarded as one of the Fates, and that her name may mean "without mind" because of her prophetic frenzy.

Worship and religion

In Rome there was an altar and a small shrine to Carmenta beside the Carmental Gate, at the foot of the Capitoline. Her priest was the flamen carmentalis, one of the lesser flamines, and dead animal skins were forbidden in her temple. Her festival, the Carmentalia, was held in January. According to Plutarch, mothers built her temple and offered sacrifices to her during childbirth.

Legacy

Carmenta's name gave rise to the English word "charm" through the Latin carmen. Boccaccio remembered her in De mulieribus claris, one of the first collections devoted entirely to biographies of women.

Ancient sources: Hyginus, Dionysius of Halicarnassus, Ovid, Plutarch, Strabo and Pausanias mention her in their accounts of Rome and Arcadia.

Trivia

A name with magic. The Latin carmen meant song, spell or oracle, and it is the root of the English word "charm".
A gate named after her. Rome kept a gate called the Carmental Gate for centuries, near the Capitoline, in memory of the goddess.
