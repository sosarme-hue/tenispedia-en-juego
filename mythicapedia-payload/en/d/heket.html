Egyptian goddess of fertility and childbirth

Heqet

Heqet, sometimes spelled Heket, is an Egyptian goddess of fertility who presides over births and assists women in labour. She was shown as a frog or as a woman with a frog's head, and her role is linked to the annual flooding of the Nile.

The myth

In Egyptian tradition, Heqet was the daughter of Ra and the wife of Shu, with whom she bore Nut and Geb. In other accounts she is the companion of Khnum, the god who shaped children's bodies on his potter's wheel. Each morning she attended the birth of the Sun, which is why she was regarded as a protector of pregnant women.

Her most characteristic role was to give the newborn the breath of life by placing the ankh, the sign of life, before its nose, earning her the epithet "She who hastens the birth" in some sources and "She who makes breathe" in others. Over time her role became linked to the myth of Osiris: she breathed life into the body of Horus at birth, and because she helped the resurrection of Osiris, she was also seen as a helper of the dead in their rebirth.

Worship and religion

Her cult is very old, already documented in the Early Dynastic Period, and her name appears in the Pyramid Texts. She was worshipped at places such as Hermopolis Magna, Abydos, Antinoopolis and Apollinopolis Parva, and in the Kom Ombo region she was considered the wife of Sobek-Ra. She was honoured in mammisi, the birth temples, and at Qus a Ptolemaic temple dedicated to Horus and Heqet has been found. Frog amulets were probably worn during childbirth to ensure an easy delivery.

Legacy

Some modern scholars propose that her name may be the origin of that of Hecate, the Greek goddess of witchcraft, although this remains an unconfirmed hypothesis.

Ancient sources: The Pyramid Texts mention her, and she appears in the story of the birth of the royal children in the Westcar Papyrus.

Trivia

A sacred frog. For the Egyptians the frog was an ancient symbol of fertility, linked to the annual flooding of the Nile. Early frog statuettes are often thought to depict the goddess.
A Christian-era amulet. In the Christian era her amulets came to bear the phrase "I am the resurrection", along with cross and lamb symbols.
