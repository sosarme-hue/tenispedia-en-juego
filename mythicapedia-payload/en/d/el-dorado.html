legendary city and kingdom of gold in South America

El Dorado

El Dorado, meaning 'the Golden One', was first the name of a ruler said to be covered in gold, and later of a mythical city or kingdom of immense wealth supposedly hidden somewhere in South America. The story grew out of a ceremony of the Muisca people of the Colombian highlands and became a lasting symbol of fabulous riches and obsessive exploration.

The myth

At the heart of the legend is a rite in which a chief, before taking power, was covered in gold dust and rafted to the middle of Lake Guatavita. There he threw gold objects and emeralds into the water as an offering. According to the chronicles, the lake was home to a deity that appeared as a small serpent or dragon, and at its bottom stood a marvellous temple where a female ruler and her son were said to live.

The same chronicles tell of a tragedy: the chieftess, accused of infidelity by her husband, threw herself into the waters with her son. Over the centuries, the golden rite of the ruler came to stand for an entire kingdom of gold. By the time the Spanish arrived, the ceremony had long been abandoned, but the rumour spread quickly.

In the sixteenth century the story expanded to other places. Indigenous peoples, aware of European greed, may have fed tales of wonderful cities, and the name was applied to almost any fantastical account, whether invented by local people or imagined by the Spanish themselves. Explorers searched first in the highlands and then in the Amazon and the Guianas. Among those who pursued it were Gonzalo Pizarro, Francisco de Orellana, Philipp von Hutten, Pedro de Ursúa, Antonio de Berrío and Walter Raleigh. In the seventeenth century some maps even showed Lake Parime, which later geographers rejected.

Worship and religion

Lake Guatavita was the centre of worship for the deity that appeared as a serpent, and the investiture ceremony took place there before a future chief assumed power. Offerings of gold and emeralds were an essential part of the rite. The practice had ended long before the Spanish arrived, so no living tradition keeps it alive today. Some of the objects looted from the lake are now held in the Museo del Oro in Bogotá.

Legacy

The legend became a symbol of fabulous wealth, utopia and impossible quests. Voltaire included it in his novel Candide, Edgar Allan Poe wrote the poem 'Eldorado', and later films, video games and adventure novels returned to it again and again.

Trivia

The Museo del Oro raft. The Muisca raft, linked to the El Dorado tradition, is now displayed at the Museo del Oro in Bogotá, alongside other objects recovered from Lake Guatavita.
A lake that never existed. Lake Parime appeared on seventeenth-century maps, but later explorers and geographers eventually rejected its existence.
