Japanese tall woman yokai

Taka-Onna

The Taka-Onna is a Japanese yokai, a jealous and ugly woman who wanders through houses and can stretch her body at will. It matters because it blends a tale of domestic revenge with a figure that later literature reinterpreted as a symbol of frustrated desire.

The myth

According to a tale from Wakayama Prefecture, a prosperous carpenter with a wife and a young son employed around thirty servants. When the boy was five he vanished without explanation, and afterwards the servants began to die one by one.

A medium came to the house to find out what was happening. The carpenter downplayed it, but the visitor left, and his wife, growing anxious when she heard of it, unsettled him so much that he feigned illness and went to bed early. A voice at his bedside warned him that when his wife returned he should look closely at her appearance and flee to the mountains without stopping.

When she came back, he saw her pretending to sleep. She then transformed into a creature over two metres tall, shed her clothes and took the shape of a beautiful woman amid a sound like thunder, though half of her body remained monstrous. The husband fled to the mountains. According to the story, she had killed the servants and would have killed him too.

Legacy

Postwar literature settled on two versions of the Taka-Onna: Fujisawa's, which places her on the upper floors of brothels, and Yamada's, which presents her as an unloved ugly woman who, as a yokai, peeks into brothels to look at what she never had. Folklorist Kenji Murakami pointed out that the first is only one reading of Sekien's print and the second is a different tale sharing the name. In 2016 a statue of her in Gobō, based on a design by Mizuki Shigeru, was smashed and later raised from the bottom of a harbour.

Trivia

A broken statue. In 2016 a statue of the Taka-Onna in a park in Gobō was severed at its base; its torso was found at the bottom of the harbour and raised again. A high school student was charged after kicking it.
A print without text. She appears in Toriyama Sekien's Gazu Hyakki Yagyō, an illustrated book that gives no explanation of what kind of yokai the figure is.
