Zoroastrian god of victory

Verethragna

Verethragna is a deity of Zoroastrianism, the god of victory in the ancient Iranian pantheon. His name is linked to the Avestan verethra, meaning obstacle, and verethragnan, meaning victorious, and the texts suggest he was among the most popular gods of ancient Iran.

The myth

The main account survives in Yasht 14, the hymn dedicated to Verethragna within the Avesta. According to the tradition, the god takes ten forms: first as an impetuous wind, then as an armed warrior and as a fifteen-year-old youth, and in seven animal shapes: a bull with golden horns, a white horse, a camel in heat, a boar, a bird of prey, a ram and a wild goat. Several of these forms are shared with other divinities, such as Tishtrya and Vayu.

The hymn portrays him in constant battle against men and demons, but also linked to virility and the power to heal. In later Middle Persian texts he is one of the Amesha Spenta, charged with driving back Angra Mainyu. He is also named as the chief companion of Mithra.

Worship and religion

Strabo, in the first century, reports a cult of a divinity of victory among the Carmanians, though it is unlikely this was Verethragna. His most visible ritual presence is in the Zoroastrian calendar, where the twentieth day of the month was dedicated to him, and in the Atash Bahram, the most sacred class of fire.

Legacy

His name gave rise to Bahram, found in personal names and in that of six Sasanian kings. In the Sasanian astronomical reforms the planet Mars was named Bahram. In the Hellenistic period he was identified with Ares and Heracles, and his image appears in royal reliefs. The scholar Mirza Husain Noori Tabarsi listed Bahrām among the names of the Shia Mahdi.

Ancient sources: The Yasht 14 of the Avesta is the main source; Strabo and Herodotus allude to cults of war gods in the region.

Trivia

Ten forms. The hymn lists ten appearances of the god, from an impetuous wind to a wild goat, which helps explain his shifting character.
A name passed to kings. Bahram, the later Persian form of the god's name, was borne by six Sasanian kings.
