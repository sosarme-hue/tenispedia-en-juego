Norse god of poetry

Bragi

Bragi is a figure of Norse mythology associated with poetry and the skalds, the court poets of the North. In the tradition recorded by Snorri Sturluson he is the god of poetry and the husband of Iðunn, while in older poetry he appears as a resident of Valhalla who welcomes fallen kings.

The myth

According to Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda, Bragi is a son of Odin, though his mother varies between versions: some name the giantess Gunnlöð, others Frigg. Snorri presents him as the most eloquent of the Æsir and the first to compose verse, so much so that accomplished poets came to be nicknamed after him. He is also the husband of Iðunn, the goddess who keeps the apples of youth that the gods eat to stay young.

In older poetry his role is far more modest. Grímnismál calls him "the most pre-eminent of poets", although it is unclear whether the line refers to the god or to the human skald Bragi Boddason. In Lokasenna he appears at Ægir's feast, where he trades insults with Loki, who mocks his cowardice and lack of martial prowess. The quarrel ends when Iðunn intervenes and urges her husband to restrain himself. In Sigrdrífumál his tongue is one of the many places where runes are carved, though the meaning of that episode remains obscure. In Eiríksmál and Hákonarmál, Bragi and Hermóðr greet dead kings arriving at Valhalla, which leaves open whether he is a god or a dead hero.

Most modern scholars believe Bragi emerged from the posthumous mythologization of the 9th-century skald Bragi Boddason, who was elevated to divine status after death, rather than from an independent pre-Christian cult.

Legacy

His name is linked to the Old Norse word bragr, meaning either "poetry" or "the first, the noblest". From this root comes the Icelandic bragur, a poem or melody. The ceremonial cup used for oath-taking, braga-full, is read as "cup of Bragi" or "cup of the lord", though its name is derived from the sense of "the noblest". Snorri took the word for poetry to come from the god's name, but Rudolf Simek regards that link as secondary and perhaps later.

Ancient sources: Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda and the Poetic Edda poems Grímnismál, Lokasenna and Sigrdrífumál, along with the skaldic poems Eiríksmál and Hákonarmál.

Trivia

A nickname for poets. According to Snorri, those who excelled at poetry were called Bragi, so the god gave his name to a whole class of artists.
Bragi's cup. The ceremonial cup braga-full, used for oath-taking, is etymologically linked to his name.
