phantom island in the Atlantic Ocean

Hy-Brasil

Hy-Brasil is a legendary island said to lie hidden in the Atlantic west of Ireland. According to Irish tradition, it is wrapped in mist and surfaces only one day every seven years, always out of reach. Its name appeared on European maps for centuries, making it one of the most persistent phantom islands in cartography.

The myth

Irish stories describe the island as cloaked in mist for most of the time, showing itself for a single day every seven years, yet remaining impossible to reach. In some versions it is linked with Saint Brendan's island, the seafaring monk's destination in the ocean.

The legend later moved onto maps. From the fourteenth century it appears on nautical charts under varying names, such as Braçir or Brazille, placed in the Atlantic off Ireland. Around 1480 expeditions set out from Bristol to search for it, and in 1674 a supposed witness, Captain John Nisbet, claimed to have visited it, although his account proved to be a literary invention. In the nineteenth century it was proposed that the island was the Porcupine Bank, a shoal discovered in 1862.

Legacy

The island inspired stories by Irish writers such as Gerald Griffin and appears as a magical place in Mary Stewart's novel The Hollow Hills, where some characters wrongly believe Merlin has hidden the young Prince Arthur there. In the comedy Erik the Viking (1989) it is the site of the Horn Resounding, which lets mortals reach Asgard. The name also led to confusion with the South American territory called Brazil, although the two are unrelated.

Trivia

An invented witness. Captain John Nisbet, who in 1674 claimed to have seen the island, was in fact a character in a story written by Irish author Richard Head.
Origin of the name. The name may come from a red dye, brazilwood, or from an Irish clan called Bresal, depending on the theory.
