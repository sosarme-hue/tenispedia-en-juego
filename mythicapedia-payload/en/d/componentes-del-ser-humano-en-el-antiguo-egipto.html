the ancient Egyptian concept of the soul and its parts

Components of the human being in ancient Egypt

The set of bodily and spiritual parts that ancient Egyptians attributed to each person, such as the ka, ba and ren. Understanding this system explains why mummification and funerary rites were so central to life in ancient Egypt.

The myth

The ancient Egyptians did not see a person as a single unit, but as a combination of parts, some physical and others intangible. Tradition held that the god Atum created the world out of chaos through his own magic, and that this magic took the form of the soul in every human being.

The body, called khet, was an inert support that had to be preserved so the deceased could go on existing. Alongside it worked the ka, the life force that Khnum shaped on his potter's wheel and that was sustained by food and drink, and the ba, the personality that flew out of the tomb as a human-headed bird to join the ka. The ib, seat of thought and emotion, was weighed against the symbol of Maat at the judgment of Osiris. The ren, the name, lived as long as it was spoken, and the sheut, the shadow, was shown as a figure painted entirely black. When the rites were completed and the deceased was judged worthy, the sah, a spiritual body able to deal with the beings of the afterlife, took form; it was sometimes feared as an avenging spirit. The akh, a luminous body linked to the stars, was at first reserved for pharaohs and gods, and later initiated people could attain it too.

Worship and religion

Offerings of food and drink were left before tombs to nourish the ka. Mummification, first reserved for the pharaoh and later extended to all the dead, aimed to preserve the body: it was cleansed with natron, filled with spices and wrapped in linen. The opening of the mouth ceremony, depicted in the tomb of Seti I, was meant to restore movement to each part of the body so it could act in the afterlife. Small figurines called ushabtis, representing servants who would attend the dead, were also placed in tombs.

Ancient sources: The Coffin Texts and the Book of the Dead describe the fate of the ba and ka after death.

Trivia

A bird with a human head. The ba was often depicted as a human-headed bird that flew out of the tomb to join the ka in the afterlife.
Erasing a name. Removing the name from an object or monument broke its link with the dead person, and this was sometimes done deliberately to harm their prospects in the afterlife.
A Greek word for the soul. When Christianity reached Egypt, it did not use the term ba for the soul but the Greek word psychē, since the Egyptian concept was hard to translate.
