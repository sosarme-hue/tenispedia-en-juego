mountain land of the Sumerian myths

Aratta

Aratta is a wealthy, mountainous land in the Sumerian myths, famed for its gold, silver, lapis lazuli and skilled craftsmen. Its rulers clashed with Enmerkar and Lugalbanda, early kings of Uruk, over the goddess Inanna. Its real location remains unknown.

The myth

In Sumerian tales, Aratta lies upstream, beyond Susa and the mountains of Anshan, and can only be reached after a long and arduous journey. Its lord, called Ensuh-keshdanna in some texts, competes with Enmerkar of Uruk for the favour of the goddess Inanna. According to the myth, Inanna leaves Aratta and switches her allegiance to Uruk, where she acts as a kind of maker of kings.

The poems about Lugalbanda tell how he, ill and abandoned in a mountain cave, regains his strength after praying to the gods and is guided by the Anzud bird. Inanna then advises Enmerkar to seize Aratta's metalworkers and stonemasons, and the city, whose battlements are described as green lapis lazuli, ends up under Uruk's control. The versions differ: in one, a sorcerer sent from Hamazi fails against a wise woman and the lord of Aratta concedes defeat; in another, the dispute is settled by a duel between champions.

Legacy

Aratta does not appear outside Sumerian literature, yet its name has inspired many theories. Samuel Kramer placed it near Lake Urmia, and the Jiroft culture in south-eastern Iran has been proposed as a candidate. The name has also been linked to Urartu and Ararat, and to the Sanskrit Āraṭṭa mentioned in the Mahabharata, though these remain unconfirmed hypotheses.

Ancient sources: Sumerian texts such as Enmerkar and the Lord of Aratta, Lugalbanda in the Mountain Cave and Lugalbanda and the Anzud Bird, as well as hymns like that of Shulgi, which praises its treasures.

Trivia

An accidental find. In 2000, a prolonged drought shifted the course of the Halil river in southern Iran and exposed pottery that triggered a massive looting spree until the Iranian government stepped in during 2001.
A pyramid bigger than the ziggurats. According to archaeologists, the stepped pyramid in the Jiroft valley measured about 400 metres per side, three times larger than Mesopotamian ziggurats and considerably older.
