Mesopotamian goddess, sister of Dumuzi

Geshtinanna

Geshtinanna was a Mesopotamian goddess, sister of the shepherd god Dumuzi, best known for her part in the myths about his death. Her cult was modest, but she shows how Mesopotamians imagined the yearly balance between the living and the dead.

The myth

According to Mesopotamian tradition, Geshtinanna was the sister of Dumuzi, a god linked to herds and fertility. When Dumuzi died, she offered to spend half of each year in the underworld in his place, so that he could return to the world of the living for the other half. This is told in the Descent of Inanna, where the goddess searches for her husband among the dead.

Other texts present her differently. In the poem Dumuzi's Dream she is a scribe skilled with tablets and an expert singer, praised for her wisdom. Sources also link her to dream interpretation, a role often given to goddesses in general. In some incantations she is called the chief scribe of the underworld, responsible for keeping track of the dead and deciding who could enter the afterlife.

Worship and religion

Her worship is documented mainly in Lagash during the Early Dynastic period, with her own cult center at Sagub and a possible temple called Esagug, which was reportedly plundered in the time of Lugalzaggesi. The ruler Gudea dedicated several statues to her and named her among the deities who looked on him with favour. She also had a temple in Girsu and was worshipped in Uruk alongside Inanna and Dumuzi. During the Ur III dynasty, royal rites were held in her honour, perhaps linked to the funerary cult. After the Old Babylonian period she ceased to be venerated, though she still appeared in god lists and literary texts into the Seleucid era. There is no evidence that her cult survived.

Ancient sources: She appears in the Descent of Inanna, Dumuzi's Dream, the Udug-hul incantations, poems of Shulgi and inscriptions of Urukagina and Gudea.

Trivia

A heavenly vine. Her name is usually translated as «grapevine of heaven». The word geštin may also have had the figurative sense of «sweet» or «lovely».
Watched by a mushussu. Images from Lagash show a goddess accompanied by a mushussu, a mythical beast that looks at her. She may be Geshtinanna, and the beast could represent her husband, Ningishzida.
