legendary rulers and culture heroes of ancient China

Three Sovereigns and Five Emperors

They are the mythical rulers who, according to Chinese tradition, governed before the Xia dynasty and laid the foundations of civilization, teaching fire, writing, agriculture and family life. Today they are regarded as culture heroes, although in ancient times they were widely worshipped as divine ancestral spirits.

The myth

According to the myth, the Three Sovereigns were demigods or god-kings who used magical and divine powers, or harmony with the Tao, to improve the lives of their people. Because of their great virtue they lived to a great age and ruled over an era of peace. They share traits with the xian, the immortals of Taoism, such as the Human Sovereign's cloud-chariot. After his death, the Yellow Emperor was said to have become a xian.

The identities of the Three Sovereigns vary by source. Sima Qian's Records of the Grand Historian names the Heavenly, Earthly and Tai Augusts, while other works substitute Suiren, Fuxi, Nüwa, Shennong, Gonggong or the Yellow Emperor. The Five Emperors were exemplary sages from a golden age when the human and divine orders were closely linked, and when power passed by abdication until Qi of Xia seized it by force and established a hereditary monarchy.

The Yellow Emperor is regarded as the ancestor of the Huaxia people and is central to most lists of the five. Other lists include Zhuanxu, Ku, Yao, Shun, Shaohao or Taihao, varying by text. Houji, the Lord of Millet, is also linked to this period, credited with introducing millet during the Xia dynasty.

Worship and religion

In ancient times these figures were worshipped as divine ancestral spirits. The Mausoleum of the Yellow Emperor in Shaanxi was built to commemorate the legend of his lineage, and Taoist temples dedicated to the Three Sovereigns exist.

Legacy

They are credited with bringing fire, writing, irrigation, silk, medicine, the calendar and the building of houses. The Yellow Emperor remains an emblematic figure of Chinese identity.

Ancient sources: Sima Qian's Records of the Grand Historian, the Shìběn, the Book of Rites (Lǐjì) and the Elegies of Chu record the various lists of sovereigns and emperors.

Trivia

A royal mausoleum. The Mausoleum of the Yellow Emperor was established in Shaanxi to commemorate the ancestral legend.
Shifting lists. Depending on the source, Gonggong or Zhurong appear in place of other sovereigns, showing how much the lists vary.
