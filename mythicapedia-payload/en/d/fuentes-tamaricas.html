intermittent springs of the Cantabri

Fontes Tamarici

Three nearby springs described by Pliny the Elder, which filled and emptied without any explanation and were read as omens. Today they are identified with La Reana in Velilla del Río Carrión, Palencia, Spain, although this identification is disputed.

The myth

Classical accounts describe springs whose water rises and vanishes with no clear cycle or logic, announced by an underground rumble. Pliny records that the naturalist Larcius Licinius visited the spring while it was dry and died a week later, around 70 AD, a story that fed the belief that the springs brought bad luck.

According to the tradition, the Tamarici, a people of the Cantabri, revered the waters and read their irregular rhythm as a sign, and the site may have been dedicated to a water god.

Worship and religion

The Tamarici revered the waters and sacred springs. Later, a hermitage dedicated to Saint John the Baptist was built beside the spring to replace pagan rites.

Legacy

Declared a Bien de Interés Cultural in 2002, the site draws visitors, and some superstitious ones still heed the plaque at the entrance warning that visiting while empty brings bad luck.

Ancient sources: Pliny the Elder's Naturalis Historia (Book XXXI) is the earliest reference, and Claudius Ptolemy mentions them in his Geography.

Trivia

A warning plaque. At the entrance, a stone slab warns that visiting the spring while it is empty brings bad luck, and some superstitious visitors still take it seriously.
A disputed name. Before 1900 the site appears as La Riana, and the form Reana may derive from La Romana, a theory that is now debated.
