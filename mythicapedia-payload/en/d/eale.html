mythical beast of European tradition and heraldry

Eale

The eale, also called the yale or centicore, is a fantastic beast resembling an antelope or goat, with swiveling horns and the tusks of a boar. It appears in European natural history and, above all, in British heraldry, where it became a royal emblem.

The myth

The eale first appears in Pliny the Elder's Natural History, which places it in Aethiopia and describes a creature whose horns could swivel in any direction, useful for both attack and defence. According to the tradition, its horns could move at will, and this is the origin of its Greek name, which alludes to turning on itself.

Its name may come from the Hebrew yael, meaning "ibex", although other explanations connect it with Greek. Pliny claimed to have seen it on the sub-Saharan African plains, and modern scholars suspect he may have misread antelopes and buffalo of that region. Solinus later included his own version of the beast in his Collectanea rerum memorabilium, and medieval bestiaries took it up in turn.

Legacy

The eale entered medieval heraldry. The Beaufort family used it, and through Lady Margaret Beaufort, mother of Henry VII, it passed to the Tudors and the British monarchy. It appears on Cambridge college gatehouses and on St George's Chapel in Windsor Castle, and it was one of the Queen's Beasts commissioned for the 1953 coronation of Elizabeth II. In the United States it is linked to Yale University in New Haven.

Ancient sources: Pliny the Elder describes it in Book VIII of his Natural History, and Solinus includes it in his Collectanea rerum memorabilium.

Trivia

Swivelling horns. According to ancient descriptions, the eale's horns could turn in any direction, which was considered an advantage both in attack and in defence.
