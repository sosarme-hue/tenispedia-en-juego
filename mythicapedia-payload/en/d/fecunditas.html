Roman goddess of fertility

Fecunditas

Fecunditas is the Roman goddess of fecundity, the personification of the ability to bear children. Her cult was tied to mothers and to the continuity of families, the imperial one included.

The myth

Fecunditas was shown as a matron with children in her arms or standing beside her, often holding a cornucopia or a hasta pura, symbols of abundance and power. Unlike many other deities, the sources give her no extended story of deeds or a genealogy of her own.

According to the tradition recorded in the sources, the Romans also worshipped Juno under this name, in one of her forms, as the protector of mothers giving birth. Another closely related title was Lucina, "bringer of light", linked to dawn and birth.

Worship and religion

Women came to Fecunditas to ask her for children. During her ritual, they struck their naked bodies with goatskin belts. The sacrifices were performed by the Luperci and the Arval Brethren. In 63 AD, the Roman Senate dedicated a temple to the goddess on the occasion of the birth of Nero's daughter with Poppaea Sabina.

Legacy

Her image frequently appears on Roman coins, especially those minted in the names of empresses, as a symbol of the continuity of the imperial family. It appears with legends such as Fecunditas Augustae and, less often, Fecunditas Temporum.

Ancient sources: The ancient texts do not describe her figure in detail; her presence is documented mainly through temples, rituals and Roman coins.

Trivia

Imperial coins. Fecunditas appears on coins minted for empresses such as Faustina the Elder, and on a gold coin of Julia Domna she is shown with a bunch of grapes, surrounded by children.
Nero's temple. The temple to the goddess, dedicated in 63 AD, was built in Rome by decision of the Senate after the birth of Emperor Nero's daughter.
