Battus

Battus is a figure of Greek mythology linked to the founding of Cyrene, on the Gulf of Sirte in North Africa, and to a story of silence and punishment involving Hermes. According to tradition, his name is associated with a stammer.

The myth

According to tradition, Battus stammered because he had secretly attended the Thesmophoria rites, which were closed to him. He then set out to found Cyrene. His voice was said to have a double effect: it frightened lions, which in return granted him the gift of speech. Herodotus suggested that his name was the Libyan word for “king”.

Ovid tells a different story with the same name. Battus was an old shepherd in Maenalus, Arcadia, who saw Hermes steal Apollo’s cattle. Bribed with a heifer, he promised silence, but the god returned in disguise and offered a reward in exchange for the cattle’s whereabouts. Battus revealed the secret out of greed and was turned into a stone, which marks the way.

Scholars still debate whether there were one or two figures with this name. Brelich linked his speech difficulties to castration, while Maurizio Giangiulio sees in him the deformed hero.

Legacy

Catullus mentions him in his poems. Lope de Vega casts him as a villain in El amor enamorado, and Rafael Sánchez Ferlosio gave his name to one of the thieves in Industrias y andanzas de Alfanhuí.

Ancient sources: Herodotus mentions him in connection with the Libyan word for “king”, and Ovid narrates his story with Hermes and Apollo in detail.

Trivia

A thief named Battus. In Rafael Sánchez Ferlosio’s novel Industrias y andanzas de Alfanhuí, one of the thieves hidden in the hayloft is called “Bato”, and his companion “Faulo” is, surprisingly, mute.
Silence in stone. In Ovid’s account, Battus is punished by being turned into stone, a way of silencing the one who knows. The stone ends up marking the way.
