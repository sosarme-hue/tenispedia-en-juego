mother goddess of Vedic Hinduism

Aditi

Aditi is one of the oldest goddesses in Hinduism, present in the Vedas as the mother of the gods. She embodies the boundless, infinite cosmos and is linked to motherhood, fertility and freedom from bonds.

The myth

According to the Vedic texts, above all the Rigveda, where she is mentioned more than 250 times, Aditi is the great feminine deity and the mother of the Adityas, a group of gods that includes Varuna, Mitra, Aryaman and Surya. Her name is understood as 'boundless' or 'unbound', and so she is asked to free petitioners from sin, sickness or chains. She was also invoked as protector of children and cattle, and as giver of food. Some texts call her mother of all the gods, and the Shatapatha Brahmana identifies her with the Earth.

Later accounts, such as the Puranas, change her genealogy. According to the Bhagavata Purana, she is a daughter of Daksha and the wife of the sage Kashyapa, with whom she had twelve sons, the Adityas, later regarded as gods of the sky. In another version, Kashyapa lived in his hermitage with Aditi and her sister Diti, and after her devoted service he granted her a boon: she asked for an ideal son, and Indra was born. Other texts make her the wife of Marichi, and the Shatapatha Brahmana presents her alone, without a husband.

Worship and religion

Aditi had no cult as broad as other Vedic gods, and no hymn is addressed to her alone; she is usually invoked alongside other deities. An old temple dedicated to her stands near a rock-cut cave in Vizhinjam, Kerala, India. In Vedic ritual, during sacrificial offerings, she is sometimes identified with the Earth.

Legacy

Her name is associated with ideas of infinity and freedom, and the twelve Adityas gave rise to the division of the year into twelve months. Theosophists read the verse 'Daksha sprang from Aditi and Aditi from Daksha' as an image of the eternal cyclic rebirth of the same divine Essence. In Vedanta philosophy she was linked to the primal substance (mulaprakriti).

Ancient sources: The Vedas, especially the Rigveda, where she is one of the most important female deities, and the Shatapatha Brahmana; later, the Puranas, such as the Bhagavata Purana.

Trivia

The stolen earrings. According to tradition, the asura Naraka stole a pair of earrings from her. Krishna, together with his consort Satyabhama, returned them after slaying the asura.
Twelve months. Her twelve sons, the Adityas, give rise to the twelve months of the year in tradition.
A granted son. In the Puranas, Indra is born in answer to Aditi's request to Kashyapa for an ideal son.
