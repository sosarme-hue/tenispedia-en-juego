Ophion

Ophion is an ancient figure of Greek mythology, linked to the serpent, who in some versions ruled the world together with Eurynome before being overthrown. His story survives mainly in fragmentary Orphic texts and later authors, and it offers one of the alternative accounts of how the cosmos came to be.

The myth

According to the tradition, Ophion and Eurynome held sway over the world from snowy Olympus, and they ruled over the Titans. The sources do not agree on the couple's origins. Apollonius of Rhodes, in his Argonautica, makes Eurynome a daughter of Oceanus, while Nonnus of Panopolis names Harmonia instead of Eurynome, a name that is probably a scribal error.

Their downfall came when Cronus and Rhea seized power and cast them into the waves of Oceanus. Lycophron says that Rhea threw the former queen Eurynome into Tartarus. In the work of Pherecydes of Syros the struggle is older and cosmic: the living principle Zas defeats Ophion and drives him down into Ogenos, the primordial sea, a battle that scholars compare with other primordial combat myths from the ancient Near East.

In the songs attributed to Orpheus, the account continues with the ordering of the cosmos, and the young Zeus is still a child in the Dictaean cave, without the thunderbolt that the Cyclopes will later give him. Genealogies of Ophion are scarce and scattered, and the only filiation that survives links him to Oceanus.

Ancient sources: Apollonius of Rhodes, Argonautica; Lycophron, Alexandra; Nonnus of Panopolis, Dionysiaca; Pherecydes of Syros and the Orphic fragments; Homer and Hesiod do not mention him.

Trivia

A very early account. Fragments of Pherecydes of Syros, a philosopher of the 6th century BCE, are the first surviving mention of Ophion, in a story where Chronos creates the universe.
A Phoenician origin. Philo of Byblos claimed that Pherecydes had taken Ophion and the Ophionidae from the Phoenicians, as recorded by Eusebius of Caesarea.
