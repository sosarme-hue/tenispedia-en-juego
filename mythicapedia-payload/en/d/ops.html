Roman goddess of abundance and earth, consort of Saturn

Ops

Ops was the Roman goddess of abundance, the fertility of the earth and agriculture, and the consort of Saturn. Her name comes from the Latin word ops, meaning wealth and goods, and she was seen as a protector of the harvest and of the people's livelihood.

The myth

According to tradition, Ops was the daughter of Caelus, the Sky, and of Tellus or Vesta, the Earth, although ancient writers disagree about her parentage. Several texts make her both the wife and sister of Saturn, and their children, depending on the version, include Jupiter, Neptune, Pluto, Juno, Ceres and Vesta.

The best-known story concerns the birth of Jupiter. To keep Saturn from devouring his son, Ops gave him a stone wrapped in swaddling clothes, which the god swallowed. In another version, milk spilled from Ops' breast when she pressed it against the stone, and this formed the Milky Way. Lactantius also tells that Ops hid several of her children and that, after Jupiter's victory, his parents were freed from the prison in which Titan had confined them.

In art she is shown seated on a throne, holding a sceptre, sheaves of wheat or a cornucopia.

Worship and religion

According to tradition, the cult of Ops was established by the Sabine king Titus Tatius. She had a famous temple on the Capitol, beside the Capitoline Triad. The Opiconsivia was celebrated on 25 August and the Opalia on 19 December, the latter alongside Saturn's festival of the Saturnalia. Ops was also honoured together with Consus, the god of stored grain.

Legacy

The name of Ops survives in the language through the word ops and the family of opus, 'work'. She also inspired Giovanni Boccaccio, who included her in De Mulieribus Claris, one of the first collections of women's biographies in Western literature.

Ancient sources: Varro mentions her in De Lingua Latina, and she also appears in texts by Festus, Macrobius, Plautus and Ovid.
