place of punishment after death in many religions

Hell

Hell is the place or state of the afterlife where, according to many religions, the souls of the wicked suffer punishment after death. The idea appears in traditions across the world, from ancient Egypt to Buddhism, and remains one of the most influential concepts in Western religion and culture.

The myth

Traditions agree that punishment follows death, although they imagine it in very different ways. According to the Egyptian tradition from the Middle Kingdom onward, the dead faced a tribunal of forty-two divine judges. Those who had lived in line with the precepts of the goddess Maat were welcomed into the heavenly reed fields. Those found guilty were thrown to Ammit, the devourer of the dead, and condemned to a lake of fire, an end of annihilation rather than eternal torture. In Persian dualism, Hell was the abode of the chief devil, Angra Manyu, and the dead chose between paradise and Hell according to their deeds, although tradition held that evil would eventually be destroyed.

In Judaism and Christianity, the Sheol of the Hebrew Bible was a neutral place where all the dead went down, while the New Testament associates Gehenna with an unquenchable fire and worms that never die. These images gradually merged, and the Ethiopic Book of Enoch placed sinners in Gehenna. In later Christian tradition, Hell filled with demons who tormented the damned, and the Devil himself came to be seen as a prisoner there. Islam and Christianity give faith and repentance more weight than deeds in determining the fate of the soul.

In Buddhism, Naraka is one of the six realms of samsara and is described in vivid detail. In Chinese mythology, Diyu is an intermediate period before a new rebirth, and in Norse mythology Helheim is the realm of the goddess Hel. Plato, in the Myth of Er, assigned specific punishments for each fault, and Dante, in the Divine Comedy, divided Hell into circles, with a frozen lake of blood and guilt at the deepest one.

Worship and religion

Hell is not worshipped as a deity, but it plays an important doctrinal role. In Catholic theology it is one of the four last things of human existence. The 1997 Catechism describes it as a state of definitive self-exclusion from communion with God, and John Paul II stressed that it is less a place than a situation caused by distance from God. Pope Francis and several contemporary theologians also regard it as a state of suffering. Some evangelical groups hold that the dead are unconscious and that Hell is not a place of torment.

Legacy

The English word hell comes from Old English helle, related to Old Norse hel and Gothic halja, all linked to an ancient Germanic root meaning to cover or conceal. The Spanish word infierno comes from Latin inférnum, meaning lower place. In art and literature, Dante's Divine Comedy gave visual form to the Western Hell, and its frozen lake is one of its most memorable images. Terms such as Hades and Helheim remain associated with the world of the dead in European culture.

Ancient sources: The Bible (Sheol, Gehenna and Hades in the Gospels, Acts and Revelation), the Ethiopic Book of Enoch, the Buddhist Kathāvatthu, Plato's Myth of Er and, later in the Middle Ages, Dante's Divine Comedy.

Trivia

A hidden word. English hell comes from a Germanic root meaning to cover or conceal, so etymologically Hell is a hidden place.
Cold hells. Tibetan Buddhist descriptions include as many cold hells as hot ones, and in the Divine Comedy the deepest circle is a frozen lake.
A very early account. The Buddhist Kathāvatthu, written between roughly 250 and 100 BCE, is one of the oldest detailed descriptions of Hell that survive.
