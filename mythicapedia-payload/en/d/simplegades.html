clashing rocks at the Bosphorus in Greek myth

Symplegades

The Symplegades, also called the Clashing Rocks or Cyanean Rocks, were a pair of rocks in Greek mythology that crashed together whenever a ship tried to pass between them. They were usually placed in the Bosphorus strait, and they are famous because the Argonauts were the first to get through them safely.

The myth

According to the myth, the rocks slammed shut violently and crushed any vessel caught between them. On their quest for the Golden Fleece, the Argonauts managed to pass by following Phineus's advice: they released a dove between the rocks to judge how fast they would have to row. The dove lost only the feathers of its tail, and the Argonauts rowed with all their strength, losing only part of the ship's stern ornament. From then on the rocks stood still forever.

In the Odyssey, the sorceress Circe warns Odysseus about the 'wandering rocks' that only the Argo had ever passed; there the rocks destroy ships, and the wreckage is scattered across the sea. Apollonius of Rhodes places the Symplegades on the outward voyage and the wandering rocks on the return, and scholars have long debated whether they are the same.

Worship and religion

According to Dionysius of Byzantium, one of the rocks held a Roman shrine to Apollo. On the European rock, near a village on the shore, an ancient altar survives, known as the Pillar of Pompey, although it has nothing to do with Pompey.

Legacy

Lord Byron mentions them at the close of Childe Harold. The critic I. A. Richards used them as a metaphor for the perils of reading poetry, caught between careless intuitive reading and overly literal reading. In his novel Jason (1961), Henry Treece turned them into icebergs drifting down a river into the Black Sea. In Turkish they are called Öreke Taşı, the 'Distaff Rock' or 'Midwife's Stool'.

Ancient sources: Homer's Odyssey, with Circe's warning about the wandering rocks, and Apollonius of Rhodes' Argonautica, which narrates the Argonauts' passage.

Trivia

A dove as a test. The Argonauts did not risk their crew: they used a dove to time the rocks before attempting the passage, and the bird survived, losing only its tail.
A misleading name. The so-called Pillar of Pompey is named after a Roman general, but it has no connection with him.
