magic ring of Norse mythology

Andvaranaut

Andvaranaut, meaning ‘Andvari’s gem’, is a magic ring from Norse mythology said to help its owner find sources of gold. Its story of greed and curse makes it one of the best-known treasures in Germanic and Scandinavian legend.

The myth

According to the myth, the ring first belonged to the dwarf Andvari, who kept it with a hoard of gold. Loki tricked him into handing it over, and Andvari, in revenge, cursed the ring to bring misfortune and destruction to anyone who possessed it. Loki quickly passed it to Hreidmar, king of the dwarves, as compensation for having accidentally killed his son Ótr.

The curse soon took effect. Fafnir, another of Hreidmar's sons, murdered his father, took the ring and turned into a dragon to guard it. Sigurd killed him and then gave the ring to Brynhildr, who took her own life when he left her. In some versions, Queen Grimhild manipulates Sigurd and Brynhildr into marrying her children, bringing the curse into her own family.

Legacy

Richard Wagner took the ring as inspiration for the title of his music drama Der Ring des Nibelungen, in which the dwarf Alberich plays the role of Andvari. Some have suggested that it also influenced J. R. R. Tolkien's The Lord of the Rings, though the author firmly denied this: in a letter he said the only resemblance between the two rings was that they were both ‘round’. Some scholars also speculate about a possible link to the curse of the Hope Diamond.

Ancient sources: The Eddas and the Völsunga saga, medieval Icelandic texts, record the story of the ring and its curse.

Trivia

A ‘round’ ring. Tolkien wrote to his publisher that the only similarity between his ring and Andvaranaut was that both were round, rejecting the usual comparisons.
A possibly cursed diamond. Some scholars speculate that the ring may have given rise to the curse attributed to the Hope Diamond, although this remains an unproven hypothesis.
