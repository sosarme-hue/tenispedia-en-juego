Greek concept of excellence and virtue

Arete

Arete is the ancient Greek concept of excellence, meaning the full realization of a thing's or person's proper function, and later also moral virtue. Though mainly a philosophical idea, the Greeks sometimes personified it as a minor goddess, sister of Homonoia.

The myth

According to tradition, Arete had little mythology of her own and appears mostly as a personification of virtue. Her only surviving story was told by the sophist Prodicus and is known as 'Heracles at the Crossroads'. Two women approach the young hero there: Arete offers him glory and a life of struggle against evil, while her counterpart Kakia promises wealth and pleasure. Heracles chooses the path of Arete.

In Homer the term is applied to warriors, nobles and even women such as Penelope, and it is associated above all with courage and effectiveness. For the early Greeks, fate could prevent a person from reaching it, since someone born blind or as a woman could not win glory in battle.

Worship and religion

Arete was occasionally personified as a minor goddess, daughter of Praxidike and sister of Homonoia (concord), and the two were known together as the Exacters of Justice. The sources record no real, widespread worship of her.

Legacy

The word passed into philosophy and ethics: Aristotle built his theory of virtue on the idea of the mean, and Greek education (paideia) aimed to cultivate it. The writer Jo Walton named a key character in her Thessaly trilogy Arete, and the tabletop game Mage: The Ascension uses arete to measure a mage's power. Robert Pirsig used it as a synonym for 'quality' in Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance.

Ancient sources: Homer's Iliad and Odyssey, where it is linked to courage and cleverness, and the works of Hesiod, which also connect it with cunning.

Trivia

Heracles at the Crossroads. The only ancient story centred on Arete was told by the sophist Prodicus, and Christian writers such as Clement of Alexandria later retold it.
Excellence of anything. The Greeks spoke of the arete of a soldier, a bull or a ship, although applying it to inanimate objects was rare.
