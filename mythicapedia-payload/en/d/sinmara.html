giantess of Norse mythology, consort of Surtr

Sinmara

Sinmara is a giantess (gýgr) in Norse mythology, usually considered the consort of Surtr, the fiery lord of Muspelheim. The meaning of her name is much debated, and it has been linked to nightmares and to the image of a pale figure.

The myth

Sinmara appears only in the Eddic poem Fjölsvinnsmál. There, Svipdagr, who seeks to win the hand of Menglöð, questions the watchman Fjölsviðr, who explains that the hounds guarding the mansion can only be distracted with the flesh of the rooster Víðópnir. Only the weapon Lævateinn can kill it, and Sinmara keeps it. She will hand it over only to someone who brings her a tail feather from that rooster, which creates a paradox. Fjölsviðr then suggests that Svipdagr bring her the "bright sickle", and so he could obtain the weapon.

In some editions and translations, Sinmara is also named beside Surtr, both threatened by the rooster perched on the tree Mímameiðr, although the original reading of that stanza does not mention her. According to Viktor Rydberg's theory, she was the wife of Mímir and mother of Nótt, while other scholars regard her as the wife of Surtr.

Ancient sources: She is mentioned in the Eddic poem Fjölsvinnsmál, where she keeps Lævateinn and, in some editions, appears as Surtr's companion.

Trivia

Possible identity with Hel. The philologist Hjalmar Falk argued that Sinmara might actually be Hel, daughter of Loki, and compared her to the Roman goddess Proserpina because of her pale or half-blue appearance.
A much-debated name. Her name has been read as "pale nightmare", "great nightmare" or "the one who maims the sinews", among other proposals, and none has become the accepted meaning.
