young hero of Greek mythology, master of a romantic trick

Acontius

Acontius is a young man from Greek mythology, from the island of Ceos, famous for the trick with which he won the hand of the beautiful Cydippe. The tale, of Hellenistic origin, explains the origin of the Acontiadae and gave rise to a proverb about clever stratagems.

The myth

According to the story, Acontius was a handsome young man from Ioulis on Ceos, born to respectable but not wealthy parents. He travelled to Delos for a festival of Apollo, where Eros struck him with an arrow and he fell in love with Cydippe, a maiden from Naxos or Athens who had come with her family and slaves to offer incense and wine. Acontius took an apple (or, rarely, a quince) from Aphrodite's garden, wrote on it "By Artemis, I will marry Acontius" and threw it at her feet. Cydippe picked it up, read the words aloud and was bound by the vow, though she did not realise it and simply discarded the fruit.

His unanswered love consumed him: he lost sleep, withdrew to the fields and vineyards and carved her name into tree bark. Cydippe was betrothed to another, but each time she entered the bridal chamber she fell gravely ill for months. Her father consulted the oracle of Apollo, which declared that the god's sister would not allow her to marry anyone but the man to whom she had sworn. Cydippe then recounted the incident at the temple of Delos, and she wed Acontius on Naxos. According to the myth, their union became the ancestry of the Acontiadae.

Worship and religion

The scene unfolds during a festival of Apollo on Delos, where the couple first meet, and Apollo's oracle rules that the vow must be honoured. The sources do not describe any cult devoted to Acontius himself.

Legacy

His story gave its name to the proverb "Acontius and Cydippe", used to refer to the effectiveness of a similar stratagem.

Ancient sources: Callimachus, in the third book of the Aetia, records the tale attributed to Xenomedes; Ovid includes it in the Heroides, and Antoninus Liberalis tells an almost identical story about Ctesylla and Hermochares.

Trivia

A name with meaning. The name Acontius is the masculine form of a Greek word meaning "javelin", in its diminutive form.
A gift of Aphrodite. The apple was Aphrodite's symbolic fruit, emblem of her victory in the beauty contest against Hera and Athena, and the love gift par excellence.
A twin story. Antoninus Liberalis records an almost identical tale about Ctesylla and Hermochares, in which she dies in childbirth because of the broken oath.
