Phrygian king in Greek myth

Mygdon

Mygdon is a Phrygian king in Greek mythology, son of Acmon and father of Coroebus. He matters because the tradition places him in a campaign against the Amazons one generation before the Trojan War.

The myth

According to the tradition preserved in the Iliad, Mygdon was the son of Acmon and king of Phrygia, and with his wife Anaximene he fathered Coroebus. He is remembered mainly for a military campaign: at the head of a Phrygian force he fought the Amazons alongside Otreus, another Phrygian leader, and the young Priam, future king of Troy. All of this happened a generation before the Trojan War.

The episode reaches the Iliad through Priam, who recalls it to Helen in Book 3 and mentions that the Phrygians were camped by the river Sangarius when the Amazons arrived. Some Phrygians were also said to have taken their name, Mygdonians, from Mygdon. Philostratus the Elder, in his Heroica, is doubtful: he finds it hard to believe that the Amazons could later have come to Ilion as allies after Priam had fought them in Mygdon's reign.

Ancient sources: Homer, in Book 3 of the Iliad, and Philostratus the Elder, in his Heroicus, which questions the episode.

Trivia

The Mygdonians. Some Phrygians were said to be called Mygdonians after Mygdon, according to the ancient account.
A doubtful story. Philostratus the Elder found it implausible that the Amazons later became allies of Troy after fighting Priam.
