god of dreams and the forms taken in dreams

Morpheus

Morpheus is the god who, in dreams, takes the shape of people the sleeper knows in order to speak to them. He appears in Ovid's Metamorphoses, in the Latin tradition, and his name comes from the Greek morphē, meaning "form", which is why he remains a lasting image of the dream world.

The myth

According to Ovid, Morpheus is one of the thousand sons of Somnus, the personification of sleep, whom the Greeks called Hypnos. His task is to appear in dreams in human shape, and no other is more skilled at copying a person's gait, face, voice, clothing and words. Like other sleep deities, he is shown with wings.

Morpheus had two brothers who were equally gifted at taking dream forms: Icelos, who adopts animal, bird or serpent shapes, and Phantasos, who imitates rocks, wood or water. Ovid is the only ancient source that mentions Morpheus, and the names of his brothers appear nowhere earlier, so they may be Ovid's own invention or come from a Hellenistic tradition.

The best-known episode is that of Ceyx and Alcyone. Ceyx drowned on a voyage to consult an oracle. Juno, through the messenger Iris, asked Morpheus to appear to Alcyone in a dream in her husband's form to tell her of his death. When she learned the truth, Alcyone threw herself into the sea, and the gods changed the couple into kingfishers.

Legacy

From the Middle Ages, and especially from the Renaissance, the name Morpheus came to stand more generally for the god of sleep, displacing other abstract dream figures. The word "morphine" derives from it, as Friedrich Sertürner chose the name for the opiate, and the phrase "to fall into the arms of Morpheus" comes from the same source. He appears in Baroque and Neoclassical painting, in sculptures by Houdon and Poussin, in Rebecca Clarke's 1917 piece for viola and piano, and in Neil Gaiman's The Sandman. An asteroid also bears his name.

Ancient sources: Ovid's Metamorphoses (Book XI) is the only ancient source that mentions him.
