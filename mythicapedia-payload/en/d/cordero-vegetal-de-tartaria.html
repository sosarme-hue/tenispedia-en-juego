legendary plant-animal of Central Asia

Vegetable Lamb of Tartary

The Vegetable Lamb of Tartary is a legendary zoophyte of Central Asia, once believed to grow sheep as its fruit. The tale circulated in Europe for centuries, blending folklore with a real Chinese tree fern, which makes it a classic example of a being halfway between plant and animal.

The myth

According to the story, the plant produced lambs from melon-like seeds. Each lamb stayed attached to the earth by a cord similar to an umbilical one, which let it graze on the grass in the circle around the plant. When no foliage was left within reach, both the lamb and the plant died. Its flesh, like that of any lamb, could be eaten, and its blood was said to taste sweet like honey. Wolves were the only carnivores drawn to it, and the people of its homeland used its wool to make caps and clothing.

The versions vary. Some believed the lamb was the fruit of the plant; others, that it was a living member of the plant that died once separated from it. Odoric of Pordenone spoke of fruits that fell into the sea and turned into geese, and the Chinese legend of the watersheep suggests another possible origin. Sir John Mandeville brought the legend to Europe with a gourd that, when opened, revealed a woolless lamb. Sigismund von Herberstein placed it near the Caspian Sea. Engelbert Kaempfer travelled to Persia in 1683, found no evidence, and concluded it was only a legend.

Legacy

Erasmus Darwin wrote verses about it in The Botanic Garden (1781), and Guillaume de Salluste Du Bartas also mentioned it in his poetry. During the Renaissance it became an object of philosophical and botanical debate about the natural order of beings and the Aristotelian scale. Thomas Browne recorded it in his Pseudodoxia Epidemica under the name Boramez.

Ancient sources: The earliest references appear in The Travels of Sir John Mandeville (14th century) and in Sigismund von Herberstein's Notes on Russia. Herodotus describes trees bearing wool in India, a possible precursor.

Trivia

A real fern. In 1698 Sir Hans Sloane linked the myth to a Chinese tree fern, Cibotium barometz. The lamb was made by removing the leaves from a section of its woolly rhizome, which when inverted resembled a lamb.
Geese born from trees. The friar Odoric of Pordenone described a tree whose fruits fell into the sea near the Orkneys and became geese. This belief was used to explain the arrival of migrating geese from the north.
