Phrygian horseman god linked to Dionysus

Sabazios

Sabazios is a god of Phrygian and Thracian origin, shown as a horseman and sky father, whose cult spread through Anatolia, Greece and the Roman world. He matters for his mystery rites and for the way Greeks and Romans identified him with both Zeus and Dionysus.

The myth

According to tradition, Sabazios was the horseman and sky father of the Phrygians and Thracians. The Greeks read him in two ways, as Zeus and as Dionysus. Some ancient authors describe him as a second Dionysus, born of Zeus and Persephone, whose rites were held at night and in secret because of the shame of that union; he was also said to have been the first to yoke oxen.

In artworks Sabazios is always shown on horseback, wielding his staff of power, sometimes as a bearded mature man and sometimes wearing Phrygian dress. His main attribute is the serpent. Strabo linked him with Zagreus, and the Orphic hymns make him the father of Dionysus.

Worship and religion

His cult had an initiatory character and was connected with that of the Magna Mater. Private mysteries were celebrated in his honour, though little is known of the ritual before the imperial period. A characteristic votive offering was a bronze or copper hand with various symbols and two raised fingers in a gesture of blessing. At Pergamum he enjoyed the patronage of the Attalids as Zeus Sabazios. There is no evidence that his cult survives today.

Legacy

His image of the rider fighting a serpent passed into the legend of Saint George and the Dragon. In Rome, the expulsion of Jews in 139 BCE was attributed to his cult, though the identification of Sabazios with the Hebrew god was probably an ancient error.

Ancient sources: Aristophanes, Demosthenes, Theophrastus, Strabo, Diodorus Siculus and the Orphic hymns mention him, and Homer indirectly alludes to a possible conflict with Cybele.

Trivia

A votive hand. Many offerings dedicated to this god take the form of a human hand with two raised fingers. Some have a small hole at the base, suggesting they were carried in processions on wooden poles.
