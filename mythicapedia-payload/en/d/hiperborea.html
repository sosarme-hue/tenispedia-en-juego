mythical land of the far north

Hyperborea

Hyperborea was, in Greek mythology, a legendary land beyond Thrace in the far north. Its people, the Hyperboreans, lived in a sunny, divinely blessed country and were closely linked to Apollo.

The myth

According to tradition, the Hyperboreans lived beyond the Riphean Mountains, which shielded them from the northern cold, and some said they reached the age of a thousand years. The oldest texts portray them as Apollo's favourites. Herodotus cites earlier sources that mentioned them, including Hesiod and Homer, though he doubts that Homer wrote about them.

Their location shifted from author to author: Herodotus assumed it lay in northeast Asia, Pindar told how Perseus travelled there before his encounter with Medusa, and others placed it in Britain or near the Urals. Temisto, daughter of the Hyperborean king Zabius, bore Galeotes to Apollo, ancestor of a line of Sicilian seers.

Worship and religion

Some Greek writers regarded the Hyperboreans as the mythical founders of Apollo's shrines at Delos and Delphi. According to Hecataeus of Abdera, their island held a great sacred precinct of Apollo with a spherical temple. No living cult survives.

Legacy

In medieval and Renaissance literature the Hyperboreans came to stand for remoteness and exoticism. Some have proposed that their spherical temple was Stonehenge, and in 1726 John Toland linked it with the Callanish Stones.

Ancient sources: Herodotus (Histories, Book IV), Pindar (Pythian Odes, X) and Aristeas (lost poem Arimaspea), along with the fragments of Hesiod and Homer that Herodotus mentions.

Trivia

One year, one day. Legend said that in Hyperborea the sun rose and set only once a year.
A failed invasion. Theopompus tells how an army from Meropis planned to conquer Hyperborea, but gave up when they found its people too strong and too blessed.
