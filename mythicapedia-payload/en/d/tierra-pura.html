Pure land

A pure land is the celestial realm of a buddha or bodhisattva in Mahayana Buddhism, a place free from the suffering of samsara where conditions for spiritual practice are favorable. It is the central idea of Pure Land Buddhism, one of the most widespread currents in East Asian Buddhism.

The myth

In Mahayana teaching, a buddha purifies a field of existence through his activity and sustaining power, and that field becomes a destination for those with an affinity for it. The texts distinguish pure, impure and mixed fields. Our world, the Saha, is the field of Shakyamuni, and some sutras say he chose to come to an impure world out of compassion.

Tradition speaks of many pure lands. The Lotus and Vimalakirti sutras link them to bodhisattvas such as Avalokiteshvara and Manjushri, who would obtain their own on attaining buddhahood. The most famous is Sukhavati, the land of Amitabha, where time may flow differently from how it flows here.

Worship and religion

Followers of Pure Land Buddhism aspire to be reborn in Sukhavati after death, and the tradition recommends calling on the buddha at the moment of dying. This practice remains alive today in East Asian Buddhism. Sand mandalas, thangkas and the field of merit in Tibetan Buddhism are also regarded as pure lands and are venerated as such.

Legacy

The Chinese term jingtu, 淨土, gives its name to these Buddhist schools. The concept also serves as a metaphor for mandalas and sacred images which, once consecrated, are understood as the pure dwelling of the deity.

Ancient sources: The Lotus Sutra, the Vimalakirti Sutra and the Sanskrit Larger Pure Land Sutra, along with the Mahavastu and the Divyavadana, Indian texts that describe buddha-fields.

Trivia

Endless fields. The Mahavastu states that there are thousands of buddha-fields beyond those it lists, and that their end cannot be discerned.
Different time. According to some texts, a day in one pure land can equal years in another.
