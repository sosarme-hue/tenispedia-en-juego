Roman deity of the voice that warned of the Gallic invasion

Aius Locutius

Aius Locutius is a Roman deity, or rather a numen, linked to the Gallic invasion of Rome around 390 BC. He matters because he was a divine voice that spoke plainly to an ordinary citizen to warn of disaster, which was unusual in official Roman religion.

The myth

According to legend, a plebeian named Marcus Caedicius heard a supernatural voice at night coming from Vesta's sacred grove, at the foot of the Palatine Hill. The voice warned him of an imminent Gallic attack and urged him to fortify Rome's walls and pass the warning on to the tribune of the plebs.

Because the messenger was a humble man, no one heeded him. The Gauls entered and burned the city, and only the Capitol, according to Roman historians, held out. After their defeat, the Senate built a temple and altar, the Ara Aius Locutius or Ara Saepta, to win the favour of this unknown deity, on the spot where Caedicius had heard the voice.

Worship and religion

The Senate built a temple and altar at the site of the revelation, but later historians disputed their exact location, and no trace of either has survived.

Legacy

Cicero recalled that, having acquired a temple, an altar and a name, the "Speaker" never spoke again. Modern scholars such as Mary Beard place it at the limit of prodigious sounds because of the clarity of its warning.

Ancient sources: Ancient references come from Roman historians and writers, including Cicero, who mentions it when discussing omens.

Trivia

A voice clearer than human. According to the sources, the voice spoke in everyday Latin and with a clarity greater than that of a human.
A name that survives. The epithet Locutius was also used to invoke one of the deities concerned with child development.
