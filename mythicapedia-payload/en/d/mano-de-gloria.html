European folklore object linked to popular magic

Hand of Glory

The Hand of Glory is the dried and pickled hand of a hanged man, an object from European folklore and popular magic. Its fame comes from the powers attributed to it in old European beliefs and from the short stories and poems of the 19th century that took it up.

The myth

According to old European beliefs, a Hand of Glory was made from the hand of a hanged man, usually the left one or, if the man was hanged for murder, the hand that had committed the crime. A candle was made from the fat of the same corpse; once lit and placed in the hand as if in a candlestick, it would leave everyone who saw it motionless. The candle could be put out only with milk. In another version, the dead man's hair served as the wick and the light could be seen only by its holder. The hand was also said to unlock any door it passed.

The method is described in 18th-century documents, such as the Petit Albert, though some steps are disputed because the phrases of that era are hard to translate. One debated word is zimat, which some read as Lapland sesame and others as verdigris or Arabian iron sulphate. The name itself is a folk etymology: it comes from French, a corruption of mandragore, the mandrake, a plant believed to shine at night.

Legacy

A real example is kept at the Whitby Museum in North Yorkshire, England. In J. K. Rowling's Harry Potter series it appears in the shop Borgin and Burkes, and Draco Malfoy later uses it with magical smoke bombs to plunge his enemies into darkness. It also appears in Cassandra Clare's Lady Midnight, Charles Stross's The Laundry Files, John Bellairs' young-adult novel The House With A Clock In Its Walls, and Grant Morrison's The Invisibles.

Ancient sources: The texts describing it are mainly 18th-century grimoires and recipe books, such as the Petit Albert and the Compendium Maleficarum; the pseudo-Apuleius herbal, in a Saxon translation, describes the mandrake shining at night like a lamp.

Trivia

A disputed word. The word zimat in the 1722 French text is so hard to translate that some scholars suggest ponie meant horse dung, while others read it as Lapland sesame.
Pigeon hands. In Charles Stross's The Laundry Files, the process needs a nerve-dense limb, so most Hands of Glory shown in the series are those of pigeons.
