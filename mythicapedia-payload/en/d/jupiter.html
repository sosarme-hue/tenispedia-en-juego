Roman god of sky and thunder

Jupiter

Jupiter is the chief god of Roman mythology, father of gods and men, lord of the sky and wielder of the thunderbolt. Equivalent to the Greek Zeus, he was the supreme deity of the Roman state from the Republic through the Empire, and the guardian of the city from his great temple on the Capitoline Hill.

The myth

Accounts of Jupiter's origin do not agree. Cicero reports that the theologians counted up to three gods of that name: one born in Arcadia, son of Aether; another, son of Caelus, who fathered Minerva; and a third, son of the Cretan Saturn, whose tomb was shown in Crete. Hyginus, following a more Greek tradition, makes him the son of Saturn and Ops, with five siblings: Vesta, Pluto, Ceres, Juno and Neptune.

His offspring are numerous. According to Hyginus, Minerva was born from his head; with Juno he had Mars, Juventas, Libertas and Vulcan; with Maia, Mercury; and with Latona, Apollo and Diana. Other Roman authors add lineages without exact parallels in Greek myth, such as Cupid, son of Venus.

Jupiter's myth largely follows that of Zeus, with borrowings from Etruscan mythology and native Latin elements. He was originally a god of the sky, weather and the agricultural cycles. Later he became protector of the league of Latin cities and, over time, guarantor of justice, law and the authority of the Roman state. He kept the thunderbolt as his emblem and, like Zeus, became the god of gods.

Worship and religion

The cult of Jupiter, probably of Sabine origin, was introduced to Rome by Numa Pompilius. His great temple stood on the Capitoline Hill, where he was worshipped as Iuppiter Optimus Maximus, protector of the City, from whom authority, laws and social order flowed. During the Republic, consuls prayed to him at the start of their term and swore their oath of office in his name. Every September, annual feriae were held in his honour on the Capitol, with the sacrifice of a white ox with gilded horns. Triumphal generals laid the tokens of their victory at the feet of his statue.

His priest was the flamen Dialis, a patrician, and his wife, the flaminica, presided over the sacrifice of a ram on each nundinae. The flamen had unusual ritual rules, such as removing his clothes only under a roof so as not to appear naked before the sky. In the Empire, several emperors made Jupiter their patron or took on his attributes. Augustus claimed to receive dreams sent by him, and Caligula called himself Optimus Maximus. Today some pagan reconstructionist groups, such as the Roman Way and Nova Roma, honour him in an effort to revive ancient Roman religion.

Legacy

The name Jupiter comes from an Indo-European root meaning light, joined to pater, father, that is, father of light. From the same root come Latin deus, the source of the English word god, and the name Iovis, Jove, from which the word jovial derives. The eagle, his protecting bird, became one of the most common symbols of the Roman army and frequently appears on Greek and Roman coins, often holding a thunderbolt.

Ancient sources: Cicero, who sets out the theologians' doctrine of three Jupiters, and Varro, who quotes Ennius, mention him in detail; Hyginus gives his genealogy and offspring, and the Vatican Mythographers preserve other versions.

Trivia

A reserved chariot. Camillus was sent into exile after driving a chariot drawn by four white horses, an honour reserved for Jupiter himself.
Never naked under the sky. The flamen Dialis could remove his clothes or his pointed hat only under a roof, so as not to appear naked before the god of the heavens.
Thunder and ritual. Whenever the flaminica saw a lightning bolt or heard thunder, she had to stop her routine until she had appeased the god.
