Indo-Iranian god of covenant and friendship, and of the Roman mystery cult

Mitra

Mitra is an Indo-Iranian divinity linked to covenants, friendship and truth, attested in the Rigveda and the Avesta. Later his figure reached Persia and the Greco-Roman world, where it gave rise to Mithraism, a mystery cult that spread across the Roman Empire.

The myth

According to the ancient texts, Mitra guards agreements and truth. In the Rigveda he is closely tied to Varuna, and both protect rta, the cosmic order; Mitra presides over contracts and assemblies. In the Avesta he is one of the ahuras of Zoroastrianism, described as having wide pastures, a thousand ears and ten thousand eyes, always awake (Yasht 10). He judges oaths and keeps from paradise anyone who breaks his word.

He is associated with the sun, yet scholars agree he is not the sun. His name comes from an old common noun meaning covenant or agreement, not from light. In Hellenistic Asia Minor he merged with Apollo, Helios and Hermes, and in Rome he became Mithras, best known from images of the young god killing a bull.

Worship and religion

In the Roman world, the cult of Mithras was organized in secret initiatory communities open only to men, with several grades of initiation. It was especially popular among soldiers and reached as far north as Hadrian's Wall and the Germanic limes. Most of its images, mainly from the 3rd century, show the god in a Phrygian cap sacrificing a bull. Few texts were written by Mithraists themselves, so much of what we know comes from paintings, inscriptions and the criticism of rivals such as Neoplatonists and Christians. Beneath the church of San Clemente in Rome, a temple dedicated to Mithras was discovered. According to some sources, Constantine I remained a follower until the end of his life. The cult does not survive today as a religion.

Legacy

Mithras has left a mark on the study of ancient mystery religions and on debates about the origins of Christianity. In Spain, the Mithras of Cabra, a white marble sculptural group now displayed in the Archaeological Museum of Córdoba, is among the best-known pieces, and a Mithraic temple was found in Lugo beside a Roman house. His name also appears in Maitreya, a bodhisattva of Buddhism, though that connection remains a hypothesis.

Ancient sources: The Rigveda, where he appears alongside Varuna; the Avesta (Yasht 10); Plutarch, who in Isis and Osiris presents him as a mediator within Zoroastrianism; and the Hittite-Mitanni peace treaty of around 1400 BC, which names him as a witness to the pact.

Trivia

A name that means covenant. It comes from an Indo-Iranian common noun meaning covenant or agreement. In modern Sanskrit, mitra means friend.
A witness to a treaty. He appears in a peace treaty of around 1400 BC between the Hittites and the Mitanni as a witness and guarantor of the pact, together with four other Indic deities.
