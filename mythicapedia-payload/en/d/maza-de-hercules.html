Roman and Germanic amulet linked to Hercules

Hercules' Club

Hercules' Club is an amulet type from the Roman Empire and the Migration era, shaped like a wooden club and most often made of gold in Roman times. Among the Germanic peoples it was linked to Donar, whom the Romans identified with Hercules.

The myth

According to Tacitus, the Germans claimed that Hercules had once visited them, and they sang of him before any other hero when going into battle. A piece found in Cologne bears the inscription DEO HER, confirming the link with the hero.

From the 2nd to the 3rd century, examples appear across the empire, including Roman Britain, in two sizes: a small one cast in molds and a larger one of sheet metal. During the Germanic migrations of the 5th to 7th centuries the type spread from the Elbe region across Europe, made of antler, bone or wood, almost always in female graves, where it was worn as a belt or ear pendant.

Worship and religion

It worked mainly as a personal protective amulet and object of devotion. A bronze votive mace found at Willingham Fen, Cambridgeshire, combines the Roman form with Celtic iconography such as animal heads and a wheel.

Legacy

In the Viking Age it was replaced by Thor's hammer pendants as Scandinavia converted to Christianity between the 8th and 9th centuries.

Ancient sources: Tacitus, in the Germania, describes the special affinity of the Germans for Hercules.

Trivia

Song before battle. Tacitus says the Germans sang of Hercules before any other hero when they went into battle.
Mace with Celtic imagery. The bronze votive mace from Willingham Fen mixes the Roman model with Celtic motifs such as animal heads and a wheel at its base.
