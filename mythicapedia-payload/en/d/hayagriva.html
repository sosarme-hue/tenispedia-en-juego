Hindu god of knowledge with a horse's head

Hayagriva

Hayagriva is a Hindu deity, regarded as a horse-headed avatar of Vishnu with a human body, linked to wisdom and learning. He also appears in Tibetan Buddhism, where he takes a fierce form associated with Amitabha. He matters for his role as the guardian of Vedic knowledge and for the devotion he receives from students and Vaishnava worshippers.

The myth

According to Hindu tradition, the demons Madhu and Kaitabha seized the Vedas from Brahma. To recover them, Vishnu took the form of Hayagriva, and in other versions he compiled the Vedas in this form at the time of creation. The Puranas tell that Hayagriva cut the bodies of the two demons into six parts each.

Another story, from the Shakta tradition, tells of a demon named Hayagriva, son of the patriarch Kashyapa, who had received a blessing from the goddess Durga that he could only die at the hands of another Hayagriva. Vishnu fought him without defeating him, and afterwards, resting, was beheaded by the snapping bowstring after termites had gnawed through it. The gods asked Durga for help, and Brahma sacrificed a white horse and attached its head to Vishnu, who then slew the demon.

Worship and religion

He is worshipped as the guide of knowledge and the patron of those who begin their studies, sacred or secular, asking for his blessing. A special festival falls on the August full moon (Shravana Purnima), the day of his descent, and on Mahanavami, the ninth day of the Navaratri festival. He is an important deity in Vaishnava traditions, including Sri Vaishnavism, and has temples across many Indian states.

Legacy

His image has been linked, in some interpretations, to the horse-like tikbalang of Philippine mythology. A Mongolian dinosaur genus was named Haya griva for its elongated skull and for the deity's presence in Mongolian Buddhist art.

Ancient sources: The Puranas, the Pancharatra texts and the Bhagavata Purana mention him, along with the Vedas and the Mahabharata.

In art

Trivia

A dinosaur with his name. An extinct basal neornithischian dinosaur from Mongolia was named Haya griva, referring to its elongated, horse-like skull and the deity's presence in Mongolian Buddhist art.
A devotee and an offering. According to tradition, the philosopher Vadirajatirtha offered horse gram to Hayagriva, and the deity appeared as a white horse to eat it.
