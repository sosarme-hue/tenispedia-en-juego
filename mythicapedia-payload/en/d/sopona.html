Yoruba god of smallpox

Sopona

Sopona is the Yoruba god of smallpox, a disease believed in traditional belief to be sent to humans as divine punishment. He is a vengeful aspect of the deity Obaluaye, and his worship spread to the Americas with the slave trade, where he became known under names such as Babalú-Ayé or Omolu.

The myth

In traditional Yoruba belief in Nigeria, smallpox was a disease inflicted on humans through Sopona's divine displeasure. His true name, Ṣọ̀pọ̀na, is considered secret and taboo, so it was avoided out of respect for his power, and he was addressed through euphemisms such as Omolu.

Among the Dahomean and Gbe-speaking peoples such as the Ewe, Fon and Adja, he is known as Sakpata, a divinity of the earth, wellness and smallpox who can heal or inflict disease and madness. According to oral history, the disease decimated their ancestors until they were cured by a Yoruba man called Torgbui Anyi.

Worship and religion

His worship was controlled by priests who looked after his shrines, and people believed these priests could cause outbreaks if angered. Early in the twentieth century, after a doctor suspected them of spreading the disease, British colonial authorities banned his worship in 1907, yet the faithful continued to honour him. In Candomblé he survives today as Sakpata-Omolu, linked to straw garments, ritual brooms and the colours red, black and white.

Legacy

In Brazil, Candomblé also associates him today with skin and contagious diseases, including HIV/AIDS.
