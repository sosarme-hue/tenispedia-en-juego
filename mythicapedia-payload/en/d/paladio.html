sacred statue of Athena, guardian of Troy and Rome

Palladium

The Palladium was an ancient wooden statue of Athena in Greek and Roman mythology, on which the safety of Troy and later Rome was said to depend. Its story, full of thefts, transfers and conflicting versions, explains why the word has come to mean any source of protection.

The myth

According to the tradition recorded by Apollodorus, Athena made the image herself in memory of Pallas, a childhood companion she killed by accident during weapons training. The statue fell from the sky in front of the tent of Ilus, founder of Ilion, and was taken as a sign that the city would be impregnable while the image remained there. Ilus was blinded when he saved it from a fire, since no mortal was allowed to look upon it, and he regained his sight after appeasing the goddess.

During the Trojan War, Helenus revealed to the Greeks that the city would not fall without the Palladium. Odysseus and Diomedes stole it, and the best-known story tells how Odysseus, on the way to the ships, tried to stab Diomedes in the back; Diomedes saw the gleam of the sword in the moonlight, disarmed him and drove him ahead. From this arose the proverb «Diomedes' necessity» for acting under compulsion. Later accounts differ: Aeneas carried it to Italy according to the Roman tradition, or Diomedes kept it himself.

Worship and religion

In Athens, the goddess was worshipped as Athena Polias, protectress of the city, and her olivewood image, regarded as of divine origin, was housed in the Erechtheum. During the Panathenaea her robe was replaced, and at the Plynteria the statue was ceremonially washed in the sea. In Rome, an object regarded as the true Palladium was kept for centuries in the Temple of Vesta, in the care of the Vestal Virgins, as one of the sacred pledges of Roman rule.

Legacy

Since around 1600, the word «palladium» has been used figuratively for anything believed to provide protection or safety. In Christian contexts, especially in the Eastern church after Justinian I, sacred relics or icons believed to protect a city or nation were given this name, and were sometimes carried in procession around besieged walls.

Ancient sources: Homer and the Epic Cycle (through the Little Iliad), Apollodorus, Virgil's Aeneid, Ovid, Plutarch and Pausanias mention the Palladium in different versions.

Trivia

Diomedes' necessity. The Greek proverb arose from the episode in which Diomedes forced Odysseus to walk ahead of him at sword point. It describes doing something under compulsion rather than by choice.
A blinded priest. According to Pliny the Elder, Lucius Caecilius Metellus was blinded when he rescued the Palladium from a fire in the Temple of Vesta in 241 BC.
