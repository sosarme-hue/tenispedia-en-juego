Maya sun god

K'inich Ahau

K'inich Ahau, the Maya sun god, is known in Yucatec sources as the lord with sun-facing eyes. He was the patron of music and poetry, and his figure is closely tied to kingship and to the cycles of the day and the year.

The myth

In Maya tradition, K'inich Ahau ruled over the gods, settling disputes and distributing lands among the peoples. Some sources link him to Ixchel, the Moon, as his wife, and consider him an aspect of Itzamna.

Recent Maya myths focus mainly on the sun's childhood and on the trials that lead to his transformation into the solar body. Among the southern Lacandons, K'inich Ahau is the elder brother of the upper god and, according to them, he will end this world by descending from the sky so that his jaguars devour humanity. The sun travels through the underworld carried on the shoulders of its lord, and in some images he paddles a canoe toward an aquatic paradise in the east.

Worship and religion

His priests, called Ah Kin, priests of the Sun and of Time, foretold the future of people. In sixteenth-century Yucatán his name marked one of the four years of the 52-year cycle, and the rites that opened that year included war dances. Offerings recorded in the sources include egg yolks and deer hearts. His solar rituals are poorly known, although he appears often in the Dresden Codex, a book largely devoted to ritual matters.

Legacy

His image appears on stelae, ceramic vessels and carved stone set beside temples, such as the Temple of the Sun at Palenque. A Maya funerary urn bearing his name is kept in the Museo de América in Madrid, and the archaeological site known as Grupo Kinich Ahau is studied in Cancún.

Ancient sources: The Dresden Codex depicts him in its ritual pages, and Diego de Landa mentions him in his sixteenth-century Relación de las cosas de Yucatán.

Trivia

Sun-facing eyes. His name combines k'in, meaning both sun and day, with ich, meaning face or eyes, and ajaw, meaning lord. The sun glyph even appears inserted into his body.
God G. In the codices he is known as God G, following the classification proposed by the German scholar Paul Schellhas.
