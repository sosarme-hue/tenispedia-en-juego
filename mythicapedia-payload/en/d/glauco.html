Greek sea god

Glaucus

Glaucus is a minor sea deity of Greek mythology, known as a prophet and as a protector of sailors and fishermen during storms. According to the myth, he was a mortal fisherman who became immortal after eating a magical herb, and he is best known through Ovid's version of the story.

The myth

According to tradition, Glaucus was a fisherman from Anthedon, in Boeotia, who made his living from the sea. One day he noticed that fish he had just caught came back to life when they touched a certain herb. He tried it himself, and the herb made him immortal, but it also changed his body: fins grew in place of arms and a fish's tail replaced his legs, so he could no longer live on land.

He plunged into the sea, where Oceanus and Tethys welcomed him, purified him of his human nature and taught him the art of prophecy. From then on he helped sailors in peril. In Apollonius Rhodius's Argonautica he appears during the voyage to reconcile Telamon and Jason and to foretell the fates of Heracles and Polyphemus. Other versions make Glaucus a member of the Argonauts himself.

His best-known episode is his love for the nymph Scylla. She rejected him in horror at his sea-creature appearance, so Glaucus turned to the witch Circe for help. Circe fell in love with him and offered her own affection, but Glaucus refused. Out of spite, she poisoned the waters where Scylla bathed and turned her into a monster.

Legacy

The composer Jean-Marie Leclair wrote the opera Scylla et Glaucus, based on Ovid's account. John Keats retold the story of Glaucus and Circe in Book III of his poem Endymion. In the Republic, Plato compares a corrupted soul to a body of Glaucus crusted with seaweed, rocks and barnacles.

Ancient sources: Ovid recounts it in Book XIII of the Metamorphoses, and Apollonius of Rhodes includes him in the Argonautica.

Trivia

A god born from a fisherman. According to Ovid, Glaucus was a mortal fisherman who became immortal after tasting a magical herb that revived fish.
A variable genealogy. Ancient sources give Glaucus different parents, including Poseidon, Nereus or Polybus, son of Hermes.
