early Roman state god, later identified with Romulus

Quirinus

Quirinus was an early god of the Roman state, probably rooted in Sabine war worship. Later he came to be identified with Romulus, the legendary first king of Rome, after the founder's mysterious disappearance, so his figure links official religion with the city's founding myth.

The myth

Quirinus most likely began as a Sabine war god. The Sabines had a settlement near the future site of Rome and raised an altar to him on the hill that would later be called the Quirinal.

The best-known story concerns Romulus. According to tradition, one night the sky darkened and, amid thunder and lightning, the city's founder was snatched away by the gods. Later, Plutarch recorded in his Life of Romulus the account of a Roman noble, Proculus Julius, who claimed that the king had appeared to him while he was travelling. Romulus had supposedly told him to inform the Romans that he was now Quirinus. Another version holds that the nobles killed and dismembered him, and each took a piece of his body home to bury on their land.

The name also had competing explanations. Ovid suggested it meant 'wielder of the spear', while others linked it to the Sabine town of Cures. The Greeks equated him with Ares the War-Bringer.

Worship and religion

Quirinus belonged to the Archaic Triad alongside Jupiter and Mars, and his cult was overseen by the Flamen Quirinalis, one of the three patrician major flamens, who held precedence over the Pontifex Maximus. His main festival, the Quirinalia, was held on 17 February, a day linked to Romulus's traditional death and to offerings of spelt to the goddess Fornax. As personal and mystical cults such as those of Bacchus, Cybele and Isis grew in appeal, his worship lost ground until little remained beyond the flamen who tended him.

Legacy

His name survives in the Quirinal hill, where in 1583 Pope Gregory XIII chose the site for his summer palace, which came to be known as the Quirinal Palace. After the Capture of Rome it became the seat of the House of Savoy's kingdom, and after the abolition of the monarchy it became the residence of the Presidents of the Italian Republic.

Ancient sources: Ovid discusses him in his Fasti, Plutarch in his Life of Romulus, and the poet Ennius, in the 2nd century BC, already identified him with Romulus.

Trivia

The Quirinal Palace. The palace Pope Gregory XIII commissioned on the hill later took the name Quirinal Palace. Centuries later it housed the Italian monarchy, and today it is the residence of the President of the Republic.
Hercules Quirinus. The name Quirinus also appears as a surname of Hercules, in the form Hercules Quirinus.
