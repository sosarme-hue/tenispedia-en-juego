personification of the protective phallus in Roman religion

Fascinus

Fascinus, or fascinum, was the personification of the divine phallus in ancient Roman religion and magic. People invoked him as a protector against envy and the evil eye, especially for children and victorious generals.

The myth

Roman tradition presented him as an embodiment of masculine generative power tied to the hearth, regarded as sacred. A myth about the begetting of Servius Tullius suggests this link with the domestic fire.

According to tradition, the Vestal Virgins guarded the image of the phallus, the fascinus populi Romani, which was one of the tokens of the state's safety alongside the Palladium. When a general celebrated a triumph, an effigy of the fascinus was hung beneath his chariot to shield him from envy.

Worship and religion

The Vestal Virgins tended his cult within public religion. According to Augustine of Hippo, who drew on the lost works of Varro, a phallic image was carried in procession each year at the festival of Father Liber to protect the fields from magical enchantment.

Legacy

Phallic amulets, often winged, were everywhere in Roman daily life, appearing in jewellery, wind chimes and lamps. The motif of a phallus striking an eye appears in reliefs from Leptis Magna. The English word fascinate derives from Latin fascinum, and the Fescennine Verses may owe their name to the same root.

Ancient sources: Pliny the Elder called him a medicus invidiae, a remedy for envy and the evil eye, while Varro and Augustine of Hippo refer to his procession and protective role.

In art

Trivia

Fist amulet. Fist-and-phallus amulets, widespread among soldiers, often show a hand making the figa, a sign of good luck. The largest known collection comes from Camulodunum.
Phallic charms for children. Some phallus rings were too small to be worn by anyone but children. Pliny mentions the custom of hanging a phallic charm around a baby's neck.
