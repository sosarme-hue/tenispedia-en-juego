Greek Muse of sacred hymns

Polyhymnia

Polyhymnia is one of the nine Muses of Greek mythology, linked to sacred poetry, hymns and eloquence. Her name means "many hymns", and she is seen as the patron of those who praise the gods and of those who speak with persuasive skill.

The myth

According to Greek tradition, Polyhymnia was a daughter of Zeus and Mnemosyne, the Titaness of memory, and like her sisters she was born to inspire poets and artists. Hesiod and other ancient writers place her among the nine Muses, alongside Clio, Calliope, Melpomene, Urania, Euterpe, Terpsichore, Erato and Thalia.

Sources credit her with many domains: dance, harmony, rhetoric, the lyre and pantomime, which some accounts describe as an art that "speaks with the hand and the gesture". Some stories also link her to knowledge and a good memory. The people of Sicyon called her Polymatheia, "many learnings", one of three Muses they named that way.

Genealogies vary by author. Apollo is said to have fathered Linus, Hymenaeus and Yalemus, songs of lament, wedding and sorrow, with one of the Muses, though their identities were not preserved. Other versions make her the mother of Triptolemus, by Cheimarrhus son of Ares, or by Celeus, and others make her the mother of the musician Orpheus by Oeagrus. Diodorus Siculus wrote that she brought immortal fame to writers whose works praised the gods.

Worship and religion

On Mount Parnassus there was a spring sacred to the Muses, said to flow between two large rocks above Delphi and then into a broad basin. The Pythia, the priestesses of the oracle, used its water for divination.

Legacy

A main belt asteroid discovered in 1854 by the French astronomer Jean Chacornac bears her name. In art she is shown dressed in white or veiled, pensive, with a finger to her lips, and sometimes with chains, a symbol of the power of eloquence. These images appear in paintings and sculptures from the Renaissance to the nineteenth century.

Ancient sources: Hesiod counts her among the nine Muses, daughters of Zeus and Mnemosyne, and Diodorus Siculus describes her role in bringing fame to writers in his Bibliotheca historica.
