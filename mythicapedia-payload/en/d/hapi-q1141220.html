Egyptian god, one of the four Sons of Horus

Hapy

Hapy is one of the four Sons of Horus, Egyptian funerary spirits who guarded the internal organs of the dead. He was associated with the lungs and, from the New Kingdom onward, was mostly shown with a baboon's head.

The myth

Funerary texts present him as a son of Horus, although some sources make him a son of Osiris, Atum, Geb or Nut, and the Coffin Texts describe him as the offspring of Isis and Horus the Elder. In the Pyramid Texts, from the late Old Kingdom, the Sons of Horus help the dead king climb into the sky and protect him from hunger and thirst. Later tradition also gives them the task of guarding Osiris: in the Dramatic Ramesseum Papyrus they help his rebirth, fight the followers of Set and return the lost Eye of Horus to their father.

Their best-known role is guarding the lungs, preserved in a canopic jar whose lid bears his head. By common arrangement he is protected by the goddess Nephthys and linked with the north. The Egyptologist James P. Allen translates his name as He of Haste.

Worship and religion

His veneration was almost entirely tied to funerary practice, and the sources do not describe temples or festivals dedicated to him as a public cult deity. He appears on burial equipment, coffins and sarcophagi until the fourth century AD, when the ancient Egyptian funerary tradition ended. He has no living cult.

Ancient sources: Pyramid Texts, Coffin Texts, Book of the Dead (Spell 137), Dramatic Ramesseum Papyrus and Book of Gates.

Trivia

A name meaning haste. According to James P. Allen, his name translates as He of Haste. His brother Imsety has been linked to dill, an herb.
Four brothers, four animals. In the New Kingdom, Hapy appears with a baboon's head, Duamutef as a jackal, Qebehsenuef as a falcon and Imsety in human form.
