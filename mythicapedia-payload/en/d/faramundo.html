legendary first king of the Salian Franks

Pharamond

Pharamond is, according to a late medieval tradition, the first king of the Salian Franks, placed in the age of the great migrations. He matters because his figure gave the Franks a past of their own and a single royal origin.

The myth

According to the Liber Historiae Francorum, after the death of Sunno, his brother Marcomer, leader of the Ampsivarii and the Chatti, proposed that the people have a single king, contrary to Frankish tradition. The Franks agreed, and Pharamond, Marcomer's son, was chosen. He is described as a young man with long hair, a detail that links him to the later Merovingian kings, his descendants. When he died, his son Chlodio succeeded him.

No source earlier than the eighth century mentions him, and Gregory of Tours, who does describe several contemporary Germanic chieftains, never names him. Modern historians therefore regard him as a legendary figure, perhaps modelled on a real tribal leader or on a symbolic ancestor's name.

Legacy

Shakespeare names him in Henry V as the originator of the Salic law, which barred women from succeeding to the French throne. Handel wrote the opera Faramondo about him. Victor Hugo mentions him in The Hunchback of Notre-Dame, comparing Quasimodo's wound to a scratch that would not have troubled King Pharamond. He also appears in the Sandman and Lucifer comics, as a playable faction leader in the video game Total War: Attila, and as the ancestor of a character in Jean Raspail's novel Sire.

Ancient sources: The eighth-century Liber Historiae Francorum is the first work to mention him, and it largely copies the Chronicle of Fredegar.

Trivia

A Handel opera. George Frideric Handel composed the opera Faramondo, which takes the legendary Frankish king as its title character.
A line from Victor Hugo. In The Hunchback of Notre-Dame, Hugo compares a wound suffered by Quasimodo to a scratch that would not trouble King Pharamond.
