Japanese god of war and archery

Hachiman

Hachiman is a Japanese deity of the Shinto tradition, shaped by Buddhist syncretism, regarded as the god of war and archery and the tutelary protector of warriors, the Japanese people and the Imperial House. He is identified with Emperor Ōjin, which makes him a key figure in Japan's religious history.

The myth

The two oldest chronicles of Japan, the Kojiki and the Nihonshoki, do not mention Hachiman, so his origin remains unclear. Tradition links him with Emperor Ōjin, son of Empress Jingū. According to the legend, Ōjin was still in his mother's womb when she urged the conquest of Korea, and he was not born until three years later, when the military campaign had ended. His name, 'God of the Eight Banners', refers to eight heavenly banners that announced his birth.

A shrine account from the late Heian period tells that Hachiman appeared as a three-year-old boy in the Usa region of Kyūshū and declared himself Emperor Ōjin. After Buddhism arrived in Japan, he was absorbed into the Buddhist pantheon and came to be seen as a protective bodhisattva of the country.

Worship and religion

About 2,500 shrines are dedicated to Hachiman, second in number only to those of Inari. The chief one is Usa Shrine in Ōita Prefecture, and other important shrines include Iwashimizu, Hakozaki and Tsurugaoka. He was especially popular with samurai, above all the Minamoto clan, which adopted him as its protector, and his worship later spread among the peasantry. Many shrines keep a stirrup or a bow as their shintai. His worship remains alive, and many believers still visit his shrines.

Legacy

Many Japanese towns and villages named Hachiman, Yawata or Yahata grew up around his shrines. His emblem, the mitsudomoe, was adopted by several samurai clans, including some that claimed descent from Emperor Kanmu.

Ancient sources: The Kojiki and the Nihonshoki do not mention him; his earliest references appear in shrine accounts from the late Heian period.

Trivia

Eight banners. His name comes from the eight heavenly banners that signaled his birth. It is said that Empress Jingū set up eight flags on Tsushima as an offering after the expedition to Korea.
A whirlpool emblem. His mon is the mitsudomoe, a round whirlpool with three heads that swirls either clockwise or counterclockwise.
