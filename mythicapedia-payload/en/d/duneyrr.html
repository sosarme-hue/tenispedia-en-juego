one of the four stags of the world tree Yggdrasil

Duneyrr

Duneyrr is one of the four stags that graze among the branches of the world tree Yggdrasil in Norse mythology. His name appears in the Poetic Edda and the Prose Edda, and his figure helps explain the origin of the world's rivers.

The myth

According to the poem Grímnismál, part of the Poetic Edda, four stags feed among the shoots of the world tree, Yggdrasil. They stretch their necks upward to nibble at its branches, and the morning dew gathers in their horns and becomes the rivers of the world. The four are named Dáinn, Dvalinn, Duneyrr and Duraþrór, and Duneyrr is usually rendered as "Thundering in the Ear".

Snorri Sturluson summarizes the same stanza in the Gylfaginning section of his Prose Edda. The word barr, most often used for the needles of fir or pine trees, has caused debate over whether the tree was really a conifer or whether the word simply meant foliage in general.

Some scholars think the stanzas naming the stags are later additions, and that there may originally have been just one stag, perhaps the Eikþyrnir mentioned earlier in the same poem.

Ancient sources: Grímnismál in the Poetic Edda, and Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda, in its Gylfaginning section.

Trivia

Stags who were winds. In 1824, Finnur Magnússon proposed that the four stags stood for winds, based on their names. Dáinn and Dvalinn would be calm winds, and Duneyrr and Duraþrór heavy ones.
A single stag at first. Finnur Jónsson suggested that there was originally only one stag, which was later turned into four, probably one on each side of the tree.
