Japanese sea goddess, dragon princess

Toyotama-hime

Toyotama-hime, also called Otohime, is a dragon princess of the seas in Japanese mythology. Daughter of the sea deity Watatsumi, she is remembered above all for her love story with the prince Hoori and for being the mother of Ugayafukiaezu, father of Jimmu, the first emperor of Japan.

The myth

According to the tale, Hoori, also called Yamasachi, a hunter, went searching under the sea for a fishing hook he had borrowed from his elder brother Umisachi. In the depths he met Toyotama-hime, who lived in a palace said to be built of fish scales. Watatsumi recognised the prince as a descendant of the heavenly gods, held a banquet, and the couple married and lived together for three years.

When Hoori remembered his quest, the hook was found in the throat of a sea bream, and the prince returned to dry land with his wife, who announced she was pregnant. He built her a birthing hut thatched with cormorant feathers, still unfinished, and she asked him not to look at her during the birth. He disobeyed and saw her transformed into a huge crocodile, or a dragon in one Nihon Shoki version, cradling the newborn. Ashamed at his betrayal, Toyotama returned to the sea and left her younger sister Tamayori to raise the child.

Legacy

She inspires the dragon-human hybrids common in Japanese video games such as Popolocrois, Fire Emblem and Breath of Fire. A character named Toyotama appears in the anime Sekirei, and Samantha Shannon's novel The Priory of the Orange Tree is loosely based on the legend. In science, an extinct crocodile genus, Toyotamaphimeia, was named after the goddess in direct reference to the myth.

Ancient sources: She appears in the Kojiki and the Nihon Shoki, which recount her story with Hoori and the birth of their son.

Trivia

Meaning of the name. Her name is believed to mean a miko who draws divine spirits with rich pearls, since toyo means rich and tama means pearl.
A widely shared tale. The folklorist Hiroko Ikeda classified the myth as type 470C, The Lost Fish Hook, and notes similar tales among peoples along the shores of the Pacific.
