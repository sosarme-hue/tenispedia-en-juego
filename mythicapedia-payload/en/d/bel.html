Mesopotamian divine title meaning 'Lord'

Bel

Bel is a title meaning 'lord' or 'master' that was applied to several gods in Mesopotamia, above all to Marduk, the chief god of Babylon. Its feminine form, Belit, means 'Lady'.

The myth

In Mesopotamian texts, Bel works more as an epithet than as a personal name, so it does not always refer to the same god. Over time it became associated mainly with Marduk, which is why it appears in Assyrian and Neo-Babylonian personal names. Belit was the epithet of Sarpanit, Marduk's wife, while Sumerian goddesses such as Ninhursag were called Belit-ili, 'Lady of the Gods'.

The Greeks transliterated the name as Belos and fitted the figure into their own mythology. Sanchuniathon mentions a Zeus Belus, son of Cronus, who probably refers to Marduk. In the Bible, Bel appears in Isaiah, Jeremiah and chapter 14 of Daniel, in a satirical tone that announces the downfall of this god of Israel's oppressors.

Worship and religion

According to Arrian, after Babylon surrendered to Alexander the Great, he ordered the rebuilding of the temples destroyed by Xerxes, especially that of Bel, whom the Babylonians revered more than any other god. In Palmyra, Bel was the chief god; he was first called Bol, and by 213 BC he had been renamed Bel. His sanctuary later became a church and then a mosque, and its remains were destroyed by ISIS in 2015. The sources do not document any current cult of Bel.

Legacy

In Mandaean cosmology, the name for Jupiter is Bil, derived from Bel. Early translators of Akkadian read the ideogram for Enlil as Bel, a reading now considered incorrect. Bel is also an East Semitic form cognate with the Northwest Semitic Baal, which has the same meaning.

Ancient sources: The biblical books of Isaiah, Jeremiah and Daniel, Arrian's Anabasis of Alexander, and the work of Sanchuniathon.
