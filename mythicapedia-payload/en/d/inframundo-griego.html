realm of the dead in Greek mythology

Greek underworld

The Greek underworld, also called Hades after its patron god, is the dark, sunless realm where, in Greek tradition, the dead go after dying. It is one of the most depicted places in Greek mythology and the setting of some of its best-known stories.

The myth

In the earliest ideas of the afterlife, at the moment of death the individual's psyche, or essence, separates from the body and is carried to the underworld. In Homer's Iliad and Odyssey the dead are grouped together without distinction and lead a shadowy, faint existence. Later, in authors such as Plato, the idea of a judgment after death emerges, separating good people from bad.

The realm takes its name from its god, Hades, and was located at the edges of the world, beside Oceanus, or beneath the earth. Several routes led down to it, such as the Taenarum headland or Avernus, and the ferryman Charon, who appears mostly in later art, carried the dead across a river. It was believed that those without a proper funeral could not cross, as with Patroclus and Hector in the Iliad.

The underworld had distinct zones. The Asphodel Meadows held most of the dead. Tartarus, in some sources a synonym for the underworld and in others a separate realm, was the deepest abyss, home to the Titans and to punishment for wrongdoers such as Sisyphus and Tantalus. The Elysian Fields were a paradise reserved for a few chosen figures, such as Menelaus, Zeus's son-in-law, according to the Odyssey. Rivers such as the Styx, Acheron, Pyriphlegethon, Cocytus and Lethe surrounded the realm.

Some heroes, such as Orpheus, Heracles, Theseus and Odysseus, managed to descend in a katabasis and return alive. For mortals in general the underworld was a place of no return.

Legacy

Hades has lent its name to the realm of the dead in the Western imagination. The word 'underworld' in English is a later coinage, not a term used in classical Latin or medieval times.

Ancient sources: Homer's Iliad and Odyssey, Hesiod's Works and Days and Plato's Gorgias describe the underworld and its inhabitants.

Trivia

Gatekeeper of Hades. One of the epithets of the god Hades was 'gate fastener', and the underworld was famous for its gates.
Rivers named after feelings. The Styx was also called the river of hatred, the Acheron the river of woe and the Cocytus the river of wailing, reflecting the meaning of their names.
