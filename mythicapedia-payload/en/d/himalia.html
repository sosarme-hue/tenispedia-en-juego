nymph of Rhodes in Greek mythology

Himalia

Himalia is a nymph from the eastern end of the island of Rhodes in Greek mythology, who was loved by Zeus and became the mother of three sons. Her story links a god's love with the fertility of grain and with a flood that submerged the island.

The myth

According to Diodorus Siculus, after Zeus defeated the Titans, he fell in love with Himalia, who lived at the eastern end of Rhodes, where the so-called Giants dwelt. He came to her in the form of a fertile rain, and the nymph bore him three sons: Spartaeus, Cronius and Cytus. Beyond their names, nothing more about them survives.

Later a flood drowned the island of Rhodes. Himalia and the sons of Zeus were saved by taking refuge in the highest places on the island, so the tale joins divine love with a catastrophe that only the family survives.

The names of the sons have been read as allusions to growing wheat: Spartaeus would recall sowing, Cronius the ripening, and Cytus, in some readings, a basket or a baker. The nymph's own name, which in Greek means miller or grain-grinder, fits this agricultural reading, though these are modern interpretations and their original sense is uncertain.

Clement of Alexandria gives another version. He says that Zeus seduced a woman called Imandra, daughter of Geneanus, in Rhodes, taking the form of a shower of rain. Because the names have come down corrupted, Imandra is probably a scribal corruption of Himalia, an example of how the names in one story could shift from one ancient author to another.

Ancient sources: Diodorus Siculus and Clement of Alexandria record her story, and the lexicographer Hesychius of Alexandria glosses the term Himalia as an abundance of wheat meal.

Trivia

A telling name. The name Himalia is linked to a Greek word for a miller, a term tied to grain and flour.
Love as rain. In the sources, Zeus approaches the nymph as a fertile rain, an image that recurs in other stories of Greek tradition.
