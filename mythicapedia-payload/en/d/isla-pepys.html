phantom island in the South Atlantic

Pepys Island

Pepys Island is a phantom island once believed to lie north of the Falklands. Its story, rooted in seventeenth-century British seafaring, makes it one of the most famous geographical mysteries of the South Atlantic.

The myth

The first account comes from the British corsair Ambrose Cowley, who in December 1683, while sailing around the world aboard the Bachelor's Delight, claimed to have sighted an unknown, uninhabited island at about 47 degrees south. He named it after Samuel Pepys, Secretary to the Admiralty. His log describes woods, an excellent harbour and countless birds.

His companion William Dampier linked the sighting to the Sebaldinas, a name then used for the Falklands. Some scholars believe Cowley simply misidentified the Falklands, while others suggest a mirage or a distant iceberg breaking off Antarctica. Throughout the eighteenth century, expeditions led by Anson, Byron, Cook and Bougainville searched for it without success.

Legacy

The name became part of the history of cartography and of the legend of phantom islands. In the nineteenth century, the historian Pedro de Angelis tried to revive interest in it, though he never settled its position.

Ancient sources: The seventeenth-century logs of Ambrose Cowley and William Dampier are the original accounts.

Trivia

Named after an official. The island was named after Samuel Pepys, Secretary to the British Admiralty, in whose honour Cowley christened it.
Other names. It was also called Pepina, and Dampier linked it to the Sebaldinas, an old name for the Falklands.
