Tantalus

Tantalus is a figure of Greek mythology, said to be a son of Zeus or of Tmolus depending on the source, and best known for his eternal punishment in Tartarus. He matters because his descendants, the House of Atreus, appear in many of the great Greek tragedies.

The myth

According to tradition, Tantalus was a son of Zeus and Pluto, although some authors make Tmolus his father. He ruled in Lydia or Phrygia, in western Asia Minor, and was received as a guest at the gods' banquets. The stories disagree about his offence: some say he revealed the secrets he heard on Olympus, others that he stole nectar and ambrosia to share with his friends, and others that he tested the gods' knowledge by serving them his own son, Pelops.

In the best-known version, Tantalus dismembered Pelops, boiled him and served him to the guests. Only Demeter, grieving for Persephone, ate a shoulder. Zeus brought the boy back to life, giving him a shoulder of ivory made by Hephaestus. For his crime, Tantalus was punished in Tartarus with endless hunger and thirst: he stood in water that receded when he tried to drink, beneath a tree whose fruit pulled away from his hand. In other variants, a huge rock hangs over his head, threatening to crush him.

Legacy

His name gives English the verb "tantalize", meaning to torment someone with the sight of something desired but out of reach. The Greeks used the phrase "Tantalean punishment" for people who have good things they cannot enjoy. Pindar and Archilochus used "the rock of Tantalus" as an image of constant peril.

Ancient sources: The Odyssey places him in Hades, and Pindar's Olympian Odes retell the story of Pelops.
