naiad of Greek mythology, mistress of Hades

Minthe

Minthe is a naiad, a daughter of the river Cocytus, whom Greek tradition remembers as a mistress of Hades. Her story explains the origin of mint, the plant that bears her name.

The myth

According to myth, Minthe became the mistress of Hades, lord of the underworld, until the god took Persephone as his wife. The sources disagree about who punished her. Strabo says that Persephone trampled her and turned her into garden mint, a plant some called hedyosmos, meaning "sweet-smelling".

Oppian gives the fullest version: Minthe, scorned, boasted to Demeter that she surpassed Persephone in beauty and that Hades would soon abandon his wife and return to her. Demeter, angered by the insolence, trampled her underfoot, and mint sprang from the earth. A third tradition, preserved in a scholiast on Nicander, has Persephone tear her to pieces and Hades turn her into a plant to keep her memory alive.

Worship and religion

Near a mountain named after Minthe, close to Pylos, stood a shrine of Hades, also venerated by the Macistians, and a precinct sacred to Demeter. Mint was used in funerary rites and formed part of the kykeon, the drink of barley, water and mint taken by initiates in the Eleusinian Mysteries.

Legacy

The name of the plant, mint, comes from the nymph's name, and an early form of the word appears on a Mycenaean Linear B tablet. Because of its pre-Greek origin, the word belongs to a group of ancient terms, like akanthos and labyrinthos, that predate the Greek language.

Ancient sources: Strabo, Oppian, Ovid in his Metamorphoses, a mention by Cratinus in his lost comedy Nomoi, and the Homeric Hymn to Demeter, which describes the kykeon.

Trivia

A Mycenaean tablet. The name of mint is written in Linear B on a Bronze Age tablet, which shows that the word is very old.
A herb of love and of silence. Mint was believed to be an aphrodisiac and, at the same time, a contraceptive if eaten before intercourse.
