Mesopotamian moon god

Sin

Sin is the moon god of Mesopotamian mythology, son of Enlil and Ninlil and father of Inanna and Utu (Shamash). His cult, centred on Ur and Harran, made him one of the great deities of Mesopotamia, and his link to the lunar cycle explains much of his importance.

The myth

According to tradition, Sin, known as Nanna or Nannar among the Sumerians, was the son of Enlil, god of wind and sky, and Ninlil, goddess of the air. His name varies by language: Nanna comes from Sumerian and Sin from Akkadian, and the two merged into a single deity at an early date. He was depicted as a bearded elder with horns, riding a winged bull, and his main symbol was the crescent moon.

A major Sumerian text, Enlil and Ninlil, tells how Ninlil, pregnant with Nanna, follows Enlil into the underworld. There three substitutions are made so that the god can rise again. The story shares similarities with The Descent of Inanna.

With his wife Ningal he had Inanna, goddess of life and love, and Utu, the sun god. Together they formed a triad of astral deities: the Moon, Venus and the Sun. Sin governed the passing of the months and was credited with control over the tides and the cyclical processes tied to the moon. Some sources also make him the father of Ereshkigal, queen of the underworld.

During the period of Ur (c. 2600-2400 BCE) he was regarded as the supreme god of the pantheon, called father of the gods and creator of all things. His wisdom was linked to the observation of lunar phases, a basis for astronomy and astrology.

Worship and religion

His main cult centres were the temple E-kishnugal in Ur, called E-gish-shir-gal, 'house of the great light', and the sanctuary E-jul-jul in Harran, 'house of joy'. In Ur, the en priestess held a prestigious office, held by princesses such as Enheduanna, daughter of King Sargon of Akkad. In 1789 BCE Hammurabi built a throne for his worship. His temples spread across Babylon, Assyria, Tutub, Uruk, Nippur and Assur. The extent of his influence on the Harranian Sabians, a community that lived in Harran after the Muslim conquest, is disputed.

Legacy

His name survives in the Hebrew Bible in personal names such as Sennacherib and Sanballat, which transliterate it as san. In cuneiform his name was written with the numeral 30, referring to the days of the lunar month, a link that explains his role in reckoning time.

Ancient sources: Sumerian texts such as Enlil and Ninlil and The Descent of Inanna, and the inscriptions of Gudea, in which the god is described as one whose name nobody can explain.

In art

Trivia

A puzzling name. An inscription of Gudea from the third millennium BCE describes Sin as a god whose name nobody can explain, suggesting its meaning was already debated in his time.
The number 30. In the astral system Sin was represented by the number 30, probably the days of the lunar month. From the Old Babylonian period, his name could be written with the sign d30.
