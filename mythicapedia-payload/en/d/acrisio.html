mythical king of Argos, grandfather of Perseus

Acrisius

Acrisius is a mythical king of Argos and the grandfather of the hero Perseus through his daughter Danaë. His story, shaped by a prophecy that comes true by accident, is one of the Greek tales about the weight of fate.

The myth

Acrisius, whose name is often read as meaning lack of judgment, was the son of Abas and Aglaea and ruled Argos. According to the myth, he quarrelled with his twin brother Proetus even in their mother's womb, a rivalry that lasted his whole life and that, in some accounts, gave rise to the invention of the shield. When their father Abas died, the brothers fought for the throne. Acrisius won, Proetus was exiled to Lycia, where he married the daughter of King Iobates, and he returned with a large army. In the end they divided the kingdom: Acrisius kept Argos, and Proetus took Tiryns, which he walled.

Acrisius had a daughter, Danaë, but no son. He consulted the oracle, which warned that he would be killed by a grandson not yet born. To prevent this, he shut Danaë away in an underground bronze chamber or in a bronze tower. Zeus visited her in the form of a golden shower, and Perseus was born. In another version, the seducer was Proetus. When Acrisius learned of the child, he put mother and son in a chest and cast it into the sea. The current carried them to the island of Seriphos, where the fisherman Dictys took them in.

Perseus grew up to become a hero. He beheaded the Gorgon Medusa and rescued Andromeda, whom he married before returning to Argos. Acrisius, warned of his coming, fled to Larissa to escape the prophecy. There, during funeral games, Perseus threw the discus. A gust of wind pushed it off course, and it struck his grandfather, who died instantly. Horrified, Perseus refused the crown of Argos and swapped it with Megapentes, a son of Proetus, so that he ruled Tiryns and Megapentes ruled Argos. The prophecy had come true.

Legacy

Some scholars suggest that the Achaeans who reached Syria may have carried with them the motif of a prenatal rivalry between twins. The Bible would later apply this motif to the division of Abraham's inheritance between Jacob and Esau, who stand for Israel and Edom. This remains a hypothesis rather than a proven fact.

Ancient sources: The Scholiast on Euripides credits him with founding the Delphic amphictyony, and Strabo mentions him in the same context.

Trivia

A discus accident. Acrisius died when a discus thrown by his own grandson at games struck him. A gust of wind that pushed the throw off course is the detail that fulfils the prophecy without anyone intending it.
Founder of the Delphic amphictyony. According to the Scholiast on Euripides, Acrisius founded the Delphic amphictyony. Strabo thinks the league already existed before his time and that he was only the first to organise its affairs and fix which cities took part.
