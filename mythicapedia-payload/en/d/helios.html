Greek god of the sun

Helios

Helios is the personification of the Sun in Greek mythology, a god who drives his chariot across the sky each day. He is one of the most ancient figures of Greek religion, although he remained a relatively minor deity in the Classical era and gained prominence in late antiquity, when he merged with Apollo and with the Roman sun god Sol.

The myth

According to Hesiod's Theogony, Helios is the son of the Titans Hyperion and Theia, and the brother of Selene, the Moon, and Eos, the Dawn. Other sources give his mother different names, such as Euryphaessa or Etra, and some authors regard him as a Titan in his own right. Each morning he rises from the eastern sea in his chariot drawn by fiery horses, crosses the sky and, at evening, sinks into the Ocean. During the night he travels back east in a golden boat or cup, according to different versions.

The best-known episode is that of his son Phaethon, who tried to drive his father's chariot, lost control and set the Earth on fire. In the Odyssey, Helios reports to Zeus that the companions of Odysseus have killed his sacred cattle on the island of Thrinacia, and Zeus sinks their ship, sparing only Odysseus. In another tale, Helios sees Aphrodite and Ares together and tells Hephaestus, who traps them in an invisible net. Heracles, exhausted by the heat of the Libyan desert, shot an arrow at him and received in exchange the golden cup in which the god crossed the sea.

Worship and religion

Helios's cult was secondary across most of Greece. His most important centre was the island of Rhodes, where he was the patron god: annual gymnastic contests were held in his honour, along with a rite in which a chariot was sent over a cliff into the sea. The Colossus of Rhodes, a great statue of the god, adorned the harbour. He also had a cult on the acropolis of Corinth. In late antiquity, the emperor Julian made him the central deity of his brief attempt to revive traditional Roman religion in the 4th century AD.

Legacy

The Greek name gave rise to the prefix helio-, found in words such as heliocentrism, aphelion, heliotrope and heliolatry. His image as a god with a radiant crown and a solar chariot shaped much ancient art and later depictions of the Sun.

Ancient sources: Hesiod's Theogony, Homer's Odyssey and the Homeric Hymns, and Pindar, Euripides and Ovid, among other authors.

Trivia

A god with twelve rays. His solar halo traditionally had twelve rays, symbolising the twelve months of the year.
Famous children. He was the father of Circe, Pasiphae and Aeetes, figures in other myths, and of the ill-fated Phaethon.
