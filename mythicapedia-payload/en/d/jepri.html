Egyptian god of the rising sun

Khepri

Khepri is the Egyptian god of the rising sun and of the renewal of life. His name, meaning «he who came into being by himself», is tied to the scarab beetle, whose image stood for the sun's daily rebirth and the constant transformation of existence.

The myth

According to the Pyramid Texts, Khepri is a self-created deity closely linked to Atum, with whom he was identified as the morning form of the sun. He was shown as a scarab beetle pushing the solar disk across the sky, or as a man with a scarab head who sometimes holds the uas scepter and the ankh. Some texts say that the Earth itself arose from a spittle of the god.

In the Amduat, Ra travels through the Duat, the underworld, at night, exhausted after his day's work. In the sixth hour he reaches the primordial waters of Nun, where Khepri's corpse lies guarded by a many-headed serpent. Ra's ba, or soul, joins the body of Khepri, and the god is reborn. In the twelfth hour, Khepri steers the solar barque which, with the help of Shu, god of air, carries the sun back into the sky at dawn. The Book of Gates and the Book of Caverns tell similar episodes.

Worship and religion

Khepri had no cult of his own: he was venerated as a manifestation of Ra, and the main centre of solar worship was Iunu (Heliopolis). Giant stone scarabs were placed in some temple complexes. At Karnak, an underground chapel represented the Duat; in a secret ritual, the cult image of Amun-Ra descended there and returned transformed into Khepri, «he who emerges from the earth». Scarab amulets, often blue, were used in funerary rites and handed out at public festivals.

Legacy

Scarab-shaped rings and seals became so common that they have been found throughout the Mediterranean. Today, a modern tourist legend at Karnak says that women must touch the statue of Khepri to conceive, and that it grants wishes if you walk around it three times.

Ancient sources: He appears in the Pyramid Texts, and the Amduat, the Book of Caverns and the Book of Gates describe him in the sun's nightly journey.

Trivia

A beetle that pushes the sun. The beetle rolls balls of dung along the ground, an image the Egyptians linked to the solar disk pushed across the sky.
Beetles without females. Because young beetles emerged from dung balls, people believed there were no females and that males reproduced on the dung, a belief Plutarch recorded in his Moralia.
