female bodhisattva, manifestation of Avalokiteśvara

Cundī

Cundī is an Indian Buddhist deity, a female bodhisattva regarded as a manifestation of Avalokiteśvara, and she remains popular in East Asian Buddhism. Devotees invoke her to purify negative karma and gain protection.

The myth

According to tradition, Cundī appears mainly in the Cundī Dhāraṇī Sūtra, translated into Chinese in the late 7th and early 8th centuries. The Kāraṇḍavyūha tells how countless buddhas recited her dhāraṇī, opening a pore in Avalokiteśvara's body that revealed a multitude of worlds.

Her name is debated. Some texts link it to Cundā, others to words meaning purity or a well, and Japanese scholars have never agreed on its etymology. The most common East Asian form has eighteen arms, representing the eighteen merits of awakening.

Worship and religion

In Chinese Buddhism she is linked to recitation of her dhāraṇī, performed with a specific mudra and a round mirror. It is believed to purify negative karma, offer protection, and help practitioners reach Buddhahood. She is called Zhǔntí Fómǔ, Mother of the Buddhas, and her worship survives in East Asia and Tibet.

Legacy

Across East Asia she came to be identified with Guanyin, and in Tibet she is known as Lhamo Cunda. Some of her images resemble those of Prajñāpāramitā, so they are sometimes hard to tell apart.

Ancient sources: Buddhist texts such as the Cundī Dhāraṇī Sūtra, Kāraṇḍavyūha, Sādhanamālā, Niṣpannayogāvalī and Śikṣāsamuccaya.

Trivia

Patron of a dynasty. Her worship was very popular in northern India during the Pala Empire, which adopted her as a protective deity. According to the Tibetan historian Tārānātha, Gopala I, the dynasty's founder, was one of her devotees.
An ancient miniature. An 11th-century manuscript of the Aṣṭasāhasrikā Prajñāpāramitā Sūtra, held at Cambridge University Library, contains a miniature of Cundā from the temple of Paṭṭikerā, in the Tippera region of present-day Bangladesh.
