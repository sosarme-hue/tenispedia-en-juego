night spirit of Germanic and Slavic folklore

Mare

The mare is a malicious being in Germanic and Slavic folklore that sits or rides on the chests of sleeping people, bringing nightmares and a feeling of suffocation. It is usually a female figure with magical powers, able to shapeshift.

The myth

According to belief, the mare could appear as a beautiful young woman, a nasty old lady, a man or an animal, such as a cat, a dog or a bird, often an owl. She rode horses, which were found exhausted and drenched in sweat by morning, and she tangled the hair of people and animals into knots that could not be undone. Some legends say she was the soul of a living or dead person, such as a sinful woman or someone who died unmarried, and Paul Devereux argues that many mares were witches who, in trance, sent their spirits out in animal form.

In German folklore the mare is closely linked to the alp and the môr. To keep her away, people recommended leaving a cold bowl and buttered bread out in the morning, or placing a pair of slippers upside down by the bed. In a Norse story recorded in the Ynglinga saga, from the 13th century, King Vanlandi died from a nightmare sent by the sorceress Huld, hired by his abandoned wife.

Legacy

In Swedish and German, the twisted dwarf pines of the coast are called mare pines, and the knots in hair are known as mare-braids. The English word nightmare comes from this same tradition, and the French word cauchemar keeps a similar Germanic element.

Ancient sources: The Ynglinga saga (13th century), the Vatnsdæla saga and the Eyrbyggja saga, Norse texts that mention the mare in stories about nightmares and sorcery.

Trivia

Mare pines. The dwarf, twisted pines growing on Swedish coastal rocks are called mare pines, because the creature was also believed to tangle trees.
A nightmare with a name. In Danish and Norwegian, the word for nightmare is mare ride, and in Icelandic it is mare tread, showing how the creature lives on in language.
