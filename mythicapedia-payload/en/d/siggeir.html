Gautish king in the Völsunga saga

Siggeir

Siggeir is a Gautish chieftain and king of Götaland in what is now Sweden, according to the Völsunga saga. He is the husband of Signy, and his story, shaped by revenge and incest, drives one of the great heroic tales of northern Europe.

The myth

According to the Völsunga saga, Siggeir married Signy, sister of Sigmund and daughter of King Völsung. At a banquet, the god Odin, disguised as a beggar, thrust the sword Balmung into a tree and promised it to whoever could pull it out. Only Sigmund succeeded. Siggeir offered three times the sword's value, but Sigmund mockingly refused, and the king left offended, planning revenge.

Three months later, Siggeir invited Völsung and his ten sons to Gautland. The Gauts attacked them, Völsung was killed and the sons captured. Signy persuaded her husband to spare her brothers, but Siggeir wanted them to suffer before dying. For nine nights, a she-wolf, whom the saga may identify as Siggeir's mother, devoured one brother each night until only Sigmund remained. Signy told him how to kill her: a servant smeared honey on his face, and when the wolf licked it, Sigmund bit off her tongue. Sigmund then hid in the forest, where Signy supplied him with what he needed.

Signy had two sons with Siggeir. When the eldest turned ten, she sent him to Sigmund to be trained for revenge, but the boy failed the test of courage, and Signy asked Sigmund to kill him. The same happened with the second son. Then, with the help of a völva's magic, Signy changed her appearance, spent three nights with her brother and bore a son, Sinfjötli, who met her expectations. Finally, Sigmund and Sinfjötli killed Siggeir.

Ancient sources: He appears in the Völsunga saga and the Poetic Edda, and the Prose Edda's Skáldskaparmál links him to the Siklings. His family ties also appear in Hversu Noregr byggðist.
