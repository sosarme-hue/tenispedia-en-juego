Roman goddess of door hinges and thresholds

Cardea

Cardea was the ancient Roman goddess of the hinge, the pivot that held doors in place, and by extension the threshold of the home. Ancient writers often blended her with Carna and Crane, who protected the body's vital organs and small children, so her identity was never fixed.

The myth

According to Ovid, the nymph Cranaë was born in the sacred grove of Alernus, beside the Tiber. She hunted in the hills and turned away her suitors by asking them to follow her to a secluded cave, then slipped away into the bushes. Janus, the two-faced god who can see behind him, caught her and seduced her. In exchange for her virginity he gave her rights over the hinges and a whitethorn branch to ward off harm at doorways.

In another story, Cranaë protected the infant Procas, king of Alba Longa, from the striges, night birds that attacked babies in their cribs. She touched the doorposts three times with arbutus leaves, sprinkled the entrance with healing water and offered the birds raw entrails of a sow. The striges then left the cradle alone, and the child regained his colour.

Worship and religion

Her cult was significant in Rome. She was honoured on 1 June, known as the Bean-Kalends, when mashed beans and lard were offered to her, since the beans matured that month. The date was linked to Beltane, as both marked a metaphorical hinge of the year. Masks, balls and small figures were hung on doorways or trees to encourage the harvest, and the hawthorn was sacred to her.

Legacy

Her name survives in English through the Latin cardo, the source of "cardinal". The word "carnal" is related to Carna's name, which ancient writers derived from caro, carnis. Modern scholar Stefan Weinstock linked the doorway deities to the Ianitores terrestres, guardians of the passage to the earthly sphere. Isidore of Seville described the two gates of heaven through which the sun rises and sets.

Ancient sources: Ovid's Fasti names her as Cranaë and Carna; Macrobius and Saint Augustine also mention her.

Trivia

June's beans. The 1 June festival was called the Bean-Kalends because ripe beans were used in her ceremonies that month.
A god with two faces. Janus could see behind him thanks to his two faces, which is why he caught Cranaë when she tried to escape.
