sword of Roland in the Carolingian epic

Durendal

Durendal, also spelled Durandal, is the sword of Roland, a paladin and nephew of Charlemagne in French epic literature. Its reputation for hardness and sharpness made it one of the most famous weapons in medieval literature, and its story later passed into Castilian romances.

The myth

According to tradition, Charlemagne gave the sword to Roland when he was knighted. The Song of Roland says its golden hilt held a tooth of Saint Peter, blood of Basil of Caesarea, hair of Saint Denis and a piece of the cloak of Mary, mother of Jesus.

At the Battle of Roncevaux Pass, the mortally wounded Roland tried to destroy the sword by striking it against a rock so that it would not fall into enemy hands, but the blade only chipped pieces of stone. Other versions say he threw it into water or hid it beneath his body. Some accounts hold that the young Charlemagne had won the sword earlier at the court of Galafre in Toledo, by defeating King Bramante.

In the Castilian ballad tradition, Durandarte became a knight who died at Roncesvalles and asked that his heart be carved out and taken to his beloved Belerma. Another tradition places the sword in the hands of Bernardo del Carpio, who took it after defeating Roland.

Legacy

The character Durandarte appears in the Spanish Romancero, and Cervantes includes him in the second part of Don Quixote, where Montesinos describes lying asleep in his cave beside his cousin Durandarte. At Rocamadour, a replica embedded in a sanctuary wall was shown for many years. The sword attributed to Bernardo del Carpio, which Charles I took in the sixteenth century, is kept in the Royal Armoury in Madrid.

Ancient sources: The Song of Roland, the Spanish Roncesvalles fragment and the Pseudo-Turpin chronicle mention it, and the Spanish Primera Crónica General records the Castilian tradition.

Trivia

Theft at Rocamadour. A replica sword embedded in a rock face at Rocamadour was reported stolen in June 2024.
A disputed name. The Pseudo-Turpin already tried to explain the meaning of the name, a sign that it was not readily understood even by French readers of the time.
