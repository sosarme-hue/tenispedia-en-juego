Mesopotamian god of storms, rain and thunder

Adad

Adad, known as Iškur in Sumerian and Hadad in the Levant, is the god of storms and rain. His worship spread from Syria to Assyria, and his figure shows how a deity brought from outside came to hold a central place in Mesopotamia.

The myth

According to Mesopotamian tradition, Adad rules thunder, rain and storms. The texts offer different accounts of his lineage: the most common version makes him a son of Anu and Uras, but other stories call him a son of Enlil, or of Nanna/Sin, and a brother of Shamash and Inanna.

Hymns give him two faces: he brings rain in season to fertilise the land, and in his rage he unleashes destructive tempests. He is shown with lightning, a thunderbolt shaped like a spear, and a helmet with bull's horns. In the Baal Cycle from Ugarit, Hadad defeats the sea god Yamm with two clubs crafted by the divine artisan Kothar-wa-Khasis. He then builds a palace and hosts a feast for the gods, until Mot, the god of death and drought, threatens to swallow him.

Worship and religion

His main cult centre in southern Babylonia was Karkara, where his chief temple stood; his consort Shala, a grain goddess, had a sanctuary of her own. In Assyria, from the reign of Tiglath-Pileser I, he shared a double sanctuary with Anu at Assur. Together with the sun god Shamash, Adad presided over oracles and divination, consulted through the liver of sacrificed animals or the movement of oil bubbles in water.

Legacy

The Greeks and Romans equated him with Zeus and Jupiter, an association that lived on in the cult of Jupiter Dolichenus. Baal, 'lord', was used as a title for him and for other gods, and several Assyrian kings bore names derived from Adad.

Ancient sources: The Baal Cycle tablets from Ugarit, composed between 1400 and 1200 BCE, name him as Hadad or Baal, while the god lists from Shuruppak and the Assyrian and Babylonian hymns and incantations record him in his two aspects.

Trivia

A very old name. Hadad is already attested at Ebla around 2500 BCE, long before his cult reached Mesopotamia through the Amorites.
The sacred bull. The bull was his symbolic animal, and from the Old Babylonian period it appears as his sacred animal in depictions.
