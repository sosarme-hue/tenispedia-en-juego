king of the Lapiths, punished in Tartarus

Ixion

Ixion was a king of the Lapiths, a people of Thessaly, and one of the clearest examples of an impious ruler in Greek mythology. His eternal punishment in Tartarus made him a symbol of arrogance, the violation of hospitality and unchecked desire.

The myth

According to the tradition, Ixion became king of the Lapiths and of Girton in Thessaly. Authors disagree about his parentage: some make him a son of Phlegyas, others of Leonteus, Antion or even Ares. To win Dia, daughter of King Deioneus, he promised her father a valuable gift, but never paid it. Deioneus took his mares as compensation, so Ixion hid his resentment, invited him to a feast and pushed him into a pit of burning coals. Killing a kinsman and a guest horrified the neighbouring kings, who refused to purify him, and Ixion lived as an outlaw shunned by all.

Zeus, moved by pity, cleansed him and welcomed him to the table of the gods. Ixion, however, tried to seduce Hera. Zeus shaped a cloud into her likeness, called Nephele, and Ixion lay with it; from this union came the Centaurs, also called Ixionidae. As punishment, Zeus struck him with a thunderbolt and ordered Hermes to bind him to a fiery wheel that spun forever. Some versions say the wheel stopped only while Orpheus played his lyre in the Underworld. With Dia, Ixion had a son, Pirithous, though some say his real father was Zeus.

Legacy

His story inspired lost tragedies by Aeschylus, Sophocles, Euripides and Timasitheos, and an engraving on an Etruscan mirror, now in the British Museum, shows him bound to a winged wheel. Painters such as Rubens, Ribera and Delaunay portrayed him, and a Pompeian fresco in the House of the Vettii shows him already fastened to the wheel. Martin Nilsson proposed that the myth may echo an ancient rain-making magic, an idea Robert L. Fowler found convincing.

Ancient sources: Homer mentions him in book 14 of the Iliad, and Pindar uses him in his Second Pythian Ode as a warning against tyrannical pride; the Greek Anthology preserves the version from the temple at Cyzicus.

Trivia

First kin-slayer. By killing his father-in-law, Ixion is regarded in the sources as the first man guilty of murdering a kinsman in Greek mythology.
A cloud shaped like a goddess. The name Nephele means cloud, and it is the figure Zeus created to deceive Ixion in the likeness of Hera.
