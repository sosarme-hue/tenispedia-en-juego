Shinto goddess of joy, fertility and dance

Ame-no-Uzume

Ame-no-Uzume is the kami of joy, fertility and dance in Japanese Shinto. Her dance before the cave of Amaterasu is regarded as the origin of kagura, the sacred dances of the tradition.

The myth

According to the accounts preserved in the Kojiki and the Nihon Shoki, the mischief of her brother Susanoo drove Amaterasu into a cave, and the world was plunged into darkness. Uzume overturned a tub at the entrance, climbed onto it and danced until she fell into a trance. As her garments loosened, the gods burst into laughter. Curious, Amaterasu opened the rock slightly, saw her reflection in a mirror and was drawn out, and light returned to the world.

Later, Uzume accompanied Ninigi, Amaterasu's grandson, on his descent to earth. When the fearsome Sarutahiko blocked their path, only she dared approach him, and with teasing and provocation she persuaded him to step aside. The two married, and after he drowned at Azaka, Uzume received the name Sarume-no-Kimi, head of the Sarume clan.

Worship and religion

Uzume is still worshipped as a kami in present-day Shinto, at shrines such as Chiyo and Tsubaki Grand Shrine, and at some she is honoured as a patroness of dancers and performers. At the November Tori-no-ichi festival, masks or pictures of the couple are sold.

Legacy

In kyōgen farce she appears as Okame, a woman who revels in her sensuality. Some scholars, such as Michael Witzel, link her to the Vedic goddess Ushas and the Greek goddess Eos.

Ancient sources: The Kojiki and the Nihon Shoki, eighth-century Japanese chronicles that record her role in luring Amaterasu out and her marriage to Sarutahiko.

Trivia

A silenced sea creature. According to the Kojiki, Uzume cut the mouth of a sea cucumber with her dagger, leaving it permanently silent.
