Aphroditus

Aphroditus was the male counterpart of Aphrodite, a deity originating from Cyprus who was also celebrated in Athens. He was shown with a female body and clothing but with male attributes, joining both sexes in a single figure.

The myth

According to ancient accounts, the cult of Aphroditus began at Amathus on Cyprus, where he appeared beside Aphrodite in temples. He was described with a woman's shape and dress but with a phallus, which explains his masculine name. Macrobius, in his Saturnalia, reports that Philochorus identified him with the Moon and said that at his sacrifices men and women exchanged clothing. Philostratus describes festivals in which women dressed and acted as men, and men put on women's garb.

The sources place his arrival in Athens around the 4th century BC. Among the earliest surviving finds is a clay mould from the Athenian agora, dating to the late 4th century BC, for a terracotta figurine about 30 centimetres tall. It shows a woman lifting her dress to reveal male genitals, a gesture believed to avert evil and bring good luck. By the 5th century BC there were also hermae of Aphroditus, phallic statues with female heads. This union of male and female, linked to the Moon, was thought to govern fertility across all animal and plant life.

Worship and religion

Ancient writers describe sacrifices involving the exchange of clothing and festivals where men and women mingled. According to Theophrastus, a devotee prepared mulled wine, myrtle wreaths and frankincense to honour the statue of Hermaphroditus. The sources do not say whether this cult survived.

Legacy

His name gave rise to that of Hermaphroditus, a god who, according to Photius, was the same as Aphroditus and whose name derives from those of his parents, Aphrodite and Hermes. Alciphron mentions the temple of Hermaphroditus in one of his letters, and Pausanias recalls a statue of Aphrodite in the Gardens, the work of Alcamenes.

Ancient sources: Theophrastus in his Characters, Philochorus in his Atthis, Pausanias, Alciphron, Philostratus, Macrobius in his Saturnalia, Aristophanes and Laevius.

Trivia

Protective figurine. A clay mould found in the Athenian agora, dating to the late 4th century BC, was used to make figurines of a woman lifting her dress to reveal male genitals, a gesture believed to be protective.
Bearded statue in Cyprus. Macrobius reports a statue of Venus on Cyprus that was bearded, dressed as a woman, held a sceptre and had male genitals, and was thought to be both male and female.
