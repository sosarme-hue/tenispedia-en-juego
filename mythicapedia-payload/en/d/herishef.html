Egyptian god of fertility and the sun

Herishef

Herishef is an Egyptian god of fertility, justice and the sun, pictured with a ram’s head. His cult was centred in Heracleopolis Magna, where he was honoured as a creator and as the protector of the Nile’s flood.

The myth

According to Egyptian tradition, Herishef is a creator god who emerged from the primordial waters, rising from a lotus flower. His name means “He who is on His Lake”, and he favoured the Nile’s flood, on which the country’s sustenance depended. He was described as king of Upper and Lower Egypt and ruler of both riverbanks, with power over the four cardinal points.

He was shown as a man with a ram’s head, wearing a royal shenti and the atef crown decorated with uraei and solar discs. He bore titles such as “The one of the mighty phallus” or “Lord of fear”. He was identified with Osiris, Ra, Khnum and Shu, and in the interpretatio graeca he was linked with Heracles, perhaps because his name was reanalysed in later times as ḥry-šf.t, “He who has great strength”.

Worship and religion

His cult centred on Heracleopolis Magna and reached great importance during the First Intermediate Period, when cattle were sacrificed to him every day. The oldest known temple dates to the Twelfth Dynasty; Ramesses II added a pronaos with sixteen palm columns. According to the Egyptologist Yasuoka, Prince Khaemweset may have directed this project. Several high priests, some of the Twenty-second Dynasty, are documented.

Ancient sources: The Palermo Stone records King Den’s visit to Herishef’s sacred lake, and the Stela of Pasenhor lists the names of his high priests.

Trivia

A reinterpreted name. In later times his name was read as “He who has great strength”, which helped link him with Heracles.
Reused columns. Ramesses II’s pronaos used sixteen palm columns taken from older temples, possibly those of Djedkare Isesi or Sahure.
A travelling coffin. The coffin of Nedjemankh, also believed to have been a priest of Herishef, was looted from Egypt in 2011, sold to the Metropolitan Museum and returned in 2019.
