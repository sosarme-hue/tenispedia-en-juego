Hindu goddess of food and nourishment

Annapurna

Annapurna is the Hindu goddess of food and nourishment, regarded as a manifestation of Parvati. Her name roughly means "she who possesses food," and she is one of the most revered deities linked to meals and provision in India.

The myth

According to a widely told story, Shiva and Parvati argued about the material world. Shiva claimed that everything material, food included, was an illusion. Parvati, who governs material things, was offended and vanished to show her importance. Without her, famine spread, and even the gods had to beg for food. At last Shiva discovered that only one kitchen remained on earth, in Varanasi (Kashi), and went there to beg. The kitchen belonged to Parvati in the form of Annapurna, who served him and reminded him that, as Brahman, he might rise above hunger, but his followers had not.

Old texts also place her in Varanasi. The Skanda Purana tells how Annapurna fed the sage Vyasa, and the Linga Purana describes Parvati, in her form as Annapurna, feeding Shiva at his doorstep. The legend is linked to the Kashi Viswanath Temple.

Worship and religion

Food is considered sacred in Hinduism, and prayers are offered before eating. Annadana, the giving of food, is highly praised, and Annapurna is worshipped by reciting her thousand names or her hundred and eight names. In Varanasi her temple distributes prasad to the elderly and disabled each noon, and during Navaratri food is handed out on a larger scale. Her worship remains alive across many shrines in India. In Marathi weddings a bride receives metal images of the goddess from her mother, and Akshaya Tritiya is considered an auspicious day for buying gold.

Legacy

Her image, holding a golden ladle and a vessel of porridge, is widely known in religious art. Kalidasa mentions her in the Kumara Sambhava, and the Bengali poet Bharatchandra Ray praised her in the Annada Mangal.

Ancient sources: She appears in the Skanda Purana and the Linga Purana, the Devi Bhagavata, and the Annapurna Upanishad, one of the minor Upanishads.
