liberated teachers of Jainism

Tīrthaṅkaras

In Jainism, tīrthaṅkaras are beings who attained liberation (mokṣa) and taught others how to cross saṃsāra, the endless cycle of birth and death. They are the core of Jain worship, yet they do not intervene in the world; they are models to follow rather than gods who grant favours.

The myth

In Jain tradition, time is cyclical, like a wheel that rises and falls in two halves, and in each half 24 tīrthaṅkaras appear. After many rebirths, these beings overcame karma, attained omniscience (kevalajñāna) and preached the dharma. According to the texts, almost all were born into noble families, renounced the world, practised asceticism and meditation, and finally withdrew to a mountain to be extinguished.

The first, Rishabhanatha, is said to have founded human civilisation and taught agriculture, law and social order. The last, Mahavira, is the only one whose historicity is considered firm: he lived in the 6th century BCE, and his predecessor Parshvanatha is also accepted as historical by most scholars. The lives of the others belong to a sacred history set in vast, non-historical timeframes.

The two main sects do not agree on every detail. For the Svetambaras there was a female tīrthaṅkara, Mallinatha, a claim the Digambaras reject. The Kalpa Sutra recounts their lives following a fixed pattern: birth, worldly life, renunciation, asceticism, omniscience and final nirvana.

Worship and religion

Tīrthaṅkaras are the central element of Jain worship. Since they are liberated and do not answer prayers, believers do not ask them for favours; offerings in temples are understood more as a renunciation by the worshipper than as a gift. They are depicted in statues seated in the lotus posture (padmasana) or standing in meditation (kayotsarga), and are told apart by their colours and emblems. Shikharji, in present-day Jharkhand, is the most important pilgrimage site, where twenty of them are said to have attained liberation. Their worship remains alive among Jains today.

Legacy

Their idea survives in the word tīrtha, which the historian of religion Mircea Eliade compared to the Latin pontifices, 'bridge-makers'. The term jina, 'victor', applied to these beings, is also the root of the name of the religion.

Ancient sources: The Kalpa Sutra narrates their lives, and the Tattvartha Sutra, a central Jain text, lists the practices that lead to the karma of becoming a tīrthaṅkara.

Trivia

Preaching hall. The samavasarana, where a tīrthaṅkara preaches after attaining omniscience, has three levels: the lowest, made of silver, is for vehicles; the middle, of gold, is for animals; and the highest, of gems, is for kings, devas and ascetics.
Possible Indus precursor. Some scholars link the nude standing figures and the bull motif on seals from the Indus Valley Civilization to a possible precursor of this cult, although this remains unproven.
