Greek goddess of darkness and the mist of death

Achlys

Achlys (in Greek Ἀχλύς, Akhlýs) is a minor deity of Greek mythology who personifies darkness and shadows, especially the shadows of death. She appears only briefly in ancient texts, but her image of a mist that clouds the eyes of the dying makes her a striking figure.

The myth

The ancient texts do not say who her parents were, as is common with many deities of the underworld. In Homer, achlys is mainly a common noun: the mist that dims the sight of mortals, often at the moment of death. He describes how the breath leaves Sarpedon and the mist spreads over his eyes.

Hesiod is the first source to present her as a character. On the Shield of Heracles she appears alongside the Keres, the Moirai and Tartarus, as a pale, exhausted figure covered in dust and tears, with huge nails and a terrible grimace. For Russo, she is the first allegorical figure of death in Greek literature, one in which all the evils of war are gathered.

Nonnus of Panopolis, in the Dionysiaca, turns her into a Thessalian witch sent by Hera to deceive Dionysus. Her unguents and a bunch of flowers from the Thessalian meadows make the god's attendants change into beings with large ears and a horse's tail. The poet thus draws on the reputation of Thessalian witches to give her a more defined personality.

Some scholars link her to Higinus's Caligo, the primordial darkness which, according to his Fabulae, gave birth to Chaos. The ancient texts do not clarify her kinship, and some modern lists make her a daughter of Nyx.

Ancient sources: Homer, Hesiod (Shield of Heracles), Nonnus of Panopolis (Dionysiaca) and Hyginus (Fabulae).

Trivia

A figure on a shield. Hesiod includes her among the figures decorating the Shield of Heracles, next to the Keres and the Moirai.
Her Roman counterpart. The Romans associated her with Caligo, whom Hyginus credits as the mother of Chaos.
