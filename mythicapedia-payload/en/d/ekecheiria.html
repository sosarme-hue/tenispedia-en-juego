personification of the Olympic truce

Ekecheiria

Ekecheiria is the personification of the truce (ekecheiria, 'armistice') declared during the ancient Greek Olympic Games. She is associated with Zeus, whose sanctuary at Olympia hosted the competitions.

The myth

Ekecheiria is less a character in stories than an allegorical figure linked to Zeus. Her presence at the sanctuary is known mainly from a statue, set up beside the Temple of Zeus at Olympia in 460 BC, which shows her crowning Iphitos, the man credited with restoring the Games.

Her name refers to the Olympic truce. Whenever the Games were announced, heralds set out from Olympia to the participating nations and city-states to call for an end to fighting. During that period travellers could reach the sanctuary without fear of war.

Worship and religion

The truce was tied to the sanctuary of Zeus at Olympia, where the Games were held for almost six hundred years. Breaking the truce was considered a serious offence, and the records mention only two cases, in which soldiers of Philip of Macedon and a King of Sparta were fined.

Legacy

In the twentieth century, Pierre de Coubertin, father of the modern Olympic Games, tried to invoke the truce during World War I so the 1916 Games could go ahead in Berlin. He was unsuccessful, and the Games were cancelled.

Trivia

A statue at Olympia. A statue showing Ekecheiria crowning Iphitos was installed beside the Temple of Zeus in 460 BC.
Fines for breaking the truce. Only two violations are recorded: soldiers of Philip of Macedon and a King of Sparta were fined.
