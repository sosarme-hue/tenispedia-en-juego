Trojan prince in Greek mythology

Paris

Paris, also called Alexander, is the Trojan prince son of King Priam and Queen Hecuba and brother of Hector in Greek mythology. His elopement with the Spartan queen Helen sparked the Trojan War, and in some versions of the tale he is the one who fatally wounds Achilles.

The myth

According to tradition, Hecuba dreamed during her pregnancy that she gave birth to a burning torch, which was read as a sign that Troy would fall. To avert it, Priam ordered the newborn left on Mount Ida, but the herdsman Agelaus raised him as his own son. Ancient writers describe him as a young man of great beauty, with long hair and a delicate face, and his looks were often considered feminine. As a youth he gained a reputation for his skill with the bow and for his love of the nymph Oenone, daughter of the river god Cebren, who promised to heal him if he were ever wounded.

At the wedding of Thetis and Peleus, Eris threw a golden apple meant for the fairest goddess. Zeus assigned Paris to judge the dispute: Hera offered him rule over the world, Athena invincibility in war, and Aphrodite the love of Helen, the most beautiful woman alive. Paris chose Aphrodite, and the other two goddesses swore revenge. After being recognised as a prince, he sailed to Sparta and ran off with Helen while Menelaus was away in Crete. Some versions say she was abducted against her will, while others say that what left with him was a phantom image of her.

During the siege, Paris challenged Menelaus to single combat, but he was wounded and rescued by Aphrodite. Versions differ on the death of Achilles: some credit Paris with the arrow in the heel, others Apollo. In the most widespread ending, Paris dies from an arrow shot by Philoctetes with the bow of Heracles. Oenone, full of remorse, arrived too late to heal him and took her own life on the pyre where he was burning.

Legacy

The Judgment of Paris is one of the most repeated subjects in art, from a mosaic in Antioch to painters such as Cranach, Rubens and Boucher. Sophocles and Euripides both wrote tragedies titled Alexander, now lost. Ovid included him among his characters, and Dante places him in the second circle of Hell among the lustful. In the twentieth century Michael Tippett wrote the 1962 opera King Priam about him, and Rudolf Borchardt took him into the novel Juego de dioses, also known as Spielball der Götter.

Ancient sources: Homer's Iliad, the Cypria, the chronicle attributed to Dictys Cretensis, and the lost tragedies of Sophocles and Euripides.

Trivia

A winning bull. In a bull contest, Paris pitted his champion against all rivals. The bull that finally beat it was Ares in disguise, and Paris handed over the golden crown without protest.
A daughter named Helen. Paris wanted to name his daughter with Helen Alexandra, but Helen chose her own name for the girl. According to Ptolemy Chennus, this child was killed by Hecuba during the fall of Troy.
