local god of the ninth nome of Lower Egypt

Andjety

Andjety is a local deity of the ninth nome of Lower Egypt, centred on the town of Andjet, which the Greeks called Busiris. Considered one of Egypt's earliest gods, he is thought to have been a forerunner of Osiris.

The myth

Funerary texts present Andjety as a sovereign figure linked to royal power. In the Pyramid Texts he is said to preside over the eastern nomes, and the deceased king is identified with him. Pharaoh Sneferu of the Fourth Dynasty, builder of the first true pyramid, is shown wearing his crown. The Coffin Texts call him the "bull of vultures", an epithet pointing to fertility, and connect him with the waters and with the drowning of the great god.

He shares features with Osiris: he holds the heka crook and the nejej flail and wears a crown similar to the atef, which later passed to Osiris. From the Libyan period he is often shown with two ostrich feathers or the henu crown, ram's horns with a solar disk, and he sometimes holds the ankh and the was sceptre. In one Coffin Text, Thoth is asked to vindicate Osiris before a great tribunal on the night of that drowning in Andjety. Egyptologists believe Andjety was originally an ancient ruler who was later deified.

Worship and religion

In the temple of Seti I at Abydos, the king offers incense to Osiris-Andjety, identified with the local god Khentyamentiu, while Isis stands beside him. The sources say little about his everyday worship, and his role was largely absorbed by that of Osiris.

Ancient sources: The Pyramid Texts and the Coffin Texts, which mention him in funerary formulas and royal epithets.

Trivia

Royal crown. Pharaoh Sneferu is shown wearing the crown of Andjety, which shows how closely the god was tied to kingship.
Bull of vultures. His most unusual epithet in the Coffin Texts is "bull of vultures", an image that alludes to his fertility.
