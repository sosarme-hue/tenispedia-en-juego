Persian king in Greek mythology, father of Leucothoe

Orchamus

Orchamus is a king of Persia (Achaemenia) who appears in Greek mythology through Ovid's Metamorphoses. He is best known as the father of Leucothoe, whom he buried alive after learning that she had been involved with the sun god Helios.

The myth

In Ovid's account, Orchamus ruled the Achaemenian cities in a land rich in spices and perfumes, and he counted among the descendants of ancient Belus. He married Eurynome, the most beautiful woman of the land, and they had a daughter, Leucothoe, who came to outshine her mother in beauty.

Helios fell in love with the young woman and, to gain access to her, took on her mother's appearance and entered her chambers. Clytia, a former lover of the god and consumed with jealousy, told Orchamus what was happening. The fierce and merciless king buried his daughter alive. Helios arrived too late to save her and turned her into a frankincense tree. Clytia, abandoned, pined away on the ground, gazing at the sun until she became the heliotrope, whose flowers still follow the sun across the sky each day.

Lactantius claims Hesiod also told the story, while other authors offer different versions: one Greek paradoxographer makes Leucothoe and Clytia sisters and daughters of Orchamus, and Boccaccio places him in a genealogy beginning with Perseus and Andromeda.

Legacy

The name of Clytia gave rise to the word heliotrope, for a plant whose flowers seem to follow the sun. The story of Leucothoe and Helios has inspired many artistic works over the centuries.

Ancient sources: Ovid's Metamorphoses is the main source; Lactantius attributes a version of the story to Hesiod.

Trivia

A plant with a mythological name. The heliotrope takes its name from Clytia, who according to the story was transformed into this flower by turning toward the sun.
