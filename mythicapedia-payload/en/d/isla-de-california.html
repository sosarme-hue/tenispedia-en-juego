phantom island on the Pacific map

Island of California

The Island of California was a legendary island that European maps showed for centuries off the west coast of North America, supposedly separated from the mainland by a strait. In fact it was the Baja California peninsula, and the mistake is one of the most famous in the history of cartography.

The myth

The legend first appears in Las sergas de Esplandián, a chivalric romance by Garci Rodríguez de Montalvo published in Seville in 1510. In the story, California is an island to the right of the Indies, very close to the Terrestrial Paradise, inhabited only by black women who live like Amazons and are guarded by griffins.

Although the novel was pure fiction, its popularity during the age of exploration may have led early navigators to identify the peninsula with that legendary island. Hernán Cortés sent expeditions to southern Baja California in the 1530s, and in 1539 Francisco de Ulloa sailed up the gulf to the mouth of the Colorado River, showing that the land was a peninsula. Even so, from the early 1600s maps kept drawing it as an island. The idea is thought to have spread through copied maps: the Carmelite friar Antonio de la Ascensión drew it as an island in 1603, and a map of his, seized by Dutch pirates, went into circulation in Europe. In 1622 the British mathematician Henry Briggs reproduced it in a pamphlet.

Legacy

The error persisted for more than two centuries on Dutch, Japanese, French, German and British maps. Stanford University's Glen McLaughlin Collection holds slightly over 1,000 maps of California as an island, the largest such collection in the world. The region's name is often linked to Montalvo's novel, although this remains a hypothesis: the book is thought to have inspired the name.

Ancient sources: Las sergas de Esplandián (1510), by Garci Rodríguez de Montalvo, is the first known mention of the island.

Trivia

A stolen map. A map by Antonio de la Ascensión, showing California as an island, was seized by Dutch pirates on the return voyage to Acapulco. Shortly afterwards, Dutch maps appeared with the same island.
An island that did not exist. In 1778 James Cook came very close to the Strait of Juan de Fuca, yet he wrote that there was not the least probability of such a passage existing. The myth of a large island off the coast lingered for decades afterwards.
