Roman goddess of spring and queen of the underworld

Proserpina

Proserpina is the Roman goddess of spring, growth and life returning after winter, and queen of the underworld as the wife of Pluto. Her figure is identical to that of the Greek Persephone, whose myth Rome adopted almost unchanged.

The myth

According to the tradition, Pluto, king of the underworld, abducted Proserpina while she was picking flowers on Mount Etna in Sicily, as Hyginus tells it, or in the grove of Henna, as Cicero has it. Her mother Ceres, goddess of the harvest, searched for her everywhere with blazing torches until she secured her return to the world above. However, Proserpina had eaten a few pomegranate seeds in the underworld, and that bound her to Pluto. It was agreed that she would spend six months with her mother and six with her husband, a story that explains the cycle of sowing and harvest.

The sources disagree about her parentage. For Cicero she was the daughter of Jupiter and sister of Liber; Hyginus presents her as the daughter of Jupiter and Ceres, and some later authors link her to Styx. Other accounts say the Sirens, daughters of the river Achelous, were turned into winged creatures by Ceres for failing to help in the search, and that the nymph Cyane, who opposed the abduction, was changed into a spring by Pluto. In the Georgics, Virgil places her in the story of Orpheus and Eurydice, where it is Proserpina who sets the condition that Eurydice must follow Orpheus out of the underworld.

Worship and religion

In Rome, Proserpina entered the official cult around 205 BC, toward the end of the Second Punic War, imported from southern Italy. She was housed in Ceres’ temple on the Aventine Hill, alongside Ceres and Libera, and priestesses of Greek origin served Ceres and Proserpina as “Mother and Maiden”. The rite, modeled on the Greek Thesmophoria, included secret initiations and nighttime processions with torches, and aimed to secure good harvests and fertility. Young women were expected to imitate Proserpina’s chastity, while married women looked to Ceres as their model. Remains of a temple to her survive at Melite, in modern Mtarfa, Malta.

Legacy

The abduction of Proserpina has been a recurring subject in art: Bernini’s sculpture of around 1621, and paintings by Rubens, Rembrandt and Dürer, fixed it in the European imagination. In literature she inspired poems by Goethe and Swinburne, and in music, Lully’s opera and Stravinsky’s oratorio-ballet Perséphone. In 1952 Juan José Castro composed the opera Proserpina and the Foreigner, and in 2006 Luis Alberto Spinetta released a song bearing her name.

Ancient sources: Hyginus’ Fables, Virgil’s Georgics, Cicero’s Against Verres and Claudian’s poem De raptu Proserpinae recount her principal episodes.

Trivia

An island dedicated to goddesses. According to Cicero, in his Against Verres, the entire island of Sicily was dedicated to Ceres and Libera, the goddesses who, in that account, first brought cereal crops to the island.
A temple in Malta. Her temple at Melite on Malta was quarried away in the 17th and 18th centuries, and only a few fragments survive.
