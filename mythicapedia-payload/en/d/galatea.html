animated statue and wife of Pygmalion in Greek myth

Galatea

Galatea is the ivory statue that Pygmalion of Cyprus carved and loved, and which came to life in Greek myth. Her name is post-antique, since no ancient text calls the statue by it.

The myth

The story first appears in a Hellenistic work on Cyprus by Philostephanus, and Ovid retells it in the Metamorphoses: King Pygmalion falls in love with a statue he has carved himself, and Aphrodite brings it to life in answer to his prayers. The couple then marry.

According to another tradition, they had a son, Paphus, who gave his name to the city of Paphos, and a daughter, Metharme.

Worship and religion

The myth is linked to the cult of Aphrodite at Paphos, whose sanctuary housed the goddess's cult image. The figure of Galatea had no cult of her own.

Legacy

The name Galatea spread with Jean-Jacques Rousseau's lyrical scene Pygmalion (1762), and the theme inspired many artworks in the eighteenth century.

Ancient sources: Philostephanus, in his history of Cyprus, and Ovid, in the Metamorphoses, which recount the story of Pygmalion.

Trivia

A late name. Ancient texts do not name the statue. The name Galatea was established by eighteenth-century writers.
A Rousseau scene. Rousseau's 1762 work popularized the name, which had earlier been used in pastoral fiction such as the story of Acis and Galatea.
