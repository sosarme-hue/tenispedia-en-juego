personification of terror in Greek mythology

Deimos

Deimos is, in Greek mythology, the personification of dread and terror that accompany war. The son of Ares and Aphrodite, he appears alongside his brother Phobos, fear, and his name lives on in astronomy as one of the two moons of Mars.

The myth

In Hesiod's Theogony, Deimos and Phobos are born to Ares, god of war, and Cytherea, the poet's name for Aphrodite. The text also lists Harmonia, who later married Cadmus, among their siblings. Hesiod describes the brothers as terrible beings who threw the tight ranks of warriors into confusion in the bloody fighting.

In the Iliad, Deimos has no role of his own, but he goes into battle with his father, together with Phobos and Eris, the goddess of discord. Ares usually appears as the god who brings disorder to armies, and his sons spread panic among the ranks. Agamemnon's shield also shows the two brothers beside their father.

In The Shield of Heracles, which is attributed to Hesiod, the brothers serve Ares. When Heracles wounds him and knocks him down, Deimos and Phobos lift him from the ground, place him in their chariot and carry him back to Olympus.

Nonnus's Dionysiaca, a late epic, adds further episodes. Zeus arms the brothers with lightning and thunder to frighten Typhon, and later they drive Ares's chariot in his war against Dionysus and the Indians. Semus of Delos, a Greek antiquarian, also makes Deimos the father of Scylla, although in that version her mother is Cratheis, daughter of Hecate and Triton.

Legacy

In 1877 the American astronomer Asaph Hall discovered the two moons of Mars and named them Phobos and Deimos. Deimos, the smaller of the two, still gives its name to one of the Martian moons.

Ancient sources: Hesiod in the Theogony and The Shield of Heracles, Homer in the Iliad, Nonnus of Panopolis in the Dionysiaca, and Semus of Delos, cited by later sources.

Trivia

A name for a moon. Deimos is the smaller of the two moons of Mars. Like Phobos, its name was chosen by Asaph Hall, who discovered it in 1877.
Horses or charioteers. The poet Antimachus presents Deimos and Phobos as the horses of Ares, a version that departs from Homer, where they act as the god's companions.
