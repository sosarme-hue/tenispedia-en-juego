Phrygian god of vegetation, consort of Cybele

Attis

Attis is a Phrygian god of vegetation and the eunuch consort of the goddess Cybele, whose cult spread through the Greek world and later into Rome. His story of death and rebirth was read as the cycle of the crops, and his figure served as the model for the castrated priests of the goddess.

The myth

Accounts of his origin do not agree. According to the native version of Pessinus, recorded by Pausanias, Zeus spilled his semen on the earth and from it sprang Agdistis, a hermaphroditic daemon whom the gods castrated. An almond tree grew from its blood, and one of its fruits made Nana, daughter of the river Sangarius, pregnant. She abandoned the child, and a he-goat raised him. Attis grew into a youth of extraordinary beauty, and Agdistis fell in love with him. During his wedding to the king's daughter, the goddess burst into the celebration, Attis went mad and castrated himself beneath a pine tree.

Other traditions make him the son of Calaus or tie him to Lydia, where a boar sent by Zeus killed Attis and several Lydians. In Ovid, Attis is a Phrygian youth loved by Cybele, who demands that he remain chaste. When he falls for the nymph Sagaritis he breaks his vow, the nymph dies, and he, out of his mind, mutilates himself on Mount Dindymus. In Catullus, his self-castration leads to a frenzied procession of galli through the mountains. In the Pessinus account, Agdistis finally asks Zeus to keep Attis's body from decaying.

Worship and religion

His worship centred on the temple at Pessinus, where the galli, eunuch priests, formed a hierarchy whose highest official took the title Attis. In Rome, the spring celebration included the Hilaria, with a day of mourning, the Dies Sanguinis, when followers flogged themselves until they bled, and a day of joy for his rebirth, falling at the equinox. The sources do not indicate whether his cult survives today.

Legacy

Lully composed the opera Atys in 1675 and Piccinni another of the same name in 1780. Oscar Wilde mentions him in his poem The Sphinx (1894). Emperor Julian read him in Neoplatonic terms as a symbol of nature. His life-size statue at Ostia Antica is among the most important surviving representations of him.

Ancient sources: Hermesianax (elegies), Pausanias, Diodorus Siculus, Ovid in the Metamorphoses and Catullus in his poem 63.

Trivia

A pine and some violets. In some versions, Attis castrates himself under a pine tree and violets grow from his blood.
No pork. The Gauls of Pessinus avoided pork, and the story of the boar sent by Zeus was offered as the explanation for this taboo.
