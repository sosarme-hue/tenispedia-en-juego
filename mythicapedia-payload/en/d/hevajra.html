principal yidam of Tantric Buddhism (Vajrayana)

Hevajra

Hevajra is one of the most important yidams, or enlightened beings, of Tantric Buddhism or Vajrayana, usually depicted with his consort Nairātmyā. His tantra, which emerged in eastern India between the 8th and 10th centuries, went on to shape Tibetan Buddhism, especially the Sakya order.

The myth

In Buddhist traditions, Hevajra is an enlightened manifestation rather than a creator god. The Hevajra Tantra is said to have been revealed by Vajranairātmā to the master Virupa, and from him it passed to his disciple Dombi Heruka, whose lineage reached the Sakya order.

The text, written in mixed-quality Sanskrit with some verses in Apabhraṃśa, has around 750 verses and is presented as an excerpt of a much larger work of up to 500,000 ślokas in 32 sections. Its major commentaries, such as the Yogaratnamālā, passed from Kṛṣṇa or Kāṇha to Tilopa, then to Nāropa, and finally to Marpa, who brought it to Tibet.

Worship and religion

In Tibet, the Sakya tradition kept the practice of the Hevajra Tantra alive after Drogmi Lotsawa translated it into Tibetan around 1030 and Dkon mchog ryal po founded Sa-skya Monastery in 1073. In Cambodia, surviving images show that its practice flourished between the 10th and 13th centuries under the Khmer Empire.

Legacy

The Sakya tradition made it central to its teachings, and in 1244 Sakya Pandita passed it on to the Mongol prince Godan. In the West, David Snellgrove's 1959 edition and translation was the first major Buddhist tantra to be translated in full into a Western language.

Ancient sources: The tradition's own texts include the Hevajra Tantra and its commentaries, such as the Yogaratnamālā, along with the Tibetan chronicles of Tāranātha and Drakpa Gyeltsen.

Trivia

Chinese translation. The Chinese version was translated by Fa-hu (Dharmapāla) in the capital of the Northern Song and presented to Emperor Renzong around 1054. In China, however, the tantra never became popular.
An exiled master. Maitrīpa, also called Advayavajra, was banished from Vikramashila for practising with a yoginī during Atīśa's abbacy, yet he still passed on the Hevajra teachings to Marpa.
