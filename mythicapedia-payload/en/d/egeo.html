Aegeus

Aegeus is, in Greek mythology, the king of Athens and father of the hero Theseus. He gave his name to the Aegean Sea and is credited with founding many Athenian institutions.

The myth

According to tradition, Aegeus was born in Megara, where his father Pandion II had taken refuge after the sons of Metion drove him out of Athens. After Pandion's death, Aegeus and his brothers Nisus, Pallas and Lycus recovered the city and divided Attica among themselves. Aegeus, the eldest, received Athens, but soon stripped his brothers of their shares: he expelled Lycus, who fled to Messenia, and Pallas rebelled with his fifty sons, only to be crushed by Theseus. Nisus lost Megara to Minos of Crete, who exploited the treachery of Nisus' daughter.

With no sons from his first two wives, Meta and Chalciope, Aegeus consulted the oracle at Delphi, which answered with a riddle. Pittheus, king of Troezen, understood it, got him drunk and led him to lie with his daughter Aethra. Aegeus buried his sword and sandals under a great rock and asked Aethra to raise their son in secret until he could lift it. That boy was Theseus, though some authors say Poseidon was also his father that same night.

Later, Medea arrived in Athens fleeing Corinth and married Aegeus, who had a son with her, Medus. She tried to secure the throne for Medus and poisoned Theseus at a feast, but Aegeus recognised his son's sword and knocked the cup away. Medea was banished, and Aegeus sent Theseus against the Marathonian Bull, as he had earlier done with Androgeus, son of Minos, who died after competing in the Panathenaic Games. Minos punished Athens with a tribute of youths for the Minotaur. Theseus returned from Crete with black sails instead of white, as he had promised his father. Aegeus, believing him dead, threw himself from a height or into the sea, and the sea took his name.

Worship and religion

According to Pausanias, Aegeus introduced the worship of Aphrodite Urania, the Heavenly one, in Athens to appease the goddess. The city had a heroon dedicated to him, called the Aigeion. There is no evidence that this cult survives today.

Legacy

His name lives on in the Aegean Sea, which according to tradition recalls the king who threw himself into its waters. His son is called Aegeides, a patronymic used mainly for Theseus. Euripides made him appear in his tragedy Medea, and Sophocles wrote a play called Aegeus, now lost.

Ancient sources: Pausanias, who describes the cult of Aphrodite Urania and the heroon of Aegeus in Athens; Strabo, who records the division of Attica according to Sophocles; Euripides' tragedy Medea.

Trivia

An oracle with a catch. In the version recorded by Pausanias, Aegeus did not understand the Delphic answer about not opening the wineskin until reaching the height of Athens. It was King Pittheus who deciphered it.
A heroon in Athens. Athens had a heroon dedicated to Aegeus, called the Aigeion, showing that he was honoured as a hero.
