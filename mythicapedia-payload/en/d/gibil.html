Mesopotamian god of fire

Gibil

Gibil is the Mesopotamian god of fire, linked both to its beneficial side and to its destructive power. His Akkadian name, Girra, also appears in written sources, and he matters chiefly for his role in ritual purification.

The myth

Gibil's genealogy varies from author to author. In some traditions he is the son of An and Ki, in others of An and Shala, and in others of Enlil, Anu or Nuska. Some texts make him a brother of Enki and Ninhursag.

He is described as a very wise god, with a mind so vast that all the gods together cannot imagine it. In some versions of the Enuma Elish he keeps the points of weapons sharp, and other texts also credit him with the wisdom of metalworking.

Worship and religion

He already appears in offering lists from Early Dynastic Shuruppak, alongside the major gods of the town, and he is linked to Eridu. In the Kassite period he was worshiped at Nippur. His symbol was a torch, though no representations of him have been identified in art. His most notable ritual role was purification.

Legacy

His legacy is mainly scholarly: specialists debate whether his name was really read Gibil or Girra, since there is no direct primary evidence for the first reading.

Ancient sources: The An = Anum god lists, the incantation series Maqlû and Šurpu, and the Zame Hymns, which include a hymn dedicated to him.

Trivia

A disputed name. The name Gibil is the conventional reading of a cuneiform sign, but scholars have not fully confirmed it from primary sources.
Fire from lightning. A ritual text held that a house set on fire by lightning was a display of Gibil's wrath.
