Odin's gold ring in Norse mythology

Draupnir

Draupnir is the gold ring owned by the god Odin in Norse mythology. Its defining trait is that it multiplies: every ninth night, eight new rings of the same size and weight as the original "drip" from it.

The myth

According to the tale, the ring was forged by the dwarven brothers Brokkr and Eitri (or Sindri) as one of three gifts. Loki had wagered that they could not match the treasures made by the Sons of Ivaldi, among them Sif's golden hair and the ship Skidblandner.

In the contest of gifts, Draupnir lost to Thor's hammer Mjölnir. Odin placed the ring on the funeral pyre of his son Baldr, and it was later retrieved by Hermóðr. In another episode, Freyr's servant Skírnir offers the ring as a gift while wooing Gerðr, as described in the poem Skírnismál.

Legacy

In modern interpretations, Draupnir has been read as a symbol of fertility and of the creative spirit, an image of ideas that pass from one generation to the next and keep growing.

Ancient sources: The ring appears in the Poetic Edda and the Prose Edda, and in the poem Skírnismál.

Trivia

A gift from dwarves. Draupnir was made by dwarves as part of a wager set up by Loki, who claimed they could not outdo the Sons of Ivaldi.
A ring that multiplies. Every ninth night, eight rings of equal value drip from Draupnir, making it an endless source of wealth.
