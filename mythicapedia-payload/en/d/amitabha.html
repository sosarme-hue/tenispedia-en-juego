Amitābha

Amitābha is one of the main Buddhas of Mahayana Buddhism and the most widely venerated Buddhist figure in East Asia. He presides over the Pure Land of Sukhāvatī, and devotion centred on reciting his name remains one of the most widespread Buddhist practices today.

The myth

According to the Sukhāvatīvyūha sutras, long ages ago Amitābha was a bodhisattva monk named Dharmākara. In some versions he was a former king who renounced his throne after encountering the teachings of the Buddha Lokeśvararāja. Over five kalpas he contemplated the qualities of countless buddha-fields and resolved to create the finest of them all. He made forty-eight vows, the most common version, which set out the kind of pure land he would create, the conditions for rebirth there, and the kind of beings who would dwell in it.

The eighteenth vow became central to Pure Land Buddhism: it promises that those who trust him and think of him, even ten times, will be reborn in his land, except those who commit the five grave offences or abuse the Right Dharma. After many eons Dharmākara attained Buddhahood as Amitābha, ten kalpas ago, and tradition holds that his vows were fulfilled. Sukhāvatī lies billions of worlds away to the west. There beings are born without parents on lotus flowers, and those who still doubt remain enclosed in their bud for five hundred years before beholding the splendour of the Buddha.

Worship and religion

Devotion to Amitābha is especially prominent in East Asia, where mindfulness of his name, known as nianfo in Chinese and nembutsu in Japanese, is regarded as a path to liberation open to everyone. Pure Land practitioners rely on his other power and faithfully recite his name, and it is believed he appears to believers at the moment of death. In East Asian art he is often shown in a triad with the bodhisattvas Avalokiteśvara and Mahāsthāmaprāpta; in Tibetan Buddhism his companions are Avalokiteśvara and Vajrapani or Padmasambhava. Devotion remains alive in monasteries and lay communities across the region.

Legacy

Amitābha is often depicted radiating light, a symbol of his wisdom, and his image is common in Himalayan and East Asian art. According to the sources, his teaching of an accessible rebirth was popular in Gandhara, from where it spread to Central and East Asia.

Ancient sources: The two Sukhāvatīvyūha sutras: the Larger Sutra, also called the Sutra of Infinite Life, and the Shorter Sutra, known as the Amitābha Sutra.

Trivia

A closed lotus. Those who doubt Amitābha are reborn in a lotus that stays closed. They live inside it as in a palace, but without the Buddha's presence, until they dispel their doubts.
Ten remembrances. The eighteenth vow requires only thinking of Amitābha ten times to be reborn in his land, which made his devotion accessible to almost anyone.
Measureless life. He is also called Amitāyus, «Measureless Life», and in Tibetan Buddhism he is distinguished from Amitābha and depicted differently.
