Slavic noon demon

Poludnitsa

Poludnitsa is a noon spirit of Slavic folklore, found across Eastern Europe, who personifies sunstroke. She appears as a young woman in white wandering the edges of fields during the hottest hours of the day.

The myth

Her name comes from a root meaning 'half-day' or 'midday'. According to tradition, she shows herself in the heat of summer as whirling dust and carries a scythe, a sickle or old-style shears. She stops people working in the fields to ask them hard questions or to chat; whoever cannot answer, or tries to change the subject, may lose their head or fall ill. She can take the form of an old hag, a beautiful woman or a twelve-year-old girl, and in the stories she is used to keep children away from valuable crops.

In northern Russia she is imagined holding a giant frying pan, with which she shields the rye from the sun or scorches it along with the herbs in bloom. She is also said to appear at midnight to show someone where to find a flower that makes its finder invisible. She loves dancing: whoever she finds sleeping in the field she wakes to dance until dawn. No one can beat her at dancing, but if a girl manages it, she is rewarded with a rich dowry.

Some accounts make her a sister of the morning, evening and night stars. She is the second youngest of the sisters, after Zarya-Zarenitsa, the morning star, while the eldest is Kupalnitsa, the night star.

Legacy

She appears in Chłopi (The Peasants), the Nobel Prize-winning novel by Władysław Reymont, set in Congress Poland in the 1880s, where the południca is mentioned twice among other Slavic beings. In Germany she is known as the Mittagsfrau ('Lady Midday'), and in Upper Silesia as the Cornflower Wraith, Chabernica.

Trivia

Sister of the stars. In some versions she belongs to a family of four sisters, each linked to a different star depending on the time of day.
The Silesian Chabernica. Around Prudnik, in Upper Silesia, people believed in a similar figure called Chabernica, who wore cornflowers in her hair and punished those who trampled the grain.
