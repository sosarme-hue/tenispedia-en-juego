Norse goddess of refusal and vigilance

Syn

Syn is a goddess of Norse mythology associated with vigilance, truth and defensive refusal. She appears in the Prose Edda as a guardian of doors and as a figure linked to legal cases in which someone wishes to reject an accusation.

The myth

According to Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda, written in the 13th century, Syn is the eleventh of the sixteen ásynjur, goddesses of the Æsir line, whom the author describes in chapter 35 of the Gylfaginning. Her task is to guard the doors of the hall and bar them against those who must not enter. She is also appointed in legal matters to defend against accusations she wishes to refute.

Snorri connects her name with the word for denial and gives a saying: when someone says no, a syn is made, and a man who proves an accusation false is said to 'make a syn'. In Old Norse, the word means refusal.

Snorri also includes her in the Skáldskaparmál, in a list of twenty-seven ásynjur. Her name further appears in skaldic poetry as a kenning: in Eilífr Goðrúnarson's Þórsdrápa a giant is called 'Syn of the hearth-stone', and in a poem attributed to Steinar a woman is called 'Syn of the soft necklace-stand'.

Scholars debate her origins. Rudolf Simek notes that Syn belongs to a group of goddesses whose names are recorded from the late heathen period, and that earlier these figures may have been part of the collective dísir, female spirits themselves linked to the Germanic Matronae.

Ancient sources: Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda (13th century), in the Gylfaginning and Skáldskaparmál books, and skaldic poetry.

Trivia

A legal saying. According to the Prose Edda, her name gave rise to an expression: a person who proved an accusation false was said to 'make a syn'.
A goddess in metaphors. Her name appears in skaldic kennings, where a giant is called 'Syn of the hearth-stone'.
