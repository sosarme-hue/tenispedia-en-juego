epithet of Zeus and other Greek apotropaic deities

Meilichios

Meilichios (Greek Μειλίχιος, roughly «one who can be appeased» or «the kind one») is an epithet applied to several Greek deities, above all Zeus. Its cult was very old and chthonic in character, centred on offerings meant to turn away harm.

The myth

Ancient accounts present Meilichios less as a figure in a long story than as a power that had to be placated. According to Pausanias, near the river Cephissus stood an ancient altar of Zeus Meilichios, where Theseus received purification after killing, among other robbers, Sinis, a relative of his.

The epithet was also applied to Dionysus on the island of Naxos and to Tyche, Fortune. The plural «the Meilichian gods» referred to deities who were appeased at night so that they would drive away all evil.

Worship and religion

In Athens the Diasia festival was held, with offerings of cakes, and the Pompaia procession, in the month of Maimakterion, carried a ram's fleece and a caduceus. The sacrifices were burned whole, and worshippers did not share in the meat. Altars stood at the Cephissus, at Sicyon and at Argos.

Legacy

His name gave rise to a genus of beetles (Coleoptera).

Ancient sources: Pausanias, the Suda and the Orphic Hymns, which invoke him under the name «Daimon».

Trivia

A Zeus in serpent form. On votive reliefs from Athens and Piraeus he often appears as a serpent, sometimes bearded, perhaps to match the usual depictions of Zeus.
A sacrifice without a feast. His offerings were burned entirely and the worshippers did not share the meat, unlike many other Greek sacrifices.
