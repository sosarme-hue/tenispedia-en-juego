Sicilian nymph of Greek and Roman mythology

Aetna

Aetna is a Sicilian nymph of Greek and Roman mythology, linked by tradition to the mountain that bears her name on Sicily. She matters because the story of the nymph and the volcano was how ancient writers explained the peak and its fiery activity through tales of gods and giants.

The myth

According to Alcimus, she was a daughter of Uranus and Gaia, though another version makes her a daughter of Briareus. Stephanus of Byzantium, by contrast, says she was a daughter of Oceanus, so the authors do not agree on her parentage. Simonides tells how she served as arbitrator between Hephaestus and Demeter over the possession of Sicily. Some accounts say she bore the Palici to Zeus; others name Hephaestus as the father.

Tradition placed Typhon, Enceladus or Briareus beneath Mount Etna, buried there by Zeus. The mountain was also believed to be where Hephaestus and the Cyclopes forged the thunderbolts of Zeus.

Legacy

Her name passed to Mount Etna, which according to ancient belief took its name from her, and also to Aitne, one of the moons of Jupiter.

Ancient sources: Alcimus, Simonides and Stephanus of Byzantium mention her in connection with her parentage and her disputes.

Trivia

Zeus's forge. The mountain was believed to be where Hephaestus and the Cyclopes made the thunderbolts of Zeus.
A moon of Jupiter. Aitne, one of the moons of Jupiter, takes its name from this nymph.
