Mesopotamian god of pastures and herds

Šumugan

Šumugan, also written Šamagan, was a god worshipped in Mesopotamia and ancient Syria, protector of grazing animals and watering places. He linked the pastoral world with agricultural fertility.

The myth

Texts portray him as a shepherd god, associated above all with quadrupeds such as donkeys or wild sheep, and tasked with caring for their habitat and the plants that grow there. He was called 'shepherd of everything' and 'god of wool'.

Because he was believed to dwell in the steppe, he is sometimes linked to the netherworld, most notably in the poem Death of Gilgamesh and in several incantations. His main attribute was a ram-headed crook, and he likely also carried a fleece. God lists present him as a son of Utu/Shamash.

Worship and religion

His cult was limited in Mesopotamia. He is attested in documents from Kish, Ur, Lagash and Fara, and in the Iron Age at Assur, where he was worshipped alongside the god Urmah. In western Syria, under the name Šamagan, he had centers at Ebla and Nabada, and mule overseers made a special sacrifice to him. There is no evidence that his cult survives today.

Ancient sources: He appears in god lists such as An = Anum and Anu ša amēli, in the poem Death of Gilgamesh, in incantations, and in Hittite sources, where he is written with the logogram SUMUQAN.

Trivia

His wife. Šumugan was married to Ellamesi, regarded as the goddess of wool. Unlike Dumuzi, another agricultural god, he was rarely portrayed in romantic settings.
His divine attendant. According to a seal inscription from Lagash, his sukkal, or attendant deity, was Edinmugi, whose name means 'he secured the plains'.
