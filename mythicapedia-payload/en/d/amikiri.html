cutter of nets, Japanese yōkai

Amikiri

The amikiri is a water-dwelling yōkai of Japanese folklore, whose name means "net cutter". Most of what is known about it comes from early modern illustrations and stories, so its folk origin is uncertain.

The myth

According to descriptions, the amikiri lives in rivers, lakes and coastal waters, especially where fishermen work, and is rarely seen out of the water. Its appearance combines lobster-like pincers, a serpent's tail and a bird's head. Some accounts describe it as a small, domestic animal, others as a creature the size of a child. It is blamed for cutting fishing nets with its pincers, yet it is generally regarded as peaceful, and it is said it may become a friend of those who respect nature. Some tales add that it can take human form for brief moments when it sets foot on land.

Its best-known image comes from Toriyama Sekien's Gazu Hyakki Yagyō (1776), which depicts it without any explanatory text. Sekien may have borrowed it from a similar yōkai in the emaki Hyakkai zukan (1737), or invented it by playing on the word ami, which means both "net" and "mysid shrimp". A modern account by Yamada, published in 1974, places in the Shōnai region of Yamagata Prefecture a story of nets and mosquito nets torn to shreds and blamed on the amikiri. Researchers have found no trace of that story elsewhere and believe Yamada probably invented it.

Legacy

The amikiri appears as a character in the fighting game Gekka no Kenshi (Last Blade), in all of its versions.

Ancient sources: Ancient texts barely mention it. Its earliest known appearance is Toriyama Sekien's illustration in the Gazu Hyakki Yagyō (1776), and a similar yōkai appears in the emaki Hyakkai zukan (1737).

Trivia

A play on words. The name may rest on a double meaning: ami means "net" and also a small mysid shrimp, which may have inspired Sekien.
A story with mosquito nets. In a modern tale from Shōnai, the amikiri also tears mosquito nets, leaving the villagers covered in bites.
