Greek paradise of heroes and the righteous

Elysium

Elysium is the Greek afterlife reserved for heroes, those favoured by the gods and the righteous. Its conception widened over time, and its image of a bright paradise without old age or death lived on in later literature and everyday language.

The myth

In the earliest Greek accounts, Elysium lay apart from the realm of Hades, and only mortals related to the gods and certain heroes could enter. Over time access widened to include those chosen by the gods, the righteous and the heroic, who lived there a blessed existence and could enjoy whatever they had loved in life.

Homer placed these fields on the western edge of the earth, beside the stream of Oceanus, and in the Odyssey Rhadamanthus dwells there. Hesiod and Pindar name Cronus as ruler, and Pindar reduces the Isles of the Blessed to one island of shaded parks, where residents took part in athletic and musical pastimes. Virgil's Aeneid has Aeneas visit his father Anchises in this land of eternal spring, with its own sun and stars, where some exercise in the wrestling grounds and others sing and dance with Orpheus.

The shades there have no flesh or bone, yet keep the look of eternal youth. Some accounts even allow a return to the world of the living, though few managed it. Similar paradises existed too, such as the island of Leuce in the Black Sea, where tradition placed Achilles after death.

Worship and religion

Elysium was the object of belief among some Greek religious and philosophical sects, who saw it as the destination of virtuous souls. Greek funerary traditions, however, were reticent about describing it in detail.

Legacy

The name passed into Latin and modern languages as a byword for paradise. In Paris, the Champs-Élysées keep the name first applied in the late sixteenth century to rural land beyond the Tuileries gardens. By extension, "l'Élysée" names the French presidential palace, and in the arts the idea appears in Schiller's Ode to Joy, which Beethoven set in his Ninth Symphony, in Mozart's The Magic Flute and in Cervantes, among others.

Ancient sources: Homer's Odyssey and Hesiod's Works and Days are the main references, alongside Pindar's Odes and Virgil's Aeneid.
