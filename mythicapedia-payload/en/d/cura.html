Roman personification of care

Cura

Cura is the personification of care and devotion in Roman mythology. According to Hyginus, she shaped the first human from clay, and her story later caught the attention of the philosopher Heidegger.

The myth

In Hyginus' fable 220, Cura was crossing a river when she noticed clay-rich earth, gathered it and began to mold a man. While she pondered what to do with him, Jupiter appeared and, at her request, granted him life. Cura wanted to give the creature her own name, but Jupiter forbade it. Then Tellus, the Earth, arrived and claimed the name, since she had provided the body.

To settle the dispute, Saturn was asked to judge, and he ruled fairly: the spirit would belong to Jupiter after death, the body to Tellus, and Cura would possess the being while it lived. Because it seemed made of humus, earth, it was named homo, "human being."

Legacy

Heidegger read the fable as a naive interpretation of his concept of Dasein, being-in-the-world, discussed in Section 42 of Being and Time. For him, care means absorption in the world and devotion, not mere worry.

Ancient sources: Hyginus, Fabulae, fable 220.

Trivia

A female creator. In the story, the creator of the first human is a woman, Cura, not a god. Heidegger saw in this a contrast with the Christian myth, in which woman is created last.
A judge for the dispute. To decide who owned the creature, the disputants chose Saturn, a god associated with time, as arbiter.
