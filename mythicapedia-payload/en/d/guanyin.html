Chinese bodhisattva of compassion

Guanyin

Guanyin is the Chinese name of Avalokiteśvara, the bodhisattva of compassion honoured in Mahāyāna Buddhism and in popular religion across East Asia. Among the most beloved figures in Buddhism, she is invoked by those facing danger or hoping to conceive a child.

The myth

In Buddhist tradition, Avalokiteśvara is a bodhisattva who hears the cries of the world and comes to the aid of anyone who calls on him. The Lotus Sutra, in its twenty-fifth chapter, describes him as able to take any form, divine, royal, human or non-human, male or female, in order to teach the Dharma.

The Chinese name Guanyin is short for Guanshiyin, "one who perceives the sounds of the world". Originally depicted as male, the figure became mostly female in China and much of East Asia from about the twelfth century. A vow keeps her from becoming a Buddha until all beings are freed from the cycle of birth, death and rebirth.

In Taoism, which tells other stories of her origin, she is known as Cihang Zhenren and regarded as an immortal. Local traditions in China and elsewhere have added many legends and distinctive traits to her figure.

Worship and religion

Many temples and shrines across East Asia are dedicated to Guanyin, among them Sensō-ji, Kiyomizu-dera and Sanjūsangen-dō in Japan, and Mount Putuo in China, regarded as her abode. Korea has a pilgrimage of thirty-three temples. Her devotion remains alive: believers invoke her in dangers involving water, fire or weapons, and to ask for children. Her enlightenment is celebrated on the nineteenth day of the sixth lunar month.

Legacy

Her image is a frequent subject of Asian art and is held in most museums around the world. The camera maker Canon Inc. was named after an older spelling of her Japanese name. Some Buddhists believe Guanyin places the dead in the heart of a lotus and sends them to the western Pure Land of Sukhāvatī.

Ancient sources: Her name appears in the Lotus Sutra, whose twenty-fifth chapter is devoted to her, and in the Kāraṇḍavyūha Sūtra, which contains the mantra Oṃ maṇi padme hūṃ.

Trivia

A goddess who is not one. Buddhists regard her as a bodhisattva, not a goddess, although in the West she is often called one through confusion with divinities.
