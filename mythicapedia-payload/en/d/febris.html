Roman goddess of fever

Febris

Febris is the Roman goddess of fever and malaria. She embodied this illness, but people sought her favour to keep it away from them, so she was both feared and worshipped.

The myth

Febris has no myth of her own and stars in no known story. Seneca, in the Apocolocyntosis, gives her as characteristic traits shrewdness and honesty.

Tradition gave her two daughters or sisters, Dea Tertiana and Dea Quartana, goddesses of the fevers that returned every three or four days. Theodorus Priscianus names Saturn as the father of both. She may have originated from Februus, an Etruscan god of purification.

Worship and religion

Rome had at least three temples to Febris: the oldest lay between the Palatine and the Velabrum, according to Valerius Maximus and Cicero; another stood on the Esquiline and a third on the Vicus Longus. The sick visited them wearing protective amulets, especially against malaria, and invoked her with the formula Febris diva, Febris sancta, Febris magna. Her cult may have survived in medieval Rome under the name Madonna delle Febbri.

Legacy

Seneca has her appear with Hercules in the Apocolocyntosis, where she exposes the lies of Emperor Claudius. In 1473, Angelo Poliziano included her in his Latin poem In Albieram Albitiam, showing her with a torch and a snowball, riding a chariot pulled by monstrous lions, and she gives poison to the young Albiera degli Albizzi. The English word fever comes from the Latin febris.

Ancient sources: Seneca (Apocolocyntosis), Valerius Maximus and Cicero, who describe her temples.

Trivia

Origin of the word fever. The Latin febris is the source of the English fever. Its Indo-European root relates to the idea of burning or warming.
Inscriptions far from Rome. Inscriptions dedicated to Quartana have been found in Nemausus, in Gaul, and to Tertiana in Habitancum, in northern England.
