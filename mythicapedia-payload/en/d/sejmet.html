Egyptian lioness goddess of war and healing

Sekhmet

Sekhmet is a goddess of ancient Egypt, daughter of the sun god Ra, associated with war, vengeance, strength and also medicine. Her importance is reflected in the many statues and amulets bearing her image as a woman with a lioness's head.

The myth

In Egyptian tradition, Sekhmet was the vengeful manifestation of Ra's power, known as the Eye of Ra. She was said to breathe fire, and the scorching desert winds were considered her breath. Plagues were also attributed to her, seen as her servants or messengers, yet she was also invoked to ward off disease and heal the sick.

The best-known myth tells of the end of Ra's rule on earth. Enraged when mortals rebelled against him, Ra sent Sekhmet, in the form of the goddess Hathor, to punish them. She slaughtered the population until the Nile ran red. Fearing she would wipe out humanity, Ra had beer dyed with red ochre so it looked like blood. Sekhmet drank it, became drunk and gave up the slaughter. In another version, the goddess, angered by the deception, left Egypt and the sun lost its strength, until the god Thoth persuaded her to return.

According to the stories, Sekhmet was the firstborn daughter of Ra and the wife of Ptah, and with them and their son Nefertum she formed the Memphite triad. Some sources also present her as the mother of the war god Maahes and the sister of the cat goddess Bastet.

Worship and religion

Sekhmet was worshipped at the temple of Mut in Karnak, and also in Luxor, Memphis, Leontopolis and the Delta. To appease her wrath, the blood of sacrificed animals was offered, and priests performed a daily ritual before a statue of her. Celebrations followed battles to calm her. During the Festival of Intoxication at the beginning of the year, Egyptians danced and drank wine and beer to imitate the drunkenness that stopped the goddess.

Trivia

More than 700 statues. It is estimated that there were more than 700 statues of Sekhmet in the temple of Amenhotep III, which explains the vast number of images of the goddess that survive.
A porch of drunkenness. Excavations at the temple of Mut in Luxor revealed a "porch of drunkenness" built by the pharaoh Hatshepsut, linked to the festival.
