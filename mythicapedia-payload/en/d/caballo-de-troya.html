wooden horse of the Trojan War

Trojan Horse

The Trojan Horse is a huge wooden horse that, in Greek mythology, the Greeks used to sneak into the city of Troy and win the war. It is one of the best-known episodes of the Trojan War cycle, and it gave its name to the expressions "Trojan horse" and "Greek gift".

The myth

According to the myth, the Greeks had besieged Troy for more than nine years and, even after the death of Achilles, still could not break through the walls. The seer Calchas watched a dove chased by a hawk: the hawk feigned retreat, the dove peeked out, and the bird of prey caught it. Calchas concluded that force would not work and that they needed a stratagem. Odysseus devised a wooden horse with the best warriors hidden inside. In other versions, the idea came from Athena.

Epeius, the best carpenter in the camp, built the horse with a hidden hatch in its side and an inscription dedicating it to Athena as an offering for a safe return. The Greeks burned their tents and pretended to sail away to Tenedos. They left behind Sinon, who convinced the Trojans that the horse was a gift to the goddess and that the Greeks would never return if it was not brought into the city. The Trojans tore down part of their walls to pull it inside. While they celebrated their supposed victory, the hidden warriors slipped out at night and opened the gates, and the army that had sailed back under cover of darkness entered and destroyed the city.

Warnings were ignored. The priest Laocoon cried out his famous warning, and Cassandra, the prophetess, foretold the city's fall, but no one believed them. In some versions, Poseidon sent two sea serpents to strangle Laocoon and his sons; the Bibliotheca instead blames Apollo, offended by the priest.

Worship and religion

The horse had no cult of its own, but the myth links its origin to an offering to Athena. According to Quintus of Smyrna, Epeius later dedicated the tools he had used to build it to the goddess, and Aristotelian writings record that they were kept as sacred offerings in a sanctuary of Athena near Metapontum.

Legacy

The name passed into everyday language as a metaphor for any trick that lures an enemy into a protected stronghold. In computing, a "Trojan" is a malicious program that deceives users into running it willingly. The scene has inspired poems, paintings, sculptures, films, novels and cartoons, and in recent times several hypothetical reconstructions of the horse have been proposed.

Ancient sources: Homer's Odyssey mentions it briefly; Euripides' Trojan Women (415 BC) alludes to it, and Virgil's Aeneid offers the most extensive and familiar account.

Trivia

An epithet of Apollo. Pausanias explains that the epithet of Apollo "Carneus" might come from sacred cornel trees in a grove of the god on Mount Ida, which the Greeks cut down to build the horse.
A rationalized version. Palaephatus, who sought to explain Greek mythology rationally, claimed that the horse was too large to pass through the gates and that the Greeks waited in a hollow place near the city.
