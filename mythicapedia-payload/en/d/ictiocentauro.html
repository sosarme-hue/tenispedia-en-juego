sea-centaur of late Classical Greek art

Ichthyocentaur

The ichthyocentaur is a sea-centaur shown in late Classical Greek art, with a human upper body, horse forelegs and a fish tail. It is best understood as a variant of the Tritons, and the name itself is a modern coinage that was not used in antiquity.

The myth

Ancient sources say little about the ichthyocentaur as a character in a story. Its presence is mainly visual, in reliefs and mosaics showing the marine retinues of Poseidon and Aphrodite. Tradition links it especially to Aphros, the personification of sea foam, and to Bythos, the personification of the sea depths.

According to the Suda, Aphros was a king of ancient Libya and the progenitor of the Aphroi, that is, the Carthaginians, and he was a son of Cronos and Philyra. This parentage would make him a brother of the centaur Chiron, who is also a son of Cronos and Philyra in Pseudo-Apollodorus. It has also been suggested that Aphros may have been regarded as Aphrodite's foster-father, given the similarity of their names.

Legacy

The term was coined late by the Byzantine writer John Tzetzes, in the twelfth century, in his commentary on Lycophron. In the modern age it was used in commentaries on classical art, and the scholar Henri van de Waal grouped it with "sea-centaur" and "centaur-triton". Conrad Gessner described a "sea-satyr" as an ichthyocentaur in his Historia animalium (1558), and the figure appears on some heraldic devices.

Ancient sources: The Suda names Aphros as a son of Cronos and Philyra, and Claudian describes in his epithalamium a Triton with bull's forelegs that may be considered an ichthyocentaur.

Trivia

A modern name. The word ichthyocentaur does not appear in classical antiquity; it was coined in the twelfth century by John Tzetzes.
Aphros in Tunisia. A mosaic found in Tunisia shows Aphros swimming beside Poseidon's chariot, next to a twin-tailed Triton of Lake Tritonis.
