Māori god of forests and birds

Tāne

Tāne is the Māori god of forests and birds, son of the sky father Ranginui and the earth mother Papatūānuku. His best-known deed, forcing his parents apart so that the world could open to light, makes him a key figure in Māori creation stories.

The myth

According to Māori tradition, Ranginui, the sky father, and Papatūānuku, the earth mother, lay in a tight embrace, while their children lived in the darkness between them. Frustrated by the confinement, the brothers debated what to do. Tū proposed killing their parents, but Tāne preferred separating them: the sky would rise and the earth would stay below to care for her children.

His brothers tried and failed. Tāne lay on his back and pushed with his strong legs until he forced his parents apart. Afterwards, to dress up his father the sky, he set stars, the Moon and the Sun in it.

Tāwhirimātea, god of storms, was enraged and punished earth and sea with gales that tore up Tāne's forests. The fish and reptiles of Tangaroa fled, and Tangaroa has resented Tāne ever since, which is why Tāne gave humans canoes, fishhooks and nets to catch his runaway children.

Another tradition says Tāne sought a wife and, after fathering insects, birds and plants with non-human females, shaped a woman from earth. In some stories he unknowingly married his daughter Hine-tītama, who fled to the underworld and became the goddess of death, Hine-nui-te-pō. Finally, he is credited with bringing down three baskets of knowledge from the heavens.

Trivia

God of Tahiti. On Tahiti, Tāne was revered as the god of peace and beauty, a role different from his figure in New Zealand.
The three baskets of knowledge. Some traditions say Tāne brought down three baskets of knowledge from the heavens for human beings.
