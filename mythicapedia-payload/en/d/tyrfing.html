cursed sword of Norse mythology

Tyrfing

Tyrfing is a cursed sword from Norse mythology, notorious for killing a man every time it is drawn from its scabbard. Its story, told in a poem of the Poetic Edda and in the Hervarar saga, shows how a curse placed by dwarves weighs on everyone who wields it.

The myth

According to the tradition, King Svafrlami, a descendant of Odin, forced some dwarves to forge a sword with a golden hilt that never missed a stroke and could cut through stone and iron as easily as cloth. The dwarves, furious at the deal, cursed it: it would kill a man each time it was drawn, cause three great evils, and eventually kill its owner too. Svafrlami died at the hands of the berserker Arngrim, who took the sword and the king's daughter.

Arngrim's sons died in a duel near Samsø, and his daughter Hervor, raised without knowing her parentage, armed herself as a shieldmaiden and travelled to her father's burial mound to reclaim the blade. Angantyr's ghost, faced with her threat of the curse of annihilation, gave the sword up willingly.

The curse then took its course. Heidrek, Hervor's son, killed his brother with the sword after drawing it in front of him. Later, after defeating a disguised Odin in a riddle contest, he was murdered in his sleep by thralls, and his son recovered the weapon. According to the story, Angantyr finally used it to slay Hlod, which satisfied the dwarves' curse.

Legacy

The sword appears in Richard Wagner's opera, where King Alberich hands it over to Odin. It has also been adopted into many video games, including Fire Emblem: Seisen no Keifu, Castlevania: Symphony of the Night, Final Fantasy III, God of War: Ragnarök, Soul Calibur V and VI, and Smash Legends.

Ancient sources: It features in the Hervararkviða, the poem from the Poetic Edda known as The Awakening of Angantyr, and in the Hervarar saga.

Trivia

Able to kill a god. Some accounts suggest the sword could kill a god. King Heidrek struck at Odin in disguise, forcing the god to transform into a hawk to escape the blow.
A disputed name. Scholars disagree on the origin of the name. One theory links it to the Thervingi, a Gothic tribe recorded by Roman sources in the 4th century.
The finger of the war god. Another interpretation splits the name into Tyr, the god of war, and fingr, the Old Norse word for finger, meaning roughly the finger of Tyr.
