the Black Tortoise, guardian of the north in Chinese mythology

Xuanwu

Xuanwu, the Black Tortoise, is one of the Four Symbols of the Chinese constellations and stands for the north, winter, water and night. Its image, a tortoise intertwined with a snake, is one of the most recognisable emblems in Chinese symbolism, and it travelled to Japan under the name Genbu.

The myth

According to Chinese legend, Xuanwu was originally a prince who, rather than inheriting the throne, left his parents at sixteen to study Taoism. Having attained divine status, he was worshipped as a deity of the northern sky.

To complete his purification he had to rid his body of human flesh. A god replaced his stomach and intestines with divine organs, and the originals turned into a tortoise and a snake that terrorised the land. Xuanwu returned to subdue them but, as both showed remorse, he put them under his command as the Tortoise General and the Snake General. Another version says that his sins, dissolved into a river as he washed his entrails, took the form of a black tortoise and a snake.

Worship and religion

Xuanwu had a temple in the Wudang Mountains of Hubei. The name is also used for a Taoist deity, and in Japan the figure appears as Genbu, one of the four guardians that protect Kyoto on its northern side, represented by the Kenkun Shrine on top of Mount Funaoka.

Legacy

In the classic novel Journey to the West, Xuanwu is a king of the north served by two generals. Their names live on in the landscape: on opposite banks of a river near Wuhan stand a Tortoise Mountain and a Snake Mountain. The figure also appears on the north wall of the burial chamber of the Kitora tomb in Asuka.

Ancient sources: Xu Shen's Shuowen jiezi and Zhang Hua's Bowu zhi record the belief that tortoises lacked males and mated with snakes, while the novel Journey to the West includes Xuanwu as a king of the north.

Trivia

A tortoise that gave rise to an insult. From the belief that tortoises mated with snakes comes the use of 'tortoise' as an insult for a cuckolded husband, attested from the late Yuan dynasty.
A name for imperial gates. The northern gates of Chinese palaces were often named after Xuanwu. At the north gate of Taiji Palace in Chang'an, Li Shimin seized power in a coup in which his brothers were killed.
