Japanese god of mountains, seas and war

Ōyamatsumi

Ōyamatsumi is a deity of Japanese mythology, lord of mountains, seas and war. As an elder brother of Amaterasu, Tsukuyomi and Susanoo, he appears in several of the best-known episodes of Japan's divine cycle.

The myth

In the Kojiki, Ōyamatsumi was born between Izanagi and Izanami, in the section known as Kamiumi. With the goddess Kaya-no-hime he fathered eight kami, among them Kamuōichihime, Konohanachiruhime, Iwanagahime and Ashinazuchi. The Nihon Shoki tells it differently: he is born when Izanagi slashes his child Kagutsuchi, the god of fire.

Tradition also links him to Susanoo's story. Ashinazuchi and Tenazuchi, the elderly couple of Izumo, appear in the myth of Yamata no Orochi as the parents of Kushinadahime, the young woman Susanoo saves from the monster. Some medieval genealogies make them children of Ōyamatsumi, but the Kojiki gives no parentage for them. His line also intertwines with Susanoo's until it reaches Ōkuninushi.

The most famous episode is the descent of Ninigi, grandson of Amaterasu. Ōyamatsumi offers Ninigi his daughters Sakuyahime and Iwanagahime. Ninigi accepts Sakuyahime but sends Iwanagahime back because of her appearance. Ōyamatsumi is angered and explains that, with Iwanagahime, he meant the heavenly grandson's life to last as long as a rock; by rejecting her, human life was shortened.

Worship and religion

His most important shrine is Ōyamazumi Shrine, on the island of Ōmishima. The accounts also associate him with Awaga Shrine in Santō, Hyōgo Prefecture, where a text recording his genealogies was kept. Like other mountain kami, he was venerated for his connection to the mountains and the waters that rise from them.

Ancient sources: He appears in the Kojiki and the Nihon Shoki, whose genealogies do not always agree.

Trivia

Two births. In the Kojiki he is born from Izanagi and Izanami, while in the Nihon Shoki he emerges when Izanagi splits his son Kagutsuchi in two.
A rejected rock. Ninigi sent Iwanagahime back because of her appearance, and Ōyamatsumi explained that with her the heavenly grandson's life would have been as lasting as a rock.
