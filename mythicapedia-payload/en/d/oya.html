Yoruba goddess of winds, storms and the dead

Oyá

Oyá (Oya) is one of the principal female deities of the Yoruba pantheon, mistress of winds, storms and lightning. Her close bond with death and cemeteries makes her a central figure in Yoruba religion and its Afro-American forms.

The myth

According to tradition, Oyá lived as a woman in Ira and became a wife of Shango, ruler of Oyo. A Cuban patakí tells that she was the youngest of three sisters, daughters of Olofin, and was kidnapped by enemies; her sister Oshún paid for her freedom with her virginity. Since Oyá received no domain when the earthly goods were shared out, Olofin gave her the cemetery.

Another account says that, after years of infertility, a babalawo advised her to make an offering, and she bore nine children, earning the name Iyansan, 'mother of nine'.

Worship and religion

Her worship spread first through the territory of Oyo and reached the New World with the Atlantic slave trade. Followers offer ochre-coloured fruit, eggplant and acarajé, and her colour is burgundy or brown. Her cult remains alive in Cuban Santería and Brazilian Candomblé.

Legacy

In Cuba and Brazil she is syncretized with Teresa of Ávila, Saint Barbara or the Virgin of Candelaria, depending on the region. Acarajé, a fried patty of black-eyed peas, is both a ritual food and a street delicacy in Bahia.

Trivia

The number nine. Her number is 9 and its multiples, and she carries nine otá stones gathered from the river. Her attributes include nine copper bracelets.
Copper tools. The copper bracelets commemorate the sacrifice of her sister Oshún, who gathered copper coins to pay for her ransom.
