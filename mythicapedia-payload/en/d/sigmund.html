hero of Norse mythology, father of Sigurd

Sigmund

Sigmund is a hero of Norse and Germanic mythology, the son of Völsung and father of Sigurd, the dragon-slayer. His story, shaped by revenge, broken family ties and tragedy, is one of the great sagas of northern Europe.

The myth

According to the Völsunga saga, Sigmund is the son of Völsung and brother of Signy, who marries King Siggeir of Gautland. During the wedding feast, Odin plunges the sword Gram into the tree Barnstokkr, which stands by Völsung's hall, and promises it to whoever can pull it out. Only Sigmund succeeds. Siggeir, consumed by envy, later invites the family to his kingdom, attacks them by surprise, kills Völsung and puts his sons in stocks. Each night a she-wolf, Siggeir's mother in another shape, devours one of them, until only Sigmund is left. Signy manages to keep him alive, and later has a son with him, Sinfjötli, whom she tests. Together they take revenge on Siggeir and kill his sons.

Sigmund later marries Borghild, with whom he has two sons, and then Hjördís. In a battle against King Lyngi, an old man who is Odin in disguise shatters Sigmund's sword, and the hero falls. Dying, he tells Hjördís that she is pregnant and that her son will forge the fragments of the blade. That son is Sigurd.

Legacy

Sigmund appears in Richard Wagner's Die Walküre, part of Der Ring des Nibelungen, where he is called Siegmund and enters an incestuous romance with his sister Sieglinde, from which Siegfried is born. Arthur Gilchrist Brodeur's novelette Vengeance (1925) retells his revenge against Siggeir.

Ancient sources: The Völsunga saga and the Anglo-Saxon poem Beowulf, which mentions Sigemund as a slayer of a dragon, together with the Poetic Edda.

Trivia

A dragon in Beowulf. In the Anglo-Saxon poem Beowulf, Sigemund kills a serpent guarding a hoard and carries the treasure away by ship while the monster melts.
A broken sword. In Wagner's opera, Wotan shatters Siegmund's sword with his spear, and Siegfried later reforges it.
