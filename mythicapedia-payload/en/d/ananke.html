Greek goddess of necessity and fate

Ananke

Ananke is the personification of inescapable necessity and fate in Greek mythology, one of the primordial powers of the cosmos. According to the tradition, neither mortals nor gods could escape her force, and in some accounts the Moirai, the Greek Fates, were her daughters.

The myth

In the Orphic tradition, Ananke emerged at the dawn of time alongside Chronos, time itself, and the two took the form of a serpent coiled around the universe. Their union was said to have broken the primal egg, whose parts became earth, heaven and sea, creating the ordered cosmos. In these texts she is less a spouse in the human sense than a power that gives order to the passage of time, and she is also linked with Adrasteia, the distributor of rewards and punishments.

Ancient sources do not agree on her parentage. Orphic texts present her as self-formed and make her the mother, or another form, of Adrasteia, while other authors do not name her parents at all. In the Orphic Rhapsodies she is said to have been born before the Moirai, and Zeus, raised by Adrasteia, is said to have fathered Himarmene, or Destiny, with her.

Plato, in the Republic, places Ananke in the heavens, turning the spindle that makes the celestial spheres revolve. Three women sit around her, the Moirai, her daughters: Lachesis sings of the past, Clotho of the present and Atropos of the future. Aeschylus, in Prometheus Bound, makes the Moirai and the Erinyes the helmsmen of Ananke, and Prometheus insists that even Zeus cannot escape what has been foretold about a son he might have with Thetis.

Worship and religion

Ananke had no cult of her own in the usual sense. Pausanias writes that on the acropolis of Corinth there was a sanctuary where Ananke and Bia, Force, were worshipped together, and entry was restricted to initiates. Her role in religious life was therefore exceptional, and in philosophy she was treated more as a principle than as an object of devotion. No living cult is documented today.

Legacy

The Latin name Necessitas made her the necessity of Roman tradition. In philosophy the term came to mean logical necessity or the laws of nature. In modern literature, Victor Hugo wrote the word Ananke on a wall of Notre-Dame in his novel. Sigmund Freud cited her in Civilization and Its Discontents as the force of reality that compels individuals to live together, and Wallace Stevens invoked her in a poem of the 1930s.

Ancient sources: Plato in the Republic and the Timaeus, Aeschylus in Prometheus Bound, Homer, Simonides, Parmenides, Empedocles, Pausanias, the Orphic hymns and rhapsodies, and the Suda.

Trivia

A shared shrine. Pausanias describes a sanctuary in Corinth where Ananke shared worship with Bia, Force, an unusual arrangement for such an abstract figure.
A word on a wall. In Victor Hugo's novel Notre-Dame de Paris, the word Ananke is carved on a wall of the cathedral as a symbol of fatal destiny.
