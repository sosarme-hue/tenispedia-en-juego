Greek goddess of the night

Nyx

Nyx is one of the primordial deities of Greek mythology and the personification of the night. She appears in early cosmogonies, in Orphic poetry and in the works of Hesiod and Homer, and Roman authors knew her as Nox.

The myth

In Hesiod's Theogony, Nyx was born from Chaos, and with Erebus, the Darkness, she became the mother of Aether and Hemera, the Day, who embody the opposite of their parents' nature. Without a father, she produced a long line of abstract figures, mostly negative, among them Moros, Ker, Thanatos, Hypnos, the Oneiroi, Momus, Oizys, the Hesperides, the Moirai, the Keres, Nemesis, Apate, Philotes, Geras and Eris. Hesiod places her home at the far western edge of the Earth, near the entrance to the underworld and wrapped in dark clouds. Each day she and Hemera pass each other at the threshold, one leaving as the other enters.

The genealogies vary between authors. Hesiod makes her a daughter of Chaos, as does Nonnus in the Dionysiaca, but other early sources make her the mother of Tartarus by Aether, the mother of Eros by Aether, or the mother of Uranus. Cicerón and Hyginus give their own lists of descendants with Erebus, and Euripides calls Lyssa, Madness, her daughter with Uranus.

In the Iliad, Homer treats her more as a natural element than as a person, but he recounts how Hypnos took refuge with her from the wrath of Zeus, since even the king of the gods feared displeasing swift Night. In Orphic poetry her role grows far larger: she is the first principle, the nurse of the gods and the mother of Uranus, and from a cave she delivers prophecies to Zeus.

Worship and religion

In Greece Nyx received little cult of her own. According to Pausanias she had an oracle on the acropolis of Megara, and she was honoured indirectly within other cults, such as that of Artemis at Ephesus, where a statue called Night stood. The Spartans worshipped Sleep and Death, regarded as twins, whose mother she would have been. In Rome, according to Ovidius and Statius, offerings were made to her during the Lemuria, including carefully chosen black bulls.

Legacy

Nyx has inspired the image of night as a winged woman driving a horse-drawn chariot, a frequent motif in Greek art alongside Selene, Helios and Eos. Her name survives in the Latin Nox and in scientific vocabulary, where the prefix nyx- appears in words related to night.

Ancient sources: Hesiod's Theogony, Orphic poetry, Homer's Iliad, Euripides, Cicero and Hyginus.

Trivia

A goddess even Zeus would not cross. In the Iliad, Hypnos escapes Zeus's wrath by taking refuge with Nyx, since the king of the gods feared displeasing her.
A mother in many versions. Different ancient authors made Nyx the daughter of Chaos, the mother of Uranus or the mother of Eros, showing that her origins were not fixed.
