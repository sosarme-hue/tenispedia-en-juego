Knight of the Swan

The Knight of the Swan is a hero of medieval European literature who arrives in a boat pulled by a swan to defend a maiden unjustly accused. His story gave rise to a legendary lineage and matters for its influence on the chansons de geste and on Wagner's opera.

The myth

According to the tradition, a nameless knight appears in a boat drawn by a swan just as a young woman, accused of killing her brother to claim their father's inheritance, is about to be condemned. The knight defends her and wins her hand on one condition: she must never ask him where he comes from or what his name is. Prompted by a sorceress, she breaks the promise. The knight then reveals that he is a guardian of the Holy Grail, frees the swan from its enchantment and leaves, because a divine law obliges him to return to his duty.

The versions differ. In the Dolopathos by John of Alta Silva (around 1190), the swan belongs to a family of enchanted brothers, and the tale is related to folk stories about brothers turned into birds. In the Old French Crusade cycle of chansons de geste, the hero is called Helias or Chevalier au Cigne and becomes the legendary ancestor of Godfrey of Bouillon. His mother appears as Elioxe, Beatrix or Isomberte depending on the text. Wolfram von Eschenbach named him Loherangrin in his Parzival, in the early thirteenth century.

Legacy

Richard Wagner drew on the swan knights of Wolfram and Konrad von Würzburg for the libretto of his opera Lohengrin, premiered in Weimar in 1850. Scholars often regard the story as a blend of chivalric motifs and folk tales about siblings transformed into swans.

Ancient sources: John of Alta Silva's Dolopathos (around 1190), the Spanish Gran conquista de Ultramar, and Wolfram von Eschenbach's Parzival.

Trivia

A chained swan. In the Dolopathos it is hinted that the swan is the one that pulled an armed knight in a boat by a golden chain.
Brangemuer. Brangemuer is another knight found dead in a boat pulled by a swan. His adventure was taken up by Guerrehet, Gawain's brother, in a continuation of Chrétien de Troyes' Perceval.
