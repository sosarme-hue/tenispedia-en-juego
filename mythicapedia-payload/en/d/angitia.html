Angitia

Angitia was a goddess of the Marsi, the Paeligni and other Oscan-Umbrian peoples of central Italy, associated with healing, snakes and the snake-charmers who claimed her as their ancestor. She matters because she preserves a local Italic tradition that Roman writers reinterpreted in their own terms.

The myth

Accounts of Angitia vary between authors. According to Gnaeus Gellius, she was one of the three daughters of Aeëtes, alongside Medea and Circe, two of the most famous sorceresses of Greek myth. Servius instead identifies her with Medea, who after leaving Colchis reached Italy with Jason, taught the local people how to charm snakes and cure their bites, and became known there as Angitia.

Boccaccio presents her as Circe's sister and a daughter of the Sun, and places her near Lake Fucino, where she practised her healing arts. Macrobius calls her Angeronia, and Verrius Flaccus links her name to angores, the worries she was said to dispel.

Worship and religion

Her best-known sacred site was the grove at Luco dei Marsi, known in antiquity as Lucus Angitiae. Three inscriptions from there name her; the earliest, from the late 4th century BC, is a dedication made on behalf of the Marsic legions. Another inscription mentions a temple and a treasury. Macrobius refers to her festivals in Rome on 12 January, and she was believed to relieve anxieties and distress of the spirit. Her cult does not survive today.

Legacy

Her figure entered literature through the tradition that links her with Medea and Circe, and Boccaccio included her among the sorceresses of antiquity. The Romans explained her name from anguis, "serpent", which gave rise to the form Anguitia.

Ancient sources: Servius, Solinus (citing Gnaeus Gellius), Macrobius, Silius Italicus and Verrius Flaccus, along with inscriptions from Luco dei Marsi and Civita d'Antino and the Iguvine Tablets.

Trivia

A grove named for her. The sanctuary at Luco dei Marsi was known in Latin as Lucus Angitiae, "Sacred Grove of Angitia", showing how strong her cult was in the region.
A name tied to the serpent. The Romans derived her name from anguis, "serpent", reinforcing her link with snakebites and charms.
