Germanic and Norse deity, older name of Freyr

Yngvi

Yngvi is an ancient Germanic and Norse theonym linked to the god Freyr, whose older name seems to have been Ing. The figure also appears as a mythical ancestor of Germanic peoples and of the Scandinavian Yngling dynasty.

The myth

According to the tradition recorded by Tacitus, the first man, Mannus, had three sons, and one of them, Ingwaz, was said to be the ancestor of the Ingvaeones, peoples of the North Sea coast. Norse sources split the figure in several ways. In the Íslendingabók, written by Ari Þorgilsson in the early twelfth century, Yngvi is the father of Njörðr, who in turn is the father of Yngvi-Freyr, ancestor of the Ynglings.

The Skjöldunga saga says that Odin, coming from Asia, gave Sweden to his son Yngvi. In Snorri Sturluson's Ynglinga saga, Freyr reigns over Sweden as a human king after his father Njörðr. The Historia Norwegiae, by contrast, makes a certain Ingui the first king of Sweden, and his descendants venerated him and his sons as gods.

Worship and religion

The sources offer little evidence of a cult of his own. The Pietroassa ring, linked to the Goths, bears damaged runes that are read as a dedication «to Ingwi of the Goths, holy», which suggests local veneration. The Norse compounds Ingvifreyr and Ingunarfreyr show that the name was used as an epithet of Freyr, «lord of the Ing».

Legacy

The element Ing survives in Scandinavian and German names such as Ingvar, Ingmar, Ingrid, Ingeborg and Ingegerd, and in the English surname Ingalls. The rune ŋ of the Elder Futhark carries the reconstructed name Ingwaz. The Yngling dynasty gave its name to Scandinavian royal lineages, and the name Igor in several Slavic languages may derive from a Scandinavian form such as Ingvar.

Ancient sources: Tacitus in the Germania, Pliny the Elder in his Natural Histories, Beowulf, the Íslendingabók, Snorri Sturluson's Edda and the Historia Norwegiae.

Trivia

A ring with a dedication. The Pietroassa ring, found in Romania, carries runic inscriptions that, in one reading, dedicate the piece to Ingwi.
A name in Beowulf. The Old English poem Beowulf calls Hrothgar «fréa inguina», meaning «lord of the Ing».
