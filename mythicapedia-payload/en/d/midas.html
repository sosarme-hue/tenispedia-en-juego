king of Phrygia with the golden touch

Midas

Midas is a king of Phrygia, in Asia Minor, best known in Greek tradition for his power to turn everything he touched into gold. His story gave us the phrase «the Midas touch» and mixes mythological tales with a real king who ruled Phrygia in the late 8th century BCE.

The myth

According to the most widespread tale, Midas received from Dionysus the power to turn into gold everything he touched, as a reward for hosting Silenus, the old satyr and Dionysus's tutor, who had wandered off drunk into his land. Ovid tells how the king entertained him for ten days and nights before returning him to Dionysus, who offered Midas any reward he wished. Midas asked that whatever he touched should turn to gold, and at first he rejoiced in the power. Soon he found that an oak twig, a stone and every rose in his garden turned golden, and that his food and drink hardened in his hands. In some versions even his daughter became gold when she tried to comfort him. Starving and regretful, he begged the god to take the gift back. Dionysus told him to wash in the river Pactolus, and from then on its waters and sands carried gold.

Another episode says that Midas was a judge in the musical contest between Apollo and Pan, or in other accounts between Apollo and Marsyas. He preferred the rustic melody of the god of the fields, and Apollo, angered, gave him donkey's ears. These are brief episodes, but they show the idea running through the myth: gifts and poor judgments bring outsized consequences.

The ancestry varies among ancient authors. Herodotus mentions a Midas son of Gordias who dedicated a royal throne at Delphi. Arrian describes him as the son of Gordius, a poor peasant, and of a maiden of prophetic lineage, and says he was made king when he arrived with his cart just as the Phrygians sought a ruler. According to Hyginus, his sons included Lityerses, a cruel reaper who was killed by Heracles, and Anchurus, who sacrificed himself to save the city of Celaenae. Plutarch notes that the Phrygians claimed a goddess called Bona as Midas's mother, while others said he was the son of Cybele and Tmolus.

Legacy

The phrase «the Midas touch» describes the ability to turn anything into material success, and its reverse, the curse of wealth that prevents one from eating or enjoying life. Nathaniel Hawthorne retold the myth in 1852 in A Wonder-Book for Girls and Boys, with Midas's daughter turned to gold. The river Pactolus, linked to its golden sands, has kept the king's name in popular memory.

Ancient sources: Ovid (Metamorphoses XI), Herodotus, Hyginus, Arrian, Plutarch, Claudian and Aristotle relate episodes of Midas or mention him.

Trivia

A garden of roses. Herodotus describes a rose garden at the foot of Mount Bermion, which he calls the garden of Midas son of Gordias, where roses grew by themselves and bore sixty blossoms each.
A golden river. The river Pactolus, in Lydia, is said to have been renamed Chrysorrhoas, meaning «flowing with gold», after Midas bathed in it.
