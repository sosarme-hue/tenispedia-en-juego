Egyptian god of war and the sun, with a falcon's head

Montu

Montu was an Egyptian god with a falcon's head, linked to the sun and to war. He mattered most in the Middle Kingdom, when pharaohs saw him as the protector of the king in battle.

The myth

According to tradition, Montu began as the scorching heat of the sun, which is why many texts call him Montu-Ra. Over time that destructive force became martial strength. The Egyptians believed he attacked the enemies of Maat, the cosmic order, while inspiring glorious deeds in battle. His name, mnṯw, is usually rendered as 'nomad'.

Accounts of his family vary. The reference data makes him a son of Amun and Mut, while one source describes him as an adoptive son of both. His consorts were the Theban goddesses Iunit and Tenenet, along with Raet-Tawy, a female form of Ra. He was linked to the falcon, a symbol of the sky, and to the bull, a symbol of strength and war.

In the Middle Kingdom, especially from the 11th Dynasty, his role grew until he was for a time the supreme god, before Amun overtook him. Pharaohs identified with him as conquerors, and armies carried the insignia of the 'four Montu', one for each major cult centre. Several rulers of the 11th Dynasty were named Mentuhotep, 'Montu is satisfied', and Ramesses II called himself 'Montu of the Two Lands'.

Worship and religion

His main centre was Hermonthis, south of Thebes, and his cult spread to Thebes (Karnak), Medamud, Armant, Tod and Nubia. At Medamud, Senusret III built a temple where a living Buchis bull, an embodiment of the god, was kept. At Armant there was a great temple and a necropolis for these sacred bulls, with burials from the reign of Nectanebo II to that of Diocletian. His festival was held on the fifth day of the second month of the season of Akhet. After losing ground to Amun, his cult was revived in the Third Intermediate Period.

Legacy

His name survived in royal names: the Mentuhoteps, and Montuherkhepeshef, the name of several sons of Ramesses. The Greeks associated him with Ares, though also with Apollo because of his solar radiance. According to an inscription, Amenhotep II boasted of Montu's skill in shooting arrows at copper targets from a chariot, and Thutmose III was described as a 'Valiant Montu on the Battlefield'.

Trivia

A bull with servants. The Buchis bull, regarded as an embodiment of Montu, had dozens of attendants and wore precious crowns and bibs.
Pharaohs named after a god. Four pharaohs of the 11th Dynasty were called Mentuhotep, meaning 'Montu is satisfied'.
Ramesses at Kadesh. In the account of the Battle of Kadesh, Ramesses II charges at the enemy 'like Montu, Lord of Thebes'.
