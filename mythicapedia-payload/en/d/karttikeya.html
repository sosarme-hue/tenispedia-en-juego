Hindu god of war, son of Shiva and Parvati

Karttikeya

Karttikeya, also known as Skanda or Murugan, is the Hindu god of war, son of Shiva and Parvati and brother of Ganesha. Worshipped in India since ancient times, he is especially revered in the south, where the Tamil community regards him as one of its most beloved deities.

The myth

According to tradition, Karttikeya was born to defeat the demons who oppressed the gods, and accounts of his birth differ widely. In the Vedic texts, such as the Atharva-veda, the Taittiriya Samhita and the Shatapatha Brahmana, he is born without Parvati: Shiva's semen falls into the fire, Agni deposits it in the Ganges, and from the waters a child emerges, nursed by the six Krittikas, which is why he grows six heads.

The Kanda Purana tells another story: sparks from Shiva's eyes fall into Lake Saravana and become six boys. Parvati embraces them until they fuse into one, with six heads and three pairs of arms. The Mahabharata offers a third version, in which Agni fathers the child with Svaha.

The Shiva Purana explains why his birth was needed. The demon Tarakasura had received a boon from Brahma: he could only be killed by a son of Shiva. Since Shiva was absorbed in meditation, the gods arranged for him to marry Parvati. Karttikeya grew quickly, became a warrior and defeated demons such as Tarakasura, Simhamukha and Surapadma. After Surapadma was split in two, he turned into a peacock and a rooster, which became Karttikeya's mount and banner. His weapon is the vel, a spear that, according to legend, was given to him by his mother.

Worship and religion

Karttikeya is venerated mainly by the Kaumaram tradition of Hinduism, and his major temples are in South India. The Aru Padai Veedu are six shrines in Tamil Nadu dedicated to him. The temple at Kataragama, in Sri Lanka, draws Tamils, Sinhalese and Veddas. The festival of Thaipusam, celebrated by Tamil communities worldwide, commemorates the day Parvati gave him the vel. In North India he is worshipped as Mahasena and Kumara. His cult remains alive and has spread to Malaysia, Singapore, Mauritius, South Africa and Canada.

Legacy

In Tamil tradition he is regarded as a patron of the language and its literature. Two works devoted to him are the Tirumurukarrupadai by Nakkirar and the Tiruppukal by the poet-saint Arunagirinathar. His image appears in the sculpture and painting of medieval temples such as the caves of Ellora and Elephanta.

Ancient sources: He appears in the Ramayana, in the Mahabharata (Shalya Parva, Anushasana Parva and Vana Parva), in the Shiva Purana, in the Kanda Purana, and in the Vedic texts Atharva-veda, Taittiriya Samhita and Shatapatha Brahmana.

Trivia

A god of thieves. According to the Mahabharata, Karttikeya is also regarded as the god of thieves and swindlers, an unusual trait for a god of war.
A peacock as his mount. His mount is the peacock Paravani, and his banner bears a rooster, both originating in the legend of Surapadma's defeat.
