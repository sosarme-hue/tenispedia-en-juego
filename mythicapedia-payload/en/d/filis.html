Thracian princess of Greek mythology

Phyllis

Phyllis is a Thracian princess in Greek mythology, wife of Demophon, son of Theseus. Her story of waiting and abandonment, told in several versions with different endings, is one of the best-known love tales of classical antiquity.

The myth

According to the tradition, Phyllis was the daughter of a Thracian king, though some accounts name Sithon as her father and others give no name at all. Demophon, on his way home from the Trojan War, stopped in Thrace, where he married her. Duty to Greece then called him to Athens to help his father, and he left her behind.

Before parting, Phyllis gave him a casket holding a sacred object linked to Rhea, asking him to open it only if he gave up hope of returning. From here the stories split. In one version, Phyllis believed he would never come back and hanged herself from a tree; an almond tree grew over her grave and blossomed when Demophon returned. In another, she died of longing, and trees that shed their leaves at intervals grew from her tomb.

In a third tradition, after Phyllis's death Demophon opened the casket, was horrified by what he saw, and rode off at great speed. His horse stumbled and he fell onto his own sword. The legend also says Phyllis returned nine times to the shore to wait for him, which gave its name to the Nine Ways.

Legacy

Phyllis appears in Ovid's Heroides, the second poem, and in the Aitia of Callimachus. In pastoral poetry she became the simple, charming shepherdess in love, a theme Lope de Vega treated in a cycle of poems. Her name became a common female given name, with variants such as Phillida and Phyllida. The composer Thomas Morley mentions her in a song, and Jacob van Eyck wrote recorder pieces that refer to her. For centuries the hazel was called nux Phyllidos, and hazelnuts are still sometimes called filberts in English.

Ancient sources: Ovid presents her in the second book of his Heroides, and she also appears in the Aitia of Callimachus.
