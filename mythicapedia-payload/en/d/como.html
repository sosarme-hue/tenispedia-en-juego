Greek god of revelry and festivity

Comus

Comus is the Greek god of festivity, revelry and nocturnal merrymaking. Cup-bearer and companion of Dionysus, his name also designates the festive procession of singing and dancing revellers who honoured the god.

The myth

In Greek mythology Comus appears mostly as a late figure, with few tales of his own. He is shown as a winged youth or a youthful satyr, embodying the anarchy and chaos of drunkenness.

Ancient sources do not name his parents. The idea that he was a son of Bacchus and Circe comes from John Milton's masque of 1634, a later invention outside classical mythology.

The fullest description comes from Philostratus the Elder, a third-century AD Greek sophist, who depicts him in a painting as a youth drowsy with wine, head bowed, torch slipping from his hand and a wreath already withered. According to Philostratus, in his procession women acted like men and men wore women's clothing.

Worship and religion

At the festivals of Dionysus, Comus's revellers gave themselves over to song, dance and wine, and it is said that men and women exchanged clothes during the celebrations. No organised, lasting cult devoted to him is recorded.

Legacy

Comus has passed into later art and literature: he appears in John Milton's masque Comus and in Ben Jonson's Pleasure Reconciled to Virtue, in baroque operas such as Purcell's King Arthur, and in Saki's novel The Unbearable Bassington. The British progressive folk group Comus took its name from him, and New Orleans Mardi Gras krewes include the Mistick Krewe of Comus, founded in 1856.

Ancient sources: The earliest surviving description is Philostratus the Elder's Imagines (Eikones), from the 3rd century AD.

Trivia

A god with almost no myth. Comus has almost no myths of his own in antiquity. His fame rests mainly on artistic descriptions and his role in the procession of Dionysus.
A name for a folk band. The British progressive folk group Comus took its name from this god for its 1971 album First Utterance.
