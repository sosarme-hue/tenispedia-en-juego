Greek muse of lyric and love poetry

Erato

Erato is, in Greek mythology, the Muse of choral and love lyric poetry. Daughter of Zeus and Mnemosyne, her name is linked to eros and to an adjective meaning 'beloved', and the stories connect her with desire turned into loyal affection.

The myth

According to Hesiod's Theogony, Erato is a daughter of Zeus and Mnemosyne and one of nine sisters, the Muses. Tradition presents her as the Muse of lyric and love poetry. Apollonius of Rhodes playfully suggested that her name shares a root with Eros, and he opened Book III of his Argonautica by invoking her to tell the passion between Medea and Jason. She is credited with inventing hymns and dance, playing the lyre and teaching geometry. Her role is to refine desire, stripping it of its uncontrolled and abusive excesses and turning it into affection and fidelity.

Genealogies vary between authors. In one version, Zeus gave Erato as a bride to Malus, eponym of Malea, and their daughter Cleophema bore Aegle, identified with Coronis, to Phlegyas. Another tradition makes her the mother of Tamiris by Aethlius. Apollo is also said to have fathered Linus, Hymenaeus and Ialemus by different Muses, though the identities of those mothers have not survived. She should not be confused with the Nereid of the same name.

A lost poem, Rhadine, began with an invocation to Erato, and the love story of its heroine made her supposed tomb on the island of Samos a pilgrimage site for star-crossed lovers, according to Pausanias. Virgil also calls on her at the start of Book VII of the Aeneid.

Legacy

Since the Renaissance she has usually been shown wearing a wreath of myrtle and roses and holding a lyre or a small kithara, an instrument associated with Apollo. In Simon Vouet's paintings, two turtle-doves peck seeds at her feet. Other images give her a golden arrow, a reminder of the eros she inspires, and sometimes Eros accompanies her, holding a bow and a lit torch. Plato links her again with love in the Phaedrus.

Ancient sources: Hesiod's Theogony, Apollonius of Rhodes' Argonautica, Virgil's Aeneid and, indirectly, works by Strabo and Pausanias.

Trivia

A pilgrimage tomb. According to Pausanias, the supposed tomb of Rhadine on Samos drew star-crossed lovers. The lost poem telling her story opened with an invocation to Erato.
An ancient wordplay. Apollonius of Rhodes playfully suggested that Erato's name comes from the same root as Eros, the word for love.
