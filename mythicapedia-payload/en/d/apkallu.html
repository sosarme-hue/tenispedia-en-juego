seven semi-divine sages of Mesopotamian mythology

Apkallu

The Apkallu (Sumerian Abgal) are seven semi-divine sages of ancient Mesopotamia, often depicted as fish-men, credited with passing on the knowledge that made civilization possible. They matter because they link the world of the gods with that of humans, and because their story shifts from benevolent teachers to ambiguous, punished figures.

The myth

According to tradition, the Apkallu were created by Ea (Enki in Sumerian), the god of wisdom, waters and creation, and dwelt in the Apsu, his abode beneath the sea. They had the form of fish-men, and some texts link the first of them, Uanna or Oannes, with teaching humanity the arts, writing and the foundations of Babylonian civilization.

The texts list seven sages in order: Uanna, Uannedugga, Enmedugga, Enmegalamma, Enmebulugga, An-Enlilda and Utuabzu, each with a title hinting at origin or fate. According to the royal lists, each sage advised a primeval king, and after the Flood further sages appear, now human and called Ummanu. The pre-flood Apkallu were thus the teachers who guided humanity before the catastrophe.

The Apkallu also appear in the myth of Adapa, a sage who visited heaven, and some scholars identify Utuabzu with him. In later texts, however, some Apkallu become transgressors: they taught forbidden knowledge and were confined to the underworld, a story reminiscent of the Watchers in the Book of Enoch.

Worship and religion

The Apkallu were represented by figurines used in apotropaic rituals, placed at doorways and walls of houses and temples to ward off evil. Sources also connect them with the founding of cities such as Eridu, Ur, Nippur and Uruk, and with the care of Marduk's statue.

Legacy

Berossus, a Babylonian priest of the 3rd century BC, described Oannes as a half-man, half-fish being who taught civilization to humans. His figure has shaped the imagery of fish-men and the idea of fallen civilizing sages.

Ancient sources: Cuneiform king and sage lists, the Uruk List of Kings and Sages, the Bit Meseri ritual, the Poem of Erra and the work of Berossus.

Trivia

Protective figurines. Some Apkallu figurines have bird heads or other forms, not only fish shapes, and were used in protective rituals.
Oannes in Berossus. Berossus credits Oannes with teaching humanity the Babylonian creation myth, the Enūma Eliš.
