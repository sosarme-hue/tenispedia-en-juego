Egyptian-Hellenistic god of silence and the rising sun

Harpocrates

Harpocrates is the Greek form of Horus the Child, the Egyptian god of the newborn sun, adopted by the Hellenistic religion of Alexandria. Because he sucks his finger in a gesture that Greeks and Romans misread as a call for silence, he came to be seen as the god of secrets and discretion.

The myth

In the Osirian myth, the child Horus waits while his mother, Isis, searches for Osiris, who had been murdered by Set and thrown into the river. Harpocrates stays at Buto, in the care of a local deity, as the weak sun of dawn or winter, naked and unprotected. Over time he grows into the warrior Horus who defeats Set and avenges his father.

According to Plutarch, he was born to Isis and Osiris after his father's death, prematurely and with weak legs. For Plutarch, the finger at the lips does not mean silence. Instead, the god corrects the flawed opinions people hold about the gods.

Worship and religion

His worship appears already in the New Kingdom and spread in the Greco-Roman period, with shrines at Coptos and El Mahamud, and at Edfu as a son of Hathor. From the Late Period, cippi amulets showing Horus on the crocodiles were made and became popular as protection against dangerous animals. Among the Greeks he was identified with Eros, and small terracotta figures spread through the Roman Empire for household shrines.

Legacy

The rose became linked to his name through a story in which Aphrodite gives a rose to Eros, who passes it to Harpocrates so that he will keep his mother's secrets. This gave rise to the phrase sub rosa, "under the rose", meaning confidentiality. Modern occultists have adopted him, and Discordians invoke him as a god of humour and mischief, identified with Harpo Marx.

Ancient sources: The Pyramid Texts, the Osirian myth, Plutarch, and Ovid, along with later Greek and Roman poets.

In art

Trivia

Bone figurine in Crimea. In 2018, archaeologists found at the ancient Greek city of Tiritaka, in Crimea, a bone figurine of the god dating to the 1st century BC. It also shows two satyrs and a goose.
Roses and confidentiality. For centuries, roses were painted or placed on ceilings of meeting rooms and over confessionals to signal that what was said there must stay secret.
