Aegisthus

Aegisthus is a figure of Greek mythology, son of Thyestes and his own daughter Pelopia. He is best known as Clytemnestra's lover and as her partner in the murder of Agamemnon.

The myth

According to the tradition, Aegisthus was born from the incestuous union of Thyestes and his daughter Pelopia. Thyestes sought revenge on his brother Atreus, who had served him his own sons at a meal. An oracle advised him to father a son with his daughter, who would kill Atreus. Pelopia, unaware of her attacker's identity, took his sword during the rape. Ashamed of the child, she abandoned him, and Aegisthus was raised by shepherds; in one version he was suckled by a goat, which is the origin of his name, linked to the male goat.

Atreus took him in and raised him as a son. When he sent Aegisthus to kill Thyestes, the sword the young man carried led to the recognition between father and son. Pelopia took her own life on learning the truth, and Aegisthus killed Atreus while he was making a sacrifice by the sea. Thyestes and Aegisthus ruled Mycenae together until Agamemnon, Atreus's son, drove them out with Spartan help.

While Agamemnon fought at Troy, Aegisthus seduced Clytemnestra. When Agamemnon returned, the couple murdered him, at a banquet according to Homer or with Clytemnestra as the executioner in other accounts. Aegisthus ruled for seven more years until Orestes, Agamemnon's son, killed him to avenge his father. In Aeschylus he is a weak figure who plots the crimes but leaves the deeds to his lover.

Legacy

In Aeschylus's work Aegisthus appears as a weak, almost feminised figure, an image that shaped later writers such as Seneca, who shows him urging Clytemnestra to murder. In Richard Strauss and Hugo von Hofmannsthal's opera Elektra he is a high-pitched tenor with effeminate gestures. A Mycenaean tomb from around 1510 BC is fancifully called the Tomb of Aegisthus.

Ancient sources: He appears in Homer's Odyssey, Aeschylus's Oresteia and Euripides's Electra, though in the latter he remains offstage.
