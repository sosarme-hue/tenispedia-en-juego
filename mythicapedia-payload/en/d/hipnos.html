Greek god of sleep

Hypnos

Hypnos is the Greek god of sleep, the personification of falling asleep. Born of Nyx, the Night, he lived alongside his twin brother Thanatos, Death, and his name gave rise to the English word "hypnosis".

The myth

In Hesiod's Theogony, Hypnos was born of Nyx without a father. In later Roman accounts he is the son of Erebus and Nox. He lived with Thanatos in the underworld, where the sun's rays never reached, in a cave near the river Lethe, surrounded by poppies and soporific plants. In Homer he is linked with the island of Lemnos, and he was described as a calm, gentle god who aided people in need.

In the Iliad, Hera persuaded Hypnos to put Zeus to sleep in exchange for a promise of marriage to Pasithea, one of the Charites. Hypnos hesitated, since the last time he had lulled Zeus, the god had raged on waking and tried to throw him from Olympus; only his mother Nyx saved him. This time Hypnos sent Zeus into slumber as the god embraced Hera, then told Poseidon to aid the Achaeans. Zeus never learned of the deception.

Other tellings say Medea called on Hypnos to lull the dragon of Colchis to sleep, and that Hypnos's children Morpheus, Phantasos and Icelus filled the dreams of kings and ordinary people.

Legacy

The name of Hypnos gave rise to the word "hypnosis", and from it come "hypnotic" and "hypnotize". In medicine, hypnotics are drugs that induce sleep, and they take their name from him. Ancient artists often depicted him on vases and in statues, usually as a winged youth pouring Lethean water over a sleeping figure.

Ancient sources: Hesiod names him in the Theogony, and Homer's Iliad includes him in the deception of Zeus. Ovid mentions his children Morpheus, Phobetor and Phantasos.

Trivia

A god of Rome and Greece. The Romans called him Somnus, and bronze and marble statues of the god survive in Rome, such as the head found at Hadrian's Villa.
Friend of the Muses. According to Pausanias, Hypnos was the dearest friend of the Muses.
