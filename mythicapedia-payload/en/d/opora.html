Greek goddess of harvest and autumn

Opora

Opora is a minor goddess of Greek mythology linked to fruit, the harvest, especially the grape harvest, and the end of summer and autumn. She is little known and barely appears in stories.

The myth

According to the myth, Opora had a romance with Sirius, the Dog Star. While she was on a mission on earth, back when the stars still visited it, Sirius fell in love with her. Unable to be together, he burned with ever greater heat, and suffering humans appealed to the gods for help.

Boreas, the north wind, sent his sons to hand Opora over to Sirius, while he cooled the heat with cold gusts. Since then Sirius has blazed each summer at harvest time, which explains the heat of the so-called dog days.

Legacy

She appears in Aristophanes' comedy Peace as one of Irene's companions. The later Greek word for autumn, phthinópōron, meaning 'end of the opora', derives from her name. She also appears in an ancient mosaic found at Daphne and on Athenian vases.

Ancient sources: Homer calls Sirius 'the star of the opora'. The myth is known mainly from ancient commentaries on lost works by Amphis and Alexis, and Opora appears in Aristophanes' Peace.

Trivia

A star with a season's name. Homer calls Sirius 'the star of the opora', although its rising does not coincide exactly with the season.
A Roman parallel. Pomona is the closest parallel in Roman mythology, though not an exact equivalent.
A proposed marriage in comedy. In Peace, Hermes suggests that Trygaeus marry Opora.
