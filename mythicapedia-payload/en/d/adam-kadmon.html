primordial man in Kabbalah

Adam Kadmon

Adam Kadmon, the «primordial man» of Kabbalah, is the first spiritual world that came into being after the contraction of God's infinite light. It differs from the physical Adam and synthesizes the Tree of Life, which makes it a key concept in Kabbalistic cosmology.

The myth

According to Kabbalistic tradition, before creation there was only God's infinite light, called Ein Sof. To make room for the world, God contracted in a process known as tzimtzum, and a ray of light penetrated the resulting void. Within it appeared the figure of Adam Kadmon, first in the form of ten concentric circles and then in an anthropomorphic shape: divine light without vessels, that is, pure potential. His name expresses that he contains the ultimate purpose of creation, humanity, as well as the totality of the sefirot.

Ancient sources connect this figure with debates about the first human being. Philo of Alexandria spoke of a heavenly man, the perfect image of the Logos, who was neither man nor woman, and he merged this with Plato's theory of forms and with Midrash. The Pharisees taught that Adam was created as an androgynous being and that the sexes were later separated. In the Zohar, the heavenly man embodies all divine manifestations, and the earthly Adam was formed in his image.

In Lurianic Kabbalah, Adam Kadmon is no longer merely the concentrated manifestation of the sefirot but a mediator between the Ein Sof and them. After his emergence came the shattering of the sefirot, known as shevirah.

Legacy

The doctrine of Adam Kadmon became a central element of Lurianic Kabbalah, spread by Ḥayyim Vital in his treatise on circles and the straight line. In Gnosticism, the primordial Anthrôpos also holds a prominent place in several systems.

Ancient sources: Philo of Alexandria, the Midrash (Genesis Rabbah) and the Zohar, among other Jewish and Gnostic texts.

Trivia

The letter yud. Each of the Four Worlds corresponds to one letter of God's four-lettered divine name. Adam Kadmon is represented by the transcendental tip of the first letter, yud.
An androgynous being. According to the Pharisees cited in the Midrash, Adam was created as a being both male and female, and the separation of the sexes came later.
