Finnish god of the forest and hunting

Tapio

Tapio is the god, or spirit, of the forest in Finnish mythology, lord of the hunt and husband of Mielikki. Linked to the Green Man archetype, he survives in folklore, in place names and in modern music.

The myth

According to tradition, Tapio is married to Mielikki, the forest goddess, and is the father of Tellervo, Tuulikki and Nyyrikki, the god of hunting. Runic songs describe him with a beard of lichen and eyebrows of moss. His name often stands for 'forest' itself, and epithets such as 'King of the Forest' or 'grey-beard' are also applied to the bear. He is a prominent figure in the Kalevala.

Scholars have proposed very different origins for his name, from Ares or Saint Stephen to Finnic words for a trap lock or for luck. Krohn thought he was a personification of the forest, formed after the original meaning of the word was forgotten.

Worship and religion

Hunters prayed to him before setting out. Worship took place at a spruce with its top removed, called Tapio's table, where special beer was brewed and hunting gear was placed at its root. The worshipper circled the tree wearing a hat, lay face down and sang a runic prayer. In some regions silver, strong liquor, roosters and the first portions of cheese or meat were also offered. The sources do not say whether this worship survives.

Legacy

Tapiola, one of the main urban centres of Espoo near Helsinki, takes its name from him, as does a community in Michigan. Jean Sibelius wrote the tone poem Tapiola (1926) to depict the forest he inhabits. Finnish metal bands, including Nightwish and Korpiklaani, also mention him in their songs.

Ancient sources: Mikael Agricola names him in the prologue to his Finnish translation of the Psalms, and he appears in runic songs and in the Kalevala.
