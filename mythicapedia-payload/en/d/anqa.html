legendary bird of pre-Islamic Arabian mythology

Anqa

The Anqa is a golden, mysterious female bird of pre-Islamic Arab mythology, described with a long white neck, a human face and four pairs of wings. It was often merged with the phoenix, the griffin and the Persian simurgh, which shows how images of a marvellous bird travelled between cultures.

The myth

According to tradition, the Anqa is a strange bird that flies far away and appears only once in many ages, although it was also said that it could be found where the sun sets. It was described as very beautiful and colourful, with a distinctive whiteness on its neck and traits resembling every living creature.

Its name is tied to the idea of a long neck, and some link it to herons or cranes, while others connect it to the strength of an eagle's or falcon's neck. An echo of its name also suggests calamity: in some accounts the bird was created perfect but became a plague and was killed.

One of the earliest accounts appears in the ninth-century Dala'il al-Nubwah, where King Solomon debates fate with the birds and the Anqa rejects the idea of predestination. Zakariya al-Qazwini, in Aja'ib al-Makhluqat, describes it as a wise bird living alone on Mount Qaf, which lives 1700 years, mates at 500, and whose chicks remain in the egg for 125 years. It was said to eat only elephants and large fish.

Legacy

The Anqa was frequently identified with the Persian simurgh and the phoenix, to the point of becoming synonymous with them. In Turkish, the name of the Konrul and that of the phoenix are joined in the phrase "the emerald Anqa". In Modern Standard Arabic it is rendered as phoenix or griffin.

Ancient sources: The ninth-century Dala'il al-Nubwah and al-Qazwini's cosmological work Aja'ib al-Makhluqat.

Trivia

A bird of many names. It is also known as Anqa al-Mughrib, where mughrib suggests the strange, the distant and the west.
An unusual diet. According to al-Qazwini, it ate nothing but elephants and large fish.
