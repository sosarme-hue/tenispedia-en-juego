Roman god of animal mating and fertility

Inuus

Inuus was a god, or an aspect of a god, in Roman religion linked to sexual intercourse and animal fertility. Evidence for him as a separate figure is scant, and he is often merged with Faunus.

The myth

Texts give him little narrative of his own. Servius treats him as an epithet of Faunus, named for his habit of mating with animals, and links the name to inire, 'to enter', in its sexual sense. Livy identifies him as the form of Faunus honoured at the Lupercalia. Ovid never names him but may allude to him when explaining the festival: Juno, as the birth goddess Lucina, tells Romulus that a sacred goat should enter the Italian matrons, and an augur suggests whipping the women with strips of goat hide.

Worship and religion

He is tied to the Lupercalia, celebrated in honour of Faunus, with young men running naked and rites aimed at fertility. No living cult survives.

Legacy

Charles Darwin used the name Inuus ecaudatus for the Barbary macaque, now Macaca sylvanus. An Etruscan bronze mirror from Chiusi, the Casuccini mirror, may depict him.

Ancient sources: Livy links him to the Lupercalia; Servius comments on the Aeneid and mentions Castrum Inui; Rutilius Namatianus describes a statue of him there.

Trivia

A play on inire. Rutilius Namatianus puns on the phrase Faunus init, 'Faunus enters', when describing the statue at the gate of Castrum Inui.
A bronze mirror. On the Casuccini mirror a bearded figure may be Inuus, though the exact meaning of the scene remains unresolved.
