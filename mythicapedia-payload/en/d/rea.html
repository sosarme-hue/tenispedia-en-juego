Titan goddess, mother of the Olympian gods

Rhea

Rhea is one of the Titans of Greek mythology, daughter of Uranus and Gaia, and both sister and wife of Cronus. She is the mother of Hestia, Demeter, Hera, Hades, Poseidon and Zeus, and is regarded as a mother goddess linked to protective motherhood and fertility.

The myth

According to Hesiod's Theogony, Rhea is one of the Titans, the children of Uranus and Gaia. After Cronus overthrew his father, he imprisoned his brothers the Cyclopes and Hecatoncheires again and took Rhea as his wife. She bore Hestia, Demeter, Hera, Hades and Poseidon, but Cronus swallowed each one at birth, because Gaia and Uranus had warned him that one of his children would overthrow him.

When Zeus was born, Rhea turned to her parents for help. Gaia and Uranus sent her to Crete, where she gave birth in a cave and left the infant in the care of Gaia, the nymphs Adrastea and Ida, and the Curetes, who clashed their shields so that Cronus would not hear the crying. She then wrapped a stone in swaddling clothes and gave it to Cronus, who swallowed it believing it was his last child. According to the Bibliotheca, Zeus was nourished with the milk of the goat Amalthea. When he grew up, Rhea and Metis gave Cronus an emetic that made him vomit up his children, who emerged alive.

There are variants about some of her children. In one version, Poseidon was not swallowed: Rhea entrusted him to the Telchines on Rhodes. In an Arcadian legend, she left him among a flock and told Cronus she had given birth to a colt, which he swallowed instead. In a later version, Cronus punished only the sons, and it is Hera who saves Zeus by taking him to Crete.

Worship and religion

Rhea's worship was not widespread, and few temples are known to have been dedicated to her. Its main centre was Crete, in the caves of Mount Ida and Mount Dicte, places linked to the birth of Zeus. She was venerated with the epithets Mḗtēr Theṓn ("Mother of the Gods") and Mḗtēr Megálē ("Great Mother"). From the classical period onwards, and especially in Roman times, her figure merged with that of Cybele, the mother goddess of Anatolia, and in Rome she was identified with Magna Mater and with Ops.

Legacy

The fusion with Cybele left its mark on iconography. Rhea is shown riding a chariot drawn by two lions, the typical image of Cybele, so the two goddesses are often hard to tell apart in art. Her name has also fuelled linguistic debate: it has been linked to Greek words such as rhéo ("to flow") or éra ("earth"), and some scholars propose a pre-Greek or Mycenaean origin.

Ancient sources: Hesiod's Theogony is the main source for her story; Homer calls her mother of the gods, and Plato and the Bibliotheca offer variants about her parentage and children.

Trivia

The milk that formed a galaxy. In one version, Rhea had Cronus suckle the stone disguised as a child. The milk that spilled as she pressed her breast formed the Milky Way.
A colt instead of a child. In an Arcadian legend, Rhea left Poseidon among a flock and gave Cronus a colt to eat in his place.
