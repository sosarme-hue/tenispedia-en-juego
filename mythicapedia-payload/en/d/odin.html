principal god of Norse mythology

Odin

Odin is the chief god of the Norse pantheon, lord of wisdom, war, death and poetry. Known under related names across the Germanic peoples, he is one of the most influential figures of Germanic tradition and has left a lasting mark on the calendar and on popular culture.

The myth

According to the Norse accounts, Odin was the son of the æsir Borr and the giantess Bestla, brother of Vili and Vé, and husband of Frigg, with whom he fathered Baldr. He had many other sons, among them Thor, whom some sources say he had with the earth goddess Jörð. He dwells in Asgard, in the hall of Valaskjálf, where his throne Hliðskjálf allowed him to look over the nine worlds. He wields the spear Gungnir and rides Sleipnir, an eight-legged horse. Two ravens, Huginn and Muninn, representing thought and memory, perch on his shoulders, and the wolves Geri and Freki accompany him.

To gain wisdom, the tradition says he gave one eye in exchange for a drink from the well of Mimir. He also hung on the world tree Yggdrasil for nine days and nights, and so learned the runes, which by some accounts he kept to himself, though other sources say he shared them with humans. He is an ambivalent god, linked to poetry and inspiration but also to fury, deceit and the magic of seiðr. He was said to travel disguised as an old, one-eyed man with a grey beard and a wide-brimmed hat.

As god of war, he sends the valkyries to gather fallen warriors, the einherjar, who dwell in Valhalla and prepare for Ragnarök. In that final battle Odin is devoured by the wolf Fenrir, and his son Vidar avenges him by killing the beast. Many tales also show him as a wise and just god, and as a contestant in contests of knowledge against giants such as Vafþrúðnir, or in the riddle contest with King Heiðrekr, from which he escapes in the shape of a hawk.

Worship and religion

Accounts by early travellers to Northern Europe describe human sacrifices made to Odin. In the Viking Age he was honoured as lord of war and victory, and he was associated with kingship. He is still venerated in modern Heathenry, where in some traditions he is the central deity.

Legacy

His name survives in Wednesday in English and in onsdag and óðinsdagr in the Scandinavian languages, while Latin-derived languages preserve the link with Mercury. Richard Wagner used the name Wotan in his Ring cycle. He appears constantly in fantasy literature, film and video games.

Ancient sources: The Poetic Edda and the Prose Edda of Snorri Sturluson, along with the Ynglinga saga, are the main sources on Odin; skaldic poets referred to him with countless kennings, such as Allföðr, 'father of all'.

Trivia

Day of the week. The name of Odin lies behind Wednesday in English, meaning 'day of Wōden'.
An eye for wisdom. According to myth, Odin gave one of his eyes in exchange for wisdom at the well of Mimir.
