supposed Slavic deity born from a misreading

Uslad

Uslad is a supposed Slavic deity who, according to scholars, never existed as such. The name came from a misreading of the phrase «усъ златъ» («golden moustache») in the Primary Chronicle, and it matters because it shows how an error and modern invention shaped the image of Slavic paganism.

The myth

For centuries it was repeated that Uslad was a god worshipped alongside the idols Vladimir of Kiev had erected. Sigismund von Herberstein read «us zlat» as an independent name in the description of the statue of Perun, and from there the reading passed into the chronicle of Polish historian Maciej Stryjkowski (1582) and into the memoir of German traveller Johann David Wunderer, who in 1589-1590 mentioned statues of Khors and Uslad near Pskov. Archaeologist Anatoliy Kirpichnikov later questioned that interpretation.

In the 18th century, several Russian authors embellished the scant knowledge of Slavic paganism with patriotic speculation, a practice historians call «cabinet mythology». A 1804 book presented 59 figures as Slavic gods, Uslad among them, described as the patron of pleasures and amusements who plays the kobza and dances. Its author admitted adding his own inventions and justified the name with a false etymology linked to uslada, «pleasure».

Legacy

The figure was passed from author to author through chronicles and travellers' accounts and, after the 1804 book, was firmly established in the literature of cabinet mythology. Today it is mainly studied as a case of how misreading and fantasy can create gods who were never worshipped.

Ancient sources: The Primary Chronicle contains the phrase «усъ златъ» in its description of Vladimir's idols, but it does not name Uslad as a deity; the name comes from a later misreading.

Trivia

An error copied for centuries. Herberstein's reading was repeated by Stryjkowski in 1582 and later by other authors, without being checked.
59 gods in one book. The 1804 volume presented 59 mythological figures as Slavic gods, although its author admitted that he had invented part of the content.
A traveller in Pskov. Johann David Wunderer travelled in Russia from 1589 to 1590 and described Pskov in his memoir, where he mentioned the statue of Uslad.
