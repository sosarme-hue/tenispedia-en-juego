jötunn of Norse mythology, father of the night

Narfi

Narfi is a jötunn, a giant of Norse mythology, and the father of Nótt, the personification of the night. His name appears in variant forms such as Nörfi, Nörr, or Nari, and scholars connect it to words meaning “narrow” or “oppressive.”

The myth

According to Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda, in the Gylfaginning section, Nótt is the daughter of the jötunn Nörfi or Narfi. Snorri presents this as an alternative form of the giant's name. In the Poetic Edda he appears as Nörr, a choice partly driven by alliteration. The Vafþrúðnismál states that Nótt was born to Naurvi, and the Alvíssmál also names her as Naurvi's daughter. The sources offer no further episodes about the father, so his role in the stories is mainly that of the night's parent.

Some scholars believe Snorri shaped Nótt's genealogy after classical models. On that reading, Narfi would be identified with Erebus, the primordial darkness of Greek myth, whose daughters were the Parcae. The link rests partly on the phrase nipt Nera in the Helgakviða Hundingsbana I, used for a norn who arrives at night.

Legacy

The Swedish Viking metal band Thyrfing refers to “Norve's starfilled sky” in the song “A Great Man's Return,” from the album Valdr Galga. J. R. R. Tolkien used the form Narvi for the dwarf who signed the Doors of Durin in The Lord of the Rings, and in his drafts he spelled the name Narfi, as in the Prose Edda. The TV series Supernatural also featured Narfi in season 13, where he captures and sells the archangel Gabriel to Asmodeus.

Ancient sources: Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda (Gylfaginning), the Poetic Edda (Vafþrúðnismál, Alvíssmál), and Old Norse poems such as those by Egill Skallagrímsson.

Trivia

A name meaning oppression. Scholars link Narfi to the Old Norse adjective nǫrr, meaning “narrow.” This would give the giant a sense of something closed-in and oppressive, fitting for the night.
A Tolkien dwarf. In The Lord of the Rings, Tolkien used the form Narvi for the dwarf who signed the Doors of Durin, though in his drafts he wrote Narfi, as in the Prose Edda.
