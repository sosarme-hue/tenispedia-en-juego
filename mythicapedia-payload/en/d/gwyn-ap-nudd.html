king of the Tylwyth Teg and ruler of the Welsh Otherworld

Gwyn ap Nudd

Gwyn ap Nudd is a figure of Welsh mythology, king of the Tylwyth Teg or "fair folk" and ruler of Annwn, the Otherworld. He is closely linked to the Wild Hunt, which makes him one of the most recognisable figures of Welsh folklore about death and the otherworld.

The myth

According to the tradition, Gwyn is the son of Nudd, probably the same figure known elsewhere as Lludd, and grandson of Beli Mawr. In the Arthurian tale Culhwch and Olwen he abducts Creiddylad, whom the text presents as his lover, and her betrothed Gwythyr ap Greidawl raises an army against him. After the battle Gwyn kills Nwython and forces Nwython's son Cyledr to eat his father's heart, which drives the young man mad. Arthur's intervention agrees that the rivals will fight for the maiden every May Day until Judgement Day.

In the poem The Dialogue of Gwyn ap Nudd and Gwyddno Garanhir, preserved in the Black Book of Carmarthen, he appears as a psychopomp who gathers the souls of fallen British warriors. In later tradition he leads a pack of supernatural hounds, the Cŵn Annwn, that carry off the dead, and hearing their barking was said to foretell a death in the family.

Legacy

Gwyn remains a common Welsh personal name, and his name is also an everyday adjective and noun meaning "white" or "bright". His role as a night hunter has passed into later British literature and folklore, where he is compared with Woden or Herne the Hunter.

Ancient sources: Culhwch and Olwen, The Dialogue of Gwyn ap Nudd and Gwyddno Garanhir, the Black Book of Carmarthen, Preiddeu Annwfn and the poems of Dafydd ap Gwilym.

Trivia

Otherworld hounds. His hounds, the Cŵn Annwn, are described as a supernatural pack. In the Black Book of Carmarthen, Gwyn states that his finest hound is called Dormarch.
Holy water against the fairies. In the Buchedd Collen, Gwyn and his retinue are defeated at Glastonbury Tor by means of holy water.
