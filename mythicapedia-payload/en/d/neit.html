Egyptian goddess of war, hunting and weaving

Neith

Neith (also spelled Neit or Net) is one of the oldest deities in Egypt, associated with war, hunting, weaving and motherhood. Her worship, attested from the predynastic period, made her the main goddess of the city of Sais.

The myth

According to Egyptian stories, Neith is the mother of Ra and Apep in some traditions, while in others she is the mother of Sobek, the crocodile god. Her figure combines very different roles: in the oldest texts she is a warrior who protects the pharaoh and gods such as Osiris and Ra with her arrows, and in other accounts she is a primordial creator who gave birth to the sun.

An inscription from the temple at Esna tells how Neith created the first land from the primordial waters of Nun, bringing the world into being through her thoughts. Tradition also says that she rewove the world on her loom every day. In the New Kingdom she was called the "Mother and Father of all things". When Horus and Set disputed the throne, the gods consulted her, and she advised that Horus should become king. In funerary belief she protected the dead, was patron of weavers and was linked to the linen bandages and shrouds of mummies.

Worship and religion

Her main cult centre was Sais, in the western Nile Delta, whose emblem was two crossed bows. She also had a major sanctuary at Memphis and temples at Esna. She rose in importance in the Twenty-sixth Dynasty, when Sais became Egypt's capital. Herodotus describes the Feast of Lamps, in which participants lit numerous lamps throughout the night. The sources do not say whether her cult survived the end of Egyptian religion.

Legacy

Greek writers identified Neith with Athena, and Plato mentions this association in his dialogue Timaeus. Plutarch records a possible link with the "Veiled Isis", and the philosopher Proclus passed on an inscription linked to her temple at Sais: "I am all that has been, is, and will be. No mortal has ever lifted my veil."

Ancient sources: Inscriptions from the temples at Sais and Esna, and texts by Herodotus, Plato, Plutarch and Proclus, which mention her in Egypt and in Greek contexts.
