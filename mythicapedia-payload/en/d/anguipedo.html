divine figure on Greco-Roman magical amulets

Anguiped

The anguiped is a figure often found on magical amulets from the Greco-Roman period, with a rooster's head and serpent legs, and it is frequently identified with Abraxas. Its presence on gems, papyri and curse tablets reflects the blend of Jewish and pagan beliefs of the era.

The myth

The sources do not preserve a single narrative about this figure, but rather an image repeated on protective objects. Its symbolism is thought to be of Persian origin, though its earliest roots are remote in both time and place.

In Graeco-Roman art, the same serpent-legged body is also given to Typhon and the giants, who are often shown this way from around 380 BCE. In Roman Germany and eastern Gaul, a common scene shows an equestrian Jupiter riding down an anguipedal giant.

Worship and religion

No organised cult is documented, but the inscription of the name Iao, a form of the Tetragrammaton, beside the figure on amulets, magical papyri and curse tablets points to syncretic cults that mixed Judaism with paganism. The Talmud refers to those who followed such cults as minim.

Trivia

Typhon and the giants. In Graeco-Roman art, Typhon and the giants are often depicted with serpent legs, just like the anguiped.
Jupiter on horseback. A common religious motif in Roman Germany and eastern Gaul shows an equestrian Jupiter riding down an anguipedal giant.
