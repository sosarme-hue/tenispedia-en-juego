Egyptian god of the Western Desert

Ash

Ash, also spelled Sha depending on the period, is an Egyptian god linked to the Western Desert and its oases, and a protector of the Libyan peoples. He matters chiefly for his close association with Set, with whom he came to share iconography and identity.

The myth

Some sources describe him as an Amazigh sky god, also called Yuc, worshipped by the Libyan and Tehenu tribes west of the Nile. The Egyptians called him the "Lord of the Tehenu" and regarded him as lord of the oases and the western deserts.

He is usually shown in human form, but his head could be that of a lion, vulture, hawk, snake or the unidentified Set animal. Some images show him with several heads, which is unusual among Egyptian deities. From at least the Second Dynasty he borrowed Set's iconography, and he is sometimes shown even with Set's head. This is thought to be because Ash originated in Ombos, in northern Egypt, where Set became the main god and was woven into Ash's myth. Some scholars believe Ash was the god of Nebut, now Naqada, before Set arrived there, which would explain his title "Nebuty", "He of Nebut". Whether he was an imported or a local deity remains debated.

Worship and religion

He was regarded as the god of the vineyards of the western Nile Delta, and so was seen as a benign deity. Old Kingdom wine jar seals bear the inscription "I am refreshed by this Ash", which suggests a devotion linked to wine.

Ancient sources: He is mentioned in Formula XCV of the Book of the Dead and in Old Kingdom wine jar inscriptions, and is still cited as late as the 26th Dynasty.

Trivia

Wine jar seals. Old Kingdom wine jars carried seals reading "I am refreshed by this Ash", one of the most common mentions of the god.
Multiple heads. Some depictions of Ash show several heads, which is unusual among Egyptian gods.
