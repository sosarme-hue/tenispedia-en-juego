Aztec goddess of hearth fires and volcanoes

Chantico

Chantico is a goddess of Aztec religion, consort of Xiuhtecutli, the god of fire. She presided over the fires of the heart, the hearth, volcanoes and kitchen stoves, and was also linked to warfare and to stonecutters.

The myth

According to the tradition preserved in the codices and in the accounts of Sahagún's informants, Chantico was punished by Tonacatecuhtli for breaking a fast. During a religious celebration she ate roasted fish with chili, and the god turned her into a dog. This is why she received the calendar name Chicunaui itzcuintli, or "Nine Dog."

The story adds that those born on the ninth day of the eighteenth trecena, which fell under her rule, would suffer misfortune, since that day was linked to sorcerers who could shape-shift into animals.

Worship and religion

Chantico was worshipped in a temple called the tetlanman, where priests prepared pigments, sandals, a robe and small sea snails for her feast. Sahagún also records a shrine for her in a building of the Templo Mayor, and she was honored on the summit of Tepeyac on March 23. Her cult does not survive today.

Legacy

Her name is used for the colossal head found at the Templo Mayor, known as the Coyolxauhqui-Chantico monument, a name proposed by Mesoamerican scholar Hermann Mayer. H. B. Nicholson argues that Chantico is a manifestation of Coyolxauhqui, although colonial sources do not explicitly confirm this.

Ancient sources: The Codex Ríos (Codex Vaticanus A) and the informants of Bernardino de Sahagún mention her in their accounts of Mexica deities.

Trivia

Colossal head monument. The name Coyolxauhqui-Chantico is given to a huge head found at the Templo Mayor, linking the goddess to that sculpture.
Many names. She was also known as Cuauhxólotl, "Yellow Woman" or "Lady of the Chili," depending on the source.
