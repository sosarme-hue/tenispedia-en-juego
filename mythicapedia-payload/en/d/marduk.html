chief god of the Babylonians

Marduk

Marduk was the patron god of Babylon and, over time, the leading deity of the Mesopotamian pantheon. His prominence grew during the reign of Hammurabi and he reached his highest status in the second millennium BC, when he was regarded as lord of the universe.

The myth

According to Babylonian tradition, Marduk was the firstborn son of Ea, god of the deep waters and wisdom, and of Damkina. His consort was Sarpanitu, and his son Nabu was the god of writing. The stories give him authority over humankind and the lands, and the Code of Hammurabi presents him as a divine authority above the king.

The best-known episode is told in the Enuma Elish. There Marduk confronts the chaos goddess Tiamat and defeats her, then shapes heaven and earth from her body. After his victory the assembled gods grant him some fifty names and titles that celebrate his power over the pantheon. His symbols included the spade and the mušḫuššu dragon.

His name has several possible readings. The most widely accepted links it to the sun god Utu of the city of Sippar, which may mean something like "son of the Sun", although no family tie to the sun god is recorded.

Worship and religion

Marduk was chiefly worshipped in Babylon, in the Esagila temple, which was also dedicated to his consort Sarpanitu. Beside it stood the ziggurat Etemenanki. During the New Year festival, known as akitu, his role was renewed together with the power of the city and its king.

Legacy

During the first millennium BC Marduk became astrologically associated with the planet Jupiter, and in some texts he is equated with Baal. His name appears in the history of Babylonian astronomy and astrology.

Ancient sources: The Babylonian poem Enuma Elish, which narrates his victory over Tiamat, and the Code of Hammurabi, where he appears as a protecting deity of the kingdom.
