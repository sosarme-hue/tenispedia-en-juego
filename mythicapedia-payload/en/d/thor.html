Norse god of thunder and strength

Thor

Thor is the god of thunder, strength and protection in Norse and Germanic mythology, famous for his hammer Mjölnir. He was widely venerated in the Viking Age, and his name survives in Thursday and in many place names.

The myth

According to medieval Icelandic sources, Thor is the son of Odin and Jörð, the earth, and so he has numerous brothers. He is married to the golden-haired Sif, with whom he has a son, Móði. Other sources also give him children from other unions, such as Þrúðr and Magni, the latter born to the giantess Járnsaxa.

The texts give him at least fifteen names and three dwellings. He rides a chariot pulled by two goats, which he can eat and then revive the next morning. To fight giants and monsters he carries the hammer Mjölnir, and he wears a belt that doubles his strength and iron gloves. His exploits include constant battles with giants and a fight with the great serpent of Midgard, with which, the tradition says, he will die at Ragnarök.

The Prose Edda and the poems of the Poetic Edda tell episodes in which his strength, appetite and impulsive temper make for humorous stories, though he always defends the order of the world against outside threats.

Worship and religion

The Germanic peoples venerated Thor in sacred groves, especially among oaks, trees dedicated to him. Tacitus, in the Germania, identifies him with Hercules, a god honoured by the Germanic tribes before battle. Among the Batavians, Donar was the main deity, with open-air sanctuaries at modern Nijmegen and at Elst, and dedications inscribed to Hercules Magusanus have been found.

In the Viking Age his hammer was worn as an amulet, and personal names containing his name multiplied, partly as a response to Christianization. After the conversion his figure was demonized, although his beliefs survived in rural folklore. Today his worship has been revived in Germanic neopaganism, within the Heathenry movement.

Legacy

His name gives rise to Thursday in several Germanic languages and survives in many place names across northern Europe. He has inspired works of art and is very present in modern popular culture, with the Marvel Comics character and films such as Thor (2011). His name is also related to the word thunder in English and other European languages.

Ancient sources: The Prose Edda of Snorri Sturluson and poems of the Poetic Edda, such as Hárbarðsljóð, describe him in detail; Tacitus's Germania mentions him under the name Hercules.

Trivia

Day of the week. The English name Thursday means 'day of Thunor', the Old English form of Thor, and derives from a weekday name calqued from Latin 'day of Jove'.
God of the Batavians. Donar was the principal god of the Batavians, and inscriptions to him have been found as far away as Rome.
