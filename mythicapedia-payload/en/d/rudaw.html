lunar god of the North Arabian pantheon

Ruḍaw

Ruḍaw was a god of northern Arabia, worshipped from the early first millennium BCE into Late Antiquity. Modern scholars understand him as a masculine, paternal deity who, in the astral pantheon, was identified with the Moon.

The myth

Few myths of Ruḍaw survive in his own voice. Later Arab tradition preserves a legend recorded by Ibn Isḥāq and Ibn Hishām: a man named Al-Mustawġir, said to have lived 330 years, destroyed his temple, burned it until it was charred black, and composed a poem celebrating the deed.

Historians regard the episode as legendary.

Worship and religion

In oasis communities, Ruḍaw formed an astral triad with Nuhay, the sun, and ʿAttarsamē, Venus. Among nomadic herders he stood at the head of the pantheon as the father of Allāt. One Safaitic inscription petitions him for rain, and another records a sacrifice made to secure a she-camel as war spoil.

Legacy

Several scholars identify him with Arsu, known from Aramaic and Palmyrene sources, and with the Zeus Safatenos named in a dedication from Bosra. His name survives in Arab tribal genealogies, as in ʿAbdu-Ruḍà.

Ancient sources: Dumaitic, Safaitic and Thamudic B inscriptions from North Arabia; from outside his own tradition, the Esarhaddon Prism, Herodotus (Orotalt) and the accounts of Ibn Isḥāq and Ibn Hishām.

Trivia

A god once thought to be female. For much of the twentieth century Ruḍaw was thought to be a female deity. More recent inscriptions revised this view, showing him as masculine.
Possible origin of the name Orotalt. Herodotus mentions a god called Orotalt among nomadic Arabs. Historians think it is a Greek rendering of the name Ruḍaw.
