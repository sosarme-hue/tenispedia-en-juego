Sumerian goddess, lady of the wild cows

Ninsun

Ninsun, also called Ninsuna, was a goddess of ancient Sumer and Babylonia, known as the lady of the wild cows. She is best known as the mother of the hero Gilgamesh and the wife of the deified king Lugalbanda, and she matters mainly for her role in the Epic of Gilgamesh.

The myth

According to the stories, Ninsun lives in Uruk, the city of her son Gilgamesh, and her role changes between versions of the epic. In the Standard Babylonian version she advises her son, interprets his dreams, asks the sun god Shamash to protect him on his journey to fight Humbaba, and welcomes Enkidu into her family. In the Old Babylonian version she barely acts, and a Hittite translation leaves her out altogether.

Older Sumerian texts also feature her, including a badly damaged myth about her first meeting with Lugalbanda and their marriage, in which the Anunna gods seem to take the place of the bride's parents. The god list An = Anum gives her several children with Lugalbanda, and one text names her as the mother of the god Dumuzi, although the usual tradition makes Duttur his mother. Her parentage is described differently: one text calls her the daughter of Anu and Urash, while other scholars consider her parents unknown.

Worship and religion

Her main cult centre was the settlement KI.KALki, and she was associated with Uruk, though she was also worshipped in Nippur and Ur. Gudea of Lagash and the kings of the Third Dynasty of Ur regarded her as their divine mother, and Ur-Nammu and Shulgi left inscriptions showing their personal devotion to her. She was believed to interpret dreams and, in texts from Lagash, to grant long and prosperous life to her devotees.

Legacy

Her role in the Epic of Gilgamesh makes her a key figure in Mesopotamian literature. The kings of the Third Dynasty of Ur used her to legitimize their rule, presenting Gilgamesh as their brother. A prince known from a single inscription bore the name Puzur-Ninsun.

Ancient sources: The Old Babylonian and Standard Babylonian versions of the Epic of Gilgamesh, Sumerian poems such as Gilgamesh and the Bull of Heaven, and the An = Anum god list.

Trivia

A name with two readings. Her name was written dNIN.GUL, and the sign GUL could be read as sun or sumun in Sumerian, both meaning wild cow.
Interpreter of dreams. The so-called Pennsylvania tablet, from the Old Babylonian version of the epic, shows that she was believed capable of interpreting dreams.
