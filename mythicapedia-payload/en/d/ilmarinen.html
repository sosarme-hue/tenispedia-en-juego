smith and sky god of Finnish mythology

Ilmarinen

Ilmarinen is the eternal smith and sky god of Finnish mythology, the archetype of the craftsman able to make almost anything. He matters because he is the great maker in the Kalevala and in the folk runic songs.

The myth

According to the runic songs, Ilmarinen forged the dome of the sky in a forge with no doors or windows, and he was also the first to work iron. His best-known role appears in the Kalevala. After the sorceress Louhi of Pohjola rescues Väinämöinen, he asks Ilmarinen to forge the Sampo, a magical artefact resembling a mill, in exchange for Louhi's daughter as a bride.

Väinämöinen tricks Ilmarinen with praise of the maiden's beauty and challenges him to bring down the moonlight from the top of a spruce. Ilmarinen succeeds, but a gust of wind carries him to Pohjola, where he agrees to forge the Sampo. Over several days the forge produces a crossbow, a ship, a cow and a plough, and Ilmarinen destroys each one because of its harmful nature. In the end, winds fan the flames and the Sampo emerges, producing grain, salt and gold. Louhi hides it underground, and the maiden refuses to leave her homeland, so Ilmarinen returns home alone and dejected.

Worship and religion

In a 1728 testimony, a man accused of witchcraft claimed that Ilmarinen was a god that people had once worshipped. Over time, part of his role passed to the sky god Ukko. There is no record of an organised cult, nor of one surviving today.

Legacy

Elias Lönnrot gathered his story into the Kalevala, which made him a central figure of the forging tales. His name gave rise to Seppo, a Finnish male given name meaning smith.

Ancient sources: The folk runic songs collected in Finland and Estonia, Mikael Agricola's first written mention in 1551, and Christfried Ganander's Mythologia Fennica (1789).

Trivia

The first iron. According to the runic songs, Ilmarinen was the first to forge iron. The metal promised not to harm anyone, but broke its word when a hornet carried snake poison into the quenching water.
A golden maiden. In another song, after several days of work, he forges a golden maiden who makes him happy, but she is cold to the touch. The song ends by warning that men should never make women of gold and silver.
