sculptor and legendary king of Cyprus

Pygmalion

Pygmalion is a sculptor from Cyprus and the protagonist of one of the best-known myths about love for a work of art. He is best known from Ovid's Metamorphoses, where a statue comes to life through the intervention of Venus.

The myth

In Ovid's account, Pygmalion turned away from women after watching the Propoetides, who practised prostitution. He resolved to remain celibate and devote himself to sculpture. He carved a woman out of ivory so perfect that he fell in love with her: he kissed her, gave her gifts, dressed her and prepared a bed for her.

When the festival of Venus arrived, Pygmalion made offerings at the goddess's altar and, too shy to admit his desire, quietly wished for a bride like his ivory girl. Back home, he kissed the statue and found that its lips felt warm. He kissed it again and the ivory lost its hardness, for the goddess had granted his wish. They married, and the statue became a woman. In Ovid's version they had a daughter, Paphos, from whom the city takes its name.

Other accounts differ. According to some, Cinyras founded Paphos and married Metharme, daughter of Pygmalion. No mythographic source reveals who his parents were. There is also a Pygmalion, king of Tyre, son of Muto and brother of Dido, who may be a different figure or a version of the same myth of Semitic origin. The name Galatea appears only in later authors.

Worship and religion

The sources do not describe a cult devoted to Pygmalion himself. His link to religion comes through Venus, at whose altar he makes offerings during her festival.

Legacy

The story has had a long afterlife in European culture. Goethe calls the statue Elise, after the variants of the Dido and Elissa story. Shakespeare includes a lifelike statue in The Winter's Tale, and George Bernard Shaw turns the myth into the play Pygmalion, about a flower seller whom a phonetics professor teaches to speak in polite society. Jean-Philippe Rameau composed the opera Pigmalion in 1748, and the ballet Coppélia echoes the myth. The story inspired paintings by Bronzino, Gérôme, Burne-Jones, Rodin and Goya, along with numerous poems. The same theme appears in the tale of Pinocchio, where the puppet has feelings before becoming a real boy.

Ancient sources: Ovid, Metamorphoses (book X), and briefly the Bibliotheke attributed to Apollodorus.
