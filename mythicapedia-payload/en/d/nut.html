Egyptian goddess of the sky and stars

Nut

Nut is the Egyptian goddess of the sky, the stars and the arching vault of the heavens. Daughter of Shu and Tefnut and wife of her brother Geb, the Earth, she is one of the oldest deities in the Egyptian pantheon and the mother of Osiris, Isis, Seth, Nephthys and Horus the Elder.

The myth

According to the theology of Heliopolis, Nut and Geb were locked in a constant embrace. Images and some texts show Shu, god of the air, pulling them apart and lifting Nut above the Earth. Her arched body, covered in stars, formed the sky, and her fingers and toes were thought to touch the four cardinal points. Nut was also the mother of the Sun: each day she gave birth to it, the sun travelled across her body, and at dusk it was swallowed, passed through her belly during the night and reborn at dawn.

The best-known story concerns the epagomenal days. According to the tradition, the calendar had 360 days and Ra, or Shu in other versions, had forbidden Nut to give birth on any day of the year. Thoth, god of wisdom, played a game with Khonsu, god of the Moon, and won scraps of moonlight from which he made five extra days at the end of the calendar. On them, one each day, Osiris, Isis, Set, Nephthys and Horus the Elder were born. Plutarch, writing in the Graeco-Roman period, changes the characters and identifies Nut with Rhea, so scholars regard his version as later and partly imaginative.

Nut was also a protector of the dead. She sheltered weary souls in the branches of her sycamore at Heliopolis, and on coffins she appeared with outspread wings protecting the deceased, or with raised arms helping them to be reborn. Funerary texts call on her to draw the dead into her starry sky so they may not die.

Worship and religion

Originally from Heliopolis, she had sanctuaries at Memphis and Dendera. She was also shown as a cow, Mehet-Urt, or with a water jar on her head, which was linked to her womb. Egyptians placed the ladder called maqet, associated with her, in tombs to protect the deceased. Her cult does not survive, but in Coptic tradition the sycamore under which the Virgin Mary rested during her journey to Egypt is linked to her sacred tree.

Legacy

Some scholars have suggested that the Egyptians may have seen the Milky Way as a depiction of Nut. Her name, meaning sky, has been transcribed in many ways. Her arched figure covered in stars has become one of the most recognisable images of Egyptian religion and a model of a sky goddess set against the more common pattern of a sky father and an earth mother.

Ancient sources: Egyptian funerary texts that invoke her as protector of the dead, an Egyptian text describing the birth of the gods while the sky was full of deities, and Plutarch's account, from the Greek tradition of the first century CE.

Trivia

The maqet ladder. The Egyptians used a symbol called maqet, a ladder, which according to tradition allowed Osiris to enter Nut's heavenly sky. It was placed in tombs to protect the deceased.
The Milky Way. Some scholars believe the Egyptians may have interpreted the Milky Way as a celestial image of Nut.
