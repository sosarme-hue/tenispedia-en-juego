king of Argos, great-grandfather of Perseus

Abas

In Greek tradition, Abas, also called Abante, was the twelfth king of Argos and the great-grandfather of Perseus. He matters because he links the Argive royal line with the stories of Danaus and Perseus, and because his name gave rise to family names and places in ancient Greece.

The myth

According to myth, Abas was the son of Lynceus and Hypermnestra, the last of the Danaides, and he ruled Argos for twenty-three years. He married Aglaea, daughter of Mantineus, and had twin sons, Acrisius and Proetus, who according to ancient authors quarrelled their whole lives, even in their mother's womb. He also had a daughter, Idomene, and, according to Pausanias, an illegitimate son, Lyrcus, who gave his name to the city of Lyrcea.

As a conqueror, he founded the city of Abae in Phocis and led a colony of Argives to Thessaly, which gave the plain its name Pelasgic Argos. When he brought his father news of Danaus's death, he was rewarded with his grandfather's shield, a marvel that could paralyse enemies, which was hung in the Heraion of Argos. At his death he left the kingdom to Acrisius and Proetus, ordering them to rule in turns, but the brothers rivalled each other from the start.

Legacy

Virgil, in the third book of the Aeneid, imagines Aeneas hanging Abas's shield in the temple of Apollo at Actium, a scene that flattered Augustus, a devotee of Apollo and a descendant of Aeneas. Winners of the Heraean Games later received a replica of that shield as their prize.

Ancient sources: Pausanias, Hyginus and Virgil's Aeneid (book III) preserve different aspects of Abas.

Trivia

Rival brothers. According to tradition, the twins Acrisius and Proetus were already fighting in their mother's womb, an image ancient authors used to stress the family rivalry.
A shield as prize. Winners of the Heraean Games received as their prize a replica of the shield of Danaus that Abas had once received.
