Norse giant and god of fire

Logi

Logi, also called Hálogi, is a jötunn, a giant in Norse mythology, and the personification of fire. Son of Fornjótr and brother of Ægir and Kári, he is linked both to fire and to the legendary origins of Hålogaland, in northern Norway.

The myth

According to Norse tradition, Logi was a son of the giant Fornjótr. While his brothers ruled the sea and the wind, he held power over fire. Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda places him in the tale of Thor and Loki's journey to the castle of the giant Útgarða-Loki in Jötunheimr. There Loki competed with Logi in an eating contest. Both seemed evenly matched, stripping meat from the bone, but Logi also devoured the bones and even the wooden platter the meat rested on. Afterwards, Útgarða-Loki revealed that Logi was really wildfire itself.

Another tradition, preserved in Þorsteins saga Víkingssonar, makes him the first king of Hálogaland, called Hálogi, meaning great Logi, for his size and strength. He married Glöð, a giant's daughter, and they had two beautiful daughters, Eisa and Eimyrja. Two rejected suitors, both jarls, abducted them: Véseti took Eisa, and Vífil took Eimyrja, who bore a son named Víkingr, father of Þorsteinn, the saga's hero.

Legacy

His name is linked to the Old Norse word for fire and, in poetry, to the word for sword. Some have suggested a connection with Loki, though wordplay is more likely. Richard Wagner used the name Loge for a demigod in his Ring of the Nibelung. A moon of Saturn is named Loge after him.

Ancient sources: Snorri Sturluson's Gylfaginning in the Prose Edda, the Flateyjarbók, and Þorsteins saga Víkingssonar.

Trivia

An eating contest. In Snorri's account, Logi ate not only the meat but also the bones and the wooden board it was served on.
A name for a region. According to the saga of Þorsteinn, the region of Hålogaland in northern Norway takes its name from King Hálogi.
