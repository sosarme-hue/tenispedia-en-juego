Myth and religion

The Roman festival of role reversal, gift-giving and winter light

Saturnalia

Saturnalia was one of the most popular celebrations of ancient Rome, dedicated to the god Saturn. For several days in December, everyday rules were suspended, slaves were treated with unusual indulgence and simple gifts were exchanged. What we know about it comes from scattered accounts by ancient authors and from the interpretations made since then.

Saturnalia was one of the best-loved festivals of ancient Rome. It honoured Saturn, and according to the sources its official celebration fell on 17 December, although the public stretched it into a week, lasting until 23 December. The poet Catullus called it "the best of days". Today we know it from fragments scattered across several authors, because no ancient source describes the whole festival from beginning to end.

According to the historical tradition, the festival was reformed in 217 BC, after the Romans suffered a crushing defeat by Carthage at Lake Trasimene during the Second Punic War. Before that, it had been celebrated in the Roman manner. After consulting the Sibylline Books, the Romans adopted the "Greek rite", with sacrifices performed in the Greek style, a public banquet and the shout of "Io, Saturnalia!". Cato the Elder remembered a time before these additions. Over time the celebration spread beyond the official ceremony and became a week of popular festivities.

The Romans linked Saturn with the Greek god Cronus, and myth presented him as the king of a Golden Age, when people lived without forced labour and without divisions between free men and slaves. The festival was meant to recall that lost time. Macrobius, a Latin writer of late antiquity and the main source on the holiday, describes that reign as a time of great happiness, when the division between bond and free did not yet exist. The Greek writer Lucian, in his work Saturnalia, has Cronos himself proclaim a festive season in which drinking is allowed and slaves may revile their lords.

The main rite took place at the temple of Saturn, at the foot of the Capitoline Hill. The god's statue had its feet bound with wool, and the bindings were removed during the festival as a gesture of liberation. The priest officiated with his head uncovered, in the Greek manner, whereas in the Roman rite he covered it. A lectisternium followed, in which the god's image was placed on a sumptuous couch as if he were taking part in the feast, and then a public banquet was held for everyone. Schools closed, courts did not sit and no war could be declared. At home, families bathed early and, if they could afford it, sacrificed a suckling pig.

The most striking feature of the festival was the reversal of social roles. Slaves were served a banquet similar to that of their masters, though the sources disagree on whether they dined together, ate first or were served by their owners. During those days they could speak frankly without fear of punishment. The poet Horace speaks of "December liberty", and in two of his satires set during the festival he has a slave criticise his master. Citizens wore the colourful clothes of dinner parties, which were unusual in daytime, and put on the pileus, the felt cap of freedmen, a cap that slaves wore too. Gambling, normally frowned upon, was allowed for everyone, slaves included.

Among the most curious figures was the "King of the Saturnalia", chosen by lot to preside over the banquet. His whimsical orders, such as "Sing naked!", had to be obeyed. Imperial sources say the future emperor Nero played this role in his youth. Many writers compare him to the medieval Lord of Misrule, although the exact relationship is debated.

Gift-giving centred mostly on 19 December, the day of the sigillaria. People gave candles, small figurines of wax or pottery and joke presents, because valuable objects would mark social rank, which went against the spirit of the season. In his poems, Martial mentions a wide range of gifts, from writing tablets and dice to pets. Some have linked these customs to Christmas, but the historian Peter Gainsford argues there is no direct connection: the dates were different, and many Christmas customs appeared centuries later. What is clear is that the festival survived well into the 3rd and 4th centuries, when the empire was coming under Christian rule.

For ancient thinkers the festival also carried a philosophical meaning. The Neoplatonist Porphyry read its freedom as a symbol of the liberation of souls into immortality. In modern times, the Italian anarchist Luigi Fabbri saw in Saturnalia a reflection of a lasting human longing: to live free and equal, without master or law.

Trivia

A week of festivities. The official celebration lasted one day, but the people stretched it to seven. Augustus and Caligula tried to shorten the holiday, but without success.
Candles in place of human offerings. Macrobius records a legend in which Hercules asked that human heads be replaced by masks. Candles were also offered, since a Greek word meant both "man" and "light".
Gifts of little value. Cheap gifts were considered more fitting for the spirit of the festival, because costly presents would mark social rank. Many were simple figurines or candles.
