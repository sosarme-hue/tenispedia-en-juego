Myth and religion

Looking for family resemblances among the world's myths

Comparative mythology

Comparative mythology sets myths from different cultures side by side to identify shared themes, structures and motifs. Scholars have used this method to trace the development of religions, to propose common origins and to support psychological theories. The field is still shaped by a debate between those who look for similarities and those who stress differences.

Comparative mythology is the systematic study of myths from different cultures, with the aim of spotting shared themes and features. Its practitioners come from folklore, anthropology, history, linguistics and religious studies, and they do not always pursue the same goals. Some want to trace how religions and cultures developed, others propose common origins for stories told thousands of miles apart, and others look for support for psychological theories. In a sense, every theorist of myth compares, since, as the historian of religion Robert Segal puts it, all of them look for similarities among myths.

The anthropologist C. Scott Littleton defined the field as 'the systematic comparison of myths and mythic themes drawn from a wide variety of cultures'. Researchers try to identify underlying similarities and, sometimes, to reconstruct a protomythology from which later traditions might have grown. Within the field, scholars are often divided into particularists, who stress differences and see the resemblances pointed out by comparativists as vague and superficial, and comparativists, who focus on what myths share. Comparative approaches were hugely popular among scholars of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, many of whom believed that all myths had evolved from a single original story. The philologist Max Müller led a school that read almost every myth as a poetic description of the sun's journey across the sky. According to this idea, such descriptions had been distorted over time into apparently unrelated tales of gods and heroes. Today particularism dominates, and scholars are wary of sweeping claims about myth.

One of the most successful cases of this method is Indo-European mythology. The Greek sky god Zeus Pater, the Roman Jupiter and the Vedic Dyaus Pitar have closely related names. This suggests that the Greeks, Romans and Indians descended from a common ancestral culture, and that these names come from an older form, *Dyēus ph2ter, which referred to the sky god, the 'father sky' of the Proto-Indo-European religion. The Germanic god Tiu, whose name survives in English Tuesday, belongs to the same family.

Other scholars focus on the structure of stories rather than their vocabulary. Vladimir Propp noticed that many Russian fairy tales follow a plot in which certain events occur in a predictable order. Claude Lévi-Strauss, by contrast, was interested in the abstract relationships between elements, such as raw versus cooked or nature versus culture. In his view, a myth works to mediate these oppositions and so ease basic tensions in human life. From a psychological angle, some Freudian thinkers have found stories resembling the Greek tale of Oedipus in many different cultures, while Jungians speak of archetypes present in the collective unconscious.

Among the most widespread motifs is the flood. The Babylonian Epic of Gilgamesh and the Hebrew Bible both describe a worldwide flood and a man who saves the earth's species by taking them aboard a boat. Stories with a single survivor also appear in Hindu mythology, where Manu saves the world from the deluge by building a boat, and in Greek accounts such as that of Deucalion, in Norse, Inca and Aztec traditions. These versions share elements such as a divine warning and the building of an ark, though each culture tells them in its own way.

Another recurring motif is creative sacrifice, in which the death of a divine figure gives rise to an essential part of the world. These stories are especially common among farming cultures, particularly those that grow tubers. According to a myth told by the Wemale people of Seram Island in Indonesia, the miraculously conceived girl Hainuwele is murdered, and from her body sprout the community's staple foods. Cosmic giants whose deaths create the world appear in the Chinese Pangu, the Vedic Purusha and the Norse Ymir. The idea of an axis that links heaven, earth and the underworld, often marked by a tree, also recurs: the cosmic tree of Vedic India, ancient China or the Germanic peoples. In Greek tradition the centre of the world is Delphi, where, according to the myth, Zeus released two birds in opposite directions and they met there.

The motif of the dying god who returns to life is especially common in Near Eastern mythologies. The anthropologist James Frazer compared these stories in The Golden Bough. Osiris in Egypt and Tammuz in Mesopotamia are clear examples, and the Greek myth of Adonis, although its hero is mortal, has often been compared with that of Osiris. As early as the first centuries of Christianity, the church father Justin Martyr discussed these parallels with the story of Jesus of Nazareth.

Myths in which a younger group of gods defeats an older one are also common. In Greek mythology the Olympian gods defeat the Titans in the Titanomachy. In India the devas battle the asuras, and the Celtic gods of light fight the Fomori. Some researchers link this motif to the spread of Indo-European peoples, although non-Indo-European cultures also have combat myths, such as the Babylonian Enuma Elish, in which a good god faces a demon of chaos.

Finally, many cultures believe in a supreme being who has withdrawn from humans. Mircea Eliade called this figure a deus otiosus, or 'idle god'. Among the Herero, a sky god has left humanity in the care of lesser deities, and in highly complex cultures the supreme being tends to disappear, replaced by an intense polytheism. Other myths explain that the gods founded a people's customs. According to the Karadjeri of Australia, the brothers Bagadjimbiri established all the customs of their culture, including the way men should squat to urinate.

Trivia

A name that survives. The Germanic name Tiu is related to the Indo-European sky god and survives in the English word Tuesday.
The centre of the world at Delphi. According to Greek tradition, Zeus released two birds in opposite directions and they met at Delphi, which came to be seen as the centre of the world.
Early parallels. In the first centuries of Christianity, Justin Martyr already compared stories of dying and returning gods with the story of Jesus of Nazareth.
