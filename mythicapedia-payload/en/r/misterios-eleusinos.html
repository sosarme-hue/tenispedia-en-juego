The initiation rites of Demeter and Persephone

Eleusinian Mysteries

The Eleusinian Mysteries were annual initiations held at Eleusis, near Athens, for the cult of Demeter and Persephone. They rested on the myth of Persephone's abduction by Hades and her return to the upper world, a cycle that was read as the passage from death to new life in nature. Their secrecy means much of what we know comes from indirect sources.

Eleusis was an agricultural town, known for its wheat and barley, about 30 kilometres northwest of Athens. The site had been inhabited since the Middle Helladic period, and local tradition held that its early inhabitants were of Thracian origin. Over time a very old agrarian cult grew up there, which some scholars believe preserved practices from the Mycenaean era. The rites became one of the most prestigious religious events in ancient Greece and continued into the Roman period.

The myth behind the ceremony tells how Persephone, daughter of Demeter, was seized by Hades, god of the underworld, while she was gathering flowers. Her mother searched for her without rest and, in her grief, neglected the land, so the earth froze and people went hungry. According to the Homeric Hymn to Demeter, Zeus eventually allowed the girl to return. Yet Persephone had eaten pomegranate seeds in the realm of the dead, and whoever eats there cannot stay fully among the living. The compromise divided the year between the two worlds, although accounts disagree on the details: some speak of six seeds and others of four, and the number of months she spends below varies from one telling to another.

The cycle had three phases: descent, search and ascent, known in Greek as anodos. The heart of the celebration was Persephone's return, which coincided with the revival of plant life. For initiates, the young goddess stood for the continuity of life passing from one generation to the next. In another strand of tradition, Triptolemus, a hero of Eleusis, learned farming from Demeter, and he is often shown holding an ear of grain.

The running of the cult mirrored the political life of Athens. The archon basileus held overall authority over the Mysteries, while the epistatai managed the finances. Two families, the Eumolpidae and the Kerykes, controlled the religious roles. The hierophant, or chief priest, belonged to the Eumolpidae, and the torch-bearer and sacred herald belonged to the Kerykes. To take part, one had to be free of blood guilt, meaning never having committed murder, and be able to speak Greek. Men, women and even slaves could be initiated.

There were two grades of Mysteries, the Lesser and the Greater. The Lesser took place at Agrai, beside the river Ilissos, in the month of Anthesterion, and prepared candidates for what followed. They purified themselves and made offerings to Demeter and Persephone. Diodorus Siculus, writing many centuries later, claimed the goddess had founded the Lesser Mysteries in honour of Heracles after he killed the centaurs, but this late explanation cannot be checked.

The Greater Mysteries were held in Boedromion, around September and October, and lasted several days. First, the sacred objects were carried from Eleusis to the Eleusinion, a temple at the foot of the Acropolis in Athens. The hierophant then proclaimed the start of the rites, the prorrhesis. At the Painted Stoa a piglet was sacrificed, and the initiates purified themselves in the sea at Phaleron. From Athens a procession set out for Eleusis, roughly 22 kilometres away, along the Sacred Way. Participants carried branches known as bakchoi and sang a hymn to Iacchus, which may have been an epithet of Dionysus or a separate god. They fasted in memory of Demeter's own fast, and afterwards drank the kykeon, a mixture of water, barley meal and pennyroyal.

On the bridge over the Kephisos, onlookers mocked the procession with satirical songs, recalling Iambe, or Baubo, an old woman who, through crude jokes, made Demeter smile while she grieved. At the Reitoi, a thread was tied to the initiates, who had to speak a password. The contents of the kiste, a large chest, and the kalathos, a small basket, were known only to initiates, and they remain unknown. Some modern writers have offered symbolic readings of the golden objects mentioned in later accounts, but these are interpretations rather than documented facts.

Inside the Telesterion, a great hall, initiates witnessed a dramatic reenactment of the myth and the display of sacred objects. Revealing what happened there was forbidden, on pain of death or exile. Some sources say a single ear of grain was shown in silence beside a great fire, a detail that Hippolytus of Rome records in the third century. The night ended with the pannychis, an all-night feast with dancing, and with libations poured from two vessels, one facing east and the other west.

The testimonies suggest that initiation gave people hope for a better existence after death, and this brought many a sense of calm about mortality. The site's history is long. Around 1500 BC there were Mycenaean structures beneath the Telesterion, and a modest sanctuary is attested in the Geometric period. Solon brought Eleusis under Athenian control, and Peisistratus made the Mysteries pan-Hellenic. The Persians destroyed the sanctuary in 480 BC, and new buildings rose in Roman times. Theodosius I closed the sanctuaries in 392, and in 396 Alaric's Goths plundered what remained.

In the twentieth century, some researchers proposed that the experience was driven by a psychoactive substance. In this hypothesis, ergot, a fungus that can also infect barley, entered the kykeon and contained compounds related to LSD. The idea remains controversial, since tests of kykeon made from infected barley have not been conclusive. Other scholars have suggested mushrooms or fly agaric, without solid evidence. What survives with certainty are the myth, the images on vases and reliefs, and the accounts of those who wrote about the rites.

Trivia

A well-kept secret. Participants swore an oath of silence, and revealing what was seen inside the Telesterion could bring death or exile. That is why much of the ceremony was never written down.
Mockery on the road. On the bridge over the Kephisos, crowds mocked the initiates with satirical songs, recalling Iambe, whose jokes made Demeter smile in her grief.
Two cycles a year. The Lesser Mysteries were held in late winter and the Greater in early autumn. According to some sources, candidates waited nearly a year between the two.
