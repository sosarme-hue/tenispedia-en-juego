Myth and religion

The beliefs of the Scandinavian peoples before Christianity

Old Norse religion

Old Norse religion was the set of beliefs, myths and rituals followed by the peoples of northern Europe before their conversion to Christianity. Scholars reconstruct it from archaeology, historical linguistics, place names and texts written long after its peak. Its myths, featuring gods such as Odin and Thor and the world tree Yggdrasil, remain among the best known mythologies in Europe.

Old Norse religion, also called Norse polytheism or Norse paganism, is a branch of the wider Germanic religion that developed during the Proto-Norse period, when the North Germanic peoples began to split into distinct branches. It was gradually replaced by Christianity as Scandinavia was converted. What we know about it comes from several directions: archaeology, historical linguistics, the study of place names and written records such as the runic inscriptions of the Younger Futhark, the distinctly North Germanic form of the runic alphabet.

The people who practised this religion probably had no word for it. The idea of a "religion" as a separate sphere arrived with Christianity, and the names later used for the older beliefs were usually coined by outsiders. Terms such as hedendom in Scandinavian, Heidentum in German, Heathenry in English or pagan from Latin were labels applied from a competitive, often hostile, position. A more romantic name comes from medieval Icelandic, forn siðr, "old custom", which puts the emphasis on ritual and behaviour rather than on a fixed set of doctrines.

The gods were divided into two groups, the Æsir and the Vanir. According to the myths, the two groups fought a war until they recognised that they were equally powerful, and from that settlement came a shared world. Among the most widespread deities were Odin and Thor. The cosmos was also inhabited by other mythological beings, including dwarfs, elves and land-wights, the spirits linked to the land, who appear in stories alongside gods and humans.

The cosmology revolved around Yggdrasil, a great world tree that linked several realms. One of them was Midgard, where humans lived. The sources describe more than one kind of afterlife, and some of these were governed by a particular deity. That variety suggests there was never a single, tightly defined doctrine about what came after death.

Because the tradition was transmitted orally rather than through codified texts, ritual practice was at its heart. Kings and chiefs played a central part in carrying out public sacrifices, which were meant to secure fertility, victory and social order. At first, worship took place in open spaces such as groves and lakes. After the third century CE, cult buildings were also purposely constructed for ritual, although they were never widespread.

Society also included practitioners of seiðr, a form of sorcery that some scholars describe as shamanistic. Burial customs varied, with both inhumation and cremation, and the dead were usually buried with grave goods, which gives archaeologists a glimpse of the beliefs that surrounded death.

The Norse maintained close contact with their neighbours, including the Sámi, the Finns, the Anglo-Saxons, the Greenlandic Inuit and speakers of Celtic and Slavic languages. Economic and marital ties existed, and religious influences moved in both directions over the centuries. Enslaved people from the British Isles were also common across the Nordic world during the Viking Age.

Scholars stress that there was no uniform religion. The archaeologists Anders Andrén, Kristina Jennbert and Catharina Raudvere have argued that pre-Christian Norse religion is not a stable, uniform category, and the scholar Karen Bek-Pedersen suggests that it should be thought of as several systems in the plural. The historian of religion Hilda Ellis Davidson observed that its beliefs ranged from complex symbolism to the simple folk beliefs of ordinary people.

By the twelfth century Christianity had replaced the old religion, although elements of it survived in Scandinavian folklore. In the nineteenth century, during the Romantic movement, interest in these myths revived, inspiring a wide range of artworks, and the first academic research on the subject began at that time.

Trivia

A name with a nostalgic flavour. The medieval Icelandic expression forn siðr, "old custom", is one of the most romantic names for this religion. It was used in medieval Icelandic sources to refer to the tradition that existed before Christianity.
A religion without a sacred book. This tradition had no codified sacred texts. Much of what is known about its myths comes from works written in the thirteenth century, when Christianity had already become dominant.
Trees and lakes as sacred places. Before cult buildings were constructed, ceremonies took place in groves and beside lakes. These natural spaces were sacred settings where sacrifices were performed.
