Myth and religion

The way of harmony with the Tao

Taoism

Taoism is a philosophical and religious tradition from China that encourages living in harmony with the Tao, the ever-changing principle that underlies and runs through everything that exists. Its key texts, the Tao Te Ching and the Zhuangzi, are linked to legendary figures, and over time the tradition developed into an organized religion with gods, rituals and priests. It remains alive today in China, Taiwan and many parts of the Chinese-speaking world.

Taoism, also spelled Daoism, emerged in China and centers on a single word: the Tao, usually translated as 'way', 'road' or 'path'. The term appears in many schools of Chinese thought, but for Taoists it names something deeper, a principle of unity that is constantly changing and that serves as the source, pattern and substance of everything that exists. It is not a creator god in the Western sense, but a force that unfolds through the world without issuing commands.

The foundational texts are the Tao Te Ching, traditionally attributed to Laozi, and the Zhuangzi, a collection of anecdotes and parables associated with Zhuang Zhou. Tradition says Laozi was an elderly sage who, on leaving China, wrote down his teaching in a short book. Scholars debate whether he really existed, and many think the text was composed well after the date traditionally given for it. What survives is its voice: brief sayings about water, emptiness and humility, suggesting that the soft can overcome the hard.

One of the most distinctive ideas is wu wei, often rendered as 'non-action' or effortless action. It does not mean doing nothing, but acting in step with the natural course of things rather than forcing them against their nature. Closely related is the notion that order in the world does not come from laws imposed from outside, but from the place each being occupies in a universe in constant motion.

Taoist ethics are often summarized through the Three Treasures: compassion, frugality and humility. These values partly contrast with the Confucian emphasis on rules, rites and social hierarchy. For Taoists, rigid regulations pull people away from spontaneity and simplicity, while wisdom lies in observing and letting oneself flow with what is natural.

Yin and yang complete this picture. They are not forces locked in a battle between good and evil, but complementary poles of a single reality. The original image is a mountain: the sunlit slope is yang, the shaded one is yin, two faces of the same landform. For this reason a Taoist does not place life absolutely above death, or the positive above the negative; everything belongs to the same flow.

Taoist stories often illustrate this attitude. One of the best known tells of a farmer whose horse runs away, and who answers his neighbours' sympathy with the words 'who knows whether it is good or bad?'. The horse returns with wild horses, then his son breaks a leg trying to ride one, and that injury later spares him from military conscription. The lesson is not fatalism but caution about hasty judgements on what happens to us.

Many practitioners aimed at immortality, understood at times literally and at others as a long and full life. To this end they developed external alchemy, working with minerals, and internal alchemy, using breathing and meditation. Laozi was later deified, and an extensive pantheon formed of immortals, folk heroes, famous generals and sages who had attained that state.

Organized religion appears during the Han period. The Way of the Celestial Masters, founded in the 2nd century CE, was the first institutional form of religious Taoism, with priestly hierarchies and rituals. Centuries later schools such as Shangqing, Lingbao and Quanzhen combined inner meditation with worship. Taoism also blended with Buddhism, which had arrived from India, and with popular religion, so that in everyday practice it is hard to separate what is Taoist from what is folk tradition.

Taoism's influence extends beyond religion. Its ideas about qi, the vital energy, shaped traditional Chinese medicine and practices such as qigong and tai chi. In martial arts, Wudang Mountain is associated with styles inspired by Taoist thought. Taoism also left its mark on politics: its rejection of war and greed contrasts with the ambitions of those who used it as a basis for movements of power, as with the Yellow Turban Rebellion at the end of the Han dynasty.

Today Taoism is one of the religions officially recognized in China, it is significant in Taiwan, and it has communities across Southeast Asia and in the West. For many of its followers it is a way of life combining meditation, simple ethics and veneration of ancestors. For an outside observer, it shows how a philosophical idea can become a religion without ceasing to be, in large part, an invitation to look at the world with humility.

Trivia

A short book. The Tao Te Ching is one of the most translated texts from China and fits on a few pages, divided into short chapters.
The mountain origin. The original image of yin and yang comes from the two slopes of a mountain: the sunlit one and the shaded one.
The farmer's horse. The story of the lost horse illustrates the idea that we cannot always tell whether an event is good or bad.
