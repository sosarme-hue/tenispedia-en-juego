Myth and religion

From Babylonian skies to Germanic gods

Names of the days of the week

The names of the days of the week in many languages come from the stars that ancient people watched move across the sky, while in others they honour local deities. Their history links Babylonian astronomy, Jewish and Christian tradition, and the Germanic adaptation of the Roman system. Today different naming and numbering systems coexist, each reflecting a different layer of that history.

A week is a cycle of seven consecutive days. The word comes from the Latin septimana, from septem, meaning seven. The international standard ISO 8601, adopted by most countries, treats Monday as the first day and Sunday as the last. It feels natural now, but the cycle does not follow the Earth's rotation or the solar year, as the day and the month do. Its roots lie in Babylonian astronomy, which linked the phases of the Moon with a group of heavenly bodies and deities.

The Babylonians noticed that roughly a week passed between two phases of the Moon, such as the new moon and the full moon. They organised a seven-day cycle that began at the new moon, with each day dedicated to different gods. According to the sources, offerings were made on these days to draw attention to different aspects of reality. Divine pairs such as Marduk and Ishtar, Ninlil and Nergal, Sin and Shamash, and Enki and Mah are associated with days 7, 14, 21 and 28. Tablets from the reigns of Cyrus the Great and Cambyses II show, however, that these dates were not fixed. The start of the month could vary, and sometimes the cycle ended with a final week of eight or nine days.

Later, this cycle was joined to the seven heavenly bodies that moved against the fixed stars: the Sun, the Moon and the five planets visible to the naked eye. In the geocentric model of the time, the order from slowest to fastest was Saturn, Jupiter, Mars, Sun, Venus, Mercury and Moon. Some Mediterranean peoples believed that each hour of the day was ruled by one of these bodies, following the reverse order of their distances. The astrologer Vettius Valens, the best-known authority on ancient astrology, explains that the first hour of the day began at sunset. If you move through this list in steps of three, you get the order of the weekdays: Saturn, Sun, Moon, Mars, Mercury, Jupiter and Venus.

In Romance languages and English, the day names still carry traces of those celestial bodies, mostly through Latin. Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday and Friday refer to the Moon, Mars, Mercury, Jupiter and Venus in various forms. Saturday and Sunday have different roots. In English they keep their Roman origins, the day of Saturn and the day of the Sun. In Spanish, sábado comes from the Hebrew shabbat, the day of rest, and domingo from the Latin dominica, the Lord's day. In Germanic regions, Roman gods were replaced by northern equivalents. Tiw, a god of combat, gave his name to Tuesday; Woden to Wednesday; Thor to Thursday; and Frigg or Freya, a goddess, to Friday. These correspondences are not exact, but they show how each people adapted the system to its own pantheon.

Other languages chose numbering. In Hebrew the days are counted from Sunday, except the seventh, which is shabbat. In Arabic they are also numbered, with exceptions such as Friday, al-Jum'ah, and Saturday, as-Sabt. Portuguese numbers the days from Monday to Friday, as in segunda-feira and terça-feira, and keeps sábado and domingo. In modern Chinese, the names are built on numbers: Monday is the first day of the cycle, and Sunday is called the day of the Sun. Hindu astrology uses planetary names too, such as śukra-vāra for Friday, named after Venus, and budha-vāra for Wednesday, named after Mercury. These terms spread into languages of South and Southeast Asia.

The starting point of the week has also varied. Jewish tradition, based on Genesis, counts the seventh day as a day of rest, the shabbat, and the week begins on Sunday. For Christianity, Sunday became the Lord's Day. In the Roman Empire, according to the sources, the seven-day week gradually replaced the eight-day nundinal cycle, and Emperor Constantine made it official in AD 321. A graffito found in Pompeii dates 6 February of AD 60 as dies solis, and it is one of the earliest records linking the name of Sunday with the Sun.

Weekend customs are not universal either. Cultures with a strong European heritage usually take Saturday and Sunday, while several Muslim-majority countries have counted Friday as part of the rest. Algeria changed its weekend in 1976, after independence from France, and modified it again in 2009. There were also experiments with different weeks: revolutionary France adopted a ten-day week, and the Soviet Union used five- and six-day weeks for years to remove the religious associations of Saturday and Sunday. The seven-day week returned in 1940.

Trivia

A Sunday in Pompeii. A Pompeian graffito dated to AD 60 names a day as dies solis, the day of the Sun. It is one of the earliest records of this link.
Counting the week. Portuguese numbers the days from Monday to Friday but keeps sábado and domingo. Hebrew and Arabic also use numbers.
A one-handed god. In Norse mythology Tiw was a one-handed god linked to single combat and pledges. He gave his name to Tuesday in English.
Changing weekends. Algeria changed its weekend in 1976 and again in 2009, showing that rest conventions also depend on political and economic decisions.
