The winter festival of the Germanic peoples and its legacy in Christmas

Yule

Yule was a midwinter festival of the Germanic peoples, tied to the solstice and to family life, which Christianity eventually merged into Christmas. Its name survives in English and several Northern European languages, and medieval texts describe feasts, sacrifices, oaths and games that marked the season.

Yule is both a midwinter festival of the Germanic peoples and the season in which it was held. Over time, Christianity folded it into Christmas, so that the word now names Christmas in English and in languages such as Swedish and Finnish. For that reason, it is not always easy to separate the older pagan celebration from the Christian holiday that absorbed it.

The earliest Germanic calendars record two Yule months around the winter solstice. A Gothic calendar preserved in the Codex Ambrosianus mentions the month before the Yule month, which implies a following Yule month that was never written down. Much later, the English monk Bede explained in his work on the reckoning of time that the heathen Anglo-Saxons called December and January Giuli, and that they began their year on 25 December, a night they named the «Mothers' Night». Bede took those mothers to be goddesses, perhaps the matres, although that identification remains a hypothesis.

Old Norse sources also record two Yule months. The Icelandic Bókarbót, usually dated to around 1220, places them from the middle of November to the middle of January in the Julian calendar. Remarkably, Sweden kept using a lunisolar calendar into the early twentieth century, in which the moon seen at Epiphany was called the «Yule moon», and a market was held on the full moon that followed.

The exact date of the pagan festival is debated. In the sixth century, the historian Procopius of Caesarea described the Heruli, who lived in «Thule», probably in present-day Norway. According to his account, the sun was not seen there for forty nights in winter, and their greatest festival fell twenty days after the solstice, when the sun returned. Some scholars read this as a description of a pagan Yule, while others suggest it may refer to Sámi peoples rather than Germanic ones. In Norse sources, the only precise date appears in Snorri Sturluson's Hákonar saga góða, which places the Nordic Yule on the Midwinter Night and says it lasted three days. Snorri puts it about a month after the solstice, and the researcher Andreas Nordberg proposes that it was held on the full moon after the solstice, roughly between 19 and 21 January in the Gregorian calendar.

In medieval Scandinavia, a blót, or ritual sacrifice, often took place during Yule. Animals were offered to the gods, their meat was cooked and shared among those present, and ale or mead was drunk in ceremony. Communal eating was the heart of the festival. Skaldic poetry even uses the word for a feast in a kenning for battle, calling it a raven's Yule feast. The poem Haraldskvæði, composed around 900, says that Harald Fairhair «drinks Yule», apparently meaning that he celebrated it. The sagas also describe the Yule feast of King Halfdan the Black, and the duties that King Hákon the Good had to fulfil when he took part in sacrificial feasts.

The custom of drinking survived Christianisation. The early Norwegian law code Den ældre Gulathings-Lov set penalties for mistakes in brewing ale for Yule. It required brewing in groups and blessing the ale in the name of Christ and St Mary for prosperity and peace, a formula that probably began in a pagan setting. According to Hákonar saga góða, King Hákon, working to Christianise Norway, moved Yule to Christmas time and fined anyone who lacked enough ale for the feast, and he decreed that the celebration should last as long as the ale did.

Other sources describe solemn oaths sworn on Yule-Eve. In the Helgakviða Hjörvarðssonar and the Hervarar saga ok Heiðreks, participants placed their hands on a pig before swearing. Some manuscripts say the animal was consecrated to Freyr and sacrificed afterwards. Games were also part of the celebration. The sagas mention wrestling, called glíma, the ball game knattleikr, and tug-of-war. The phrase «Frey's game» in Haraldskvæði may refer to a Yule ritual dedicated to the god, although it can also be read as a poetic term for battle.

A tenth-century Byzantine book of ceremonies describes a dance called the gothikon, performed at Twelfth Night. Two groups of men moved in circles, one inside the other, while two masked men dressed in skins beat their shields and shouted «Toúl», a word many scholars take to be a corruption of Yule. The dancers are called Goths, but they were more likely Scandinavians, perhaps Varangians. The scene has been compared with a fresco in St Sophia Cathedral in Kyiv, where one of the warriors appears with the head of a dog.

Norse myth also left its mark on Yule folklore. Stories of trolls and undead draugar visiting halls and farms at this time, and customs such as julebukking and the Wild Hunt, are sometimes linked to older beliefs. Odin himself has among his names the Yule-man, which shows how closely the god was tied to these winter festivities. Today, some neopagan groups celebrate Yule as a festival separate from Christmas, rebuilding their rituals from these ancient sources.
