Myth and religion

An Afro-Brazilian religion of the orixás

Candomblé

Candomblé is an Afro-Brazilian religion that took shape mainly in Bahia during the 19th century, blending African traditions with Roman Catholicism. Its mythology centres on the orixás, spirits tied to natural forces who act as intermediaries between people and a supreme creator. Each house of worship tells these stories in its own way.

Candomblé emerged in Brazil from the beliefs that enslaved West and Central Africans carried across the Atlantic, above all those of Yoruba, Fon, Ewe and Bantu peoples, which blended with the Catholicism of the Portuguese colonists. It has no central authority and no single sacred text. Each house, called a terreiro, operates independently, which is why its stories vary from one place to another.

At the heart of its mythology is the pantheon of the orixás, known as inkices in the Angola nation and as voduns in the Jeje nation. They are understood as intermediaries between humanity and a supreme being, called Olorum or Olodumare, who created everything but is so remote that he receives little direct worship. Accounts of the origin of the world differ. One tradition holds that Olorum charged Oxalá, a frail old man who walks with a sceptre, with creating human beings. Another says that the junior orixás are all children of Oxalá and one of his wives, Nanã or Iemanjá. A third claims that Oxalá made the world alone, out of something like a thick porridge. Some say instead that Nanã is Oxalá's grandmother and Iemanjá's mother, and that Iemanjá later becomes his wife as well.

Each orixá has its own personality and is linked to a natural phenomenon. Xangô is associated with thunder and lightning, and one of his wives is Obá, a warrior who has only one ear. Ogum is the orixá of war and iron, often shown with a machete, and his companion is Oxóssi, the hunter and lord of the forest. Oyá governs winds and storms. Oxumaré is regarded as both male and female and appears as a serpent or a rainbow. Oxum embodies love, beauty and wealth, and is linked to fresh water, fish and butterflies. She is married to Ifá, the orixá of divination. Obaluaiê, also called Omolu, is linked to infectious disease and its cure, while Ossaim is the keeper of leaves and herbal knowledge. Tempo, the orixá of time, is associated with trees.

Exú holds a special place. He guards doorways and carries messages between people and the other orixás, so he is honoured and fed first in any ritual. Many houses keep a clay head decorated with cowrie shells or nails at the entrance to represent him. In everyday speech he is sometimes described as a devil, but within Candomblé he is not a force of pure evil. He is believed to open or close the paths of a person's destiny, bringing both help and harm, and he must be treated with care.

According to belief, each person is linked before birth to a tutelary orixá, the dono da cabeça, or owner of the head, which shapes their character. Its identity is discovered through divination, using the Opele-Ifá chain or the casting of cowrie shells. Some followers add further orixás connected to a person, and some believe in one inherited from a deceased relative. The child forms of the orixás, called erês, are considered the most mischievous and uncontrollable of spirits.

Other spirits also belong to this world. The caboclos usually represent Indigenous people or boiadeiros, backcountry cowboys. Their name probably comes from a Tupi term, and they are said to dwell in a forest realm called Aruanda. They are often pictured smoking cigars and drinking beer. Other Candomblé practitioners, especially those of Nagô tradition, regard the caboclo nation as inferior, and movements seeking a return to African roots usually reject these spirits.

During slavery, many worshippers hid their devotion to the orixás behind altars holding images of Catholic saints. Ogum thus came to be linked with Saint Anthony of Padua, Omolu with Saint Lazarus, Oxum with the Immaculate Conception and Oxalá with Our Lord of Bonfim. Some scholars think this fusion began in Africa itself, encouraged by missionaries. Since the late twentieth century, some houses have tried to separate the orixás from the saints to stress their African origins.

The religion is divided into nations, each shaped mainly by a particular African tradition. The best known are the Ketu, rooted in Yoruba belief; the Jeje, linked to Ewe and Fon traditions; and the Angola, drawing on Bantu roots. The word Jeje comes from a Yoruba term meaning outsider and does not name any single people in Africa. Each nation has its own songs, its own sacred language and its own deities.

On life and death, Candomblé draws much of its cosmology from traditional Yoruba religion. The material world of the living is called aiê, and the spirit realm, orun, is divided into nine levels. Death is personified as Iku, and the spirits of the dead, the egun, can receive offerings.
