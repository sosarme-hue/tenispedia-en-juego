Proto-Indo-European mythology

Proto-Indo-European mythology is a reconstruction of the gods and stories of the speakers of an ancestral language from which Sanskrit, Latin, Greek and Old Norse descend. Proto-Indo-European mythology is a scholarly reconstruction of the gods and stories of the people who spoke the ancestral language behind Sanskrit, Latin, Greek and Old Norse. Because these speakers lived before writing, they left no texts about their myths. What we know comes from comparative mythology. Researchers match related divine names, shared attributes and similar stories across distant traditions, and assume that some of the original belief system survived in those later cultures. The most repeated features are fairly solid, but each specific detail is still a conjecture.

The best-reconstructed figures are those with related names and similar roles across several branches. Dyḗws Ph₂tḗr embodies the daylight sky and light, and his consort Dʰéǵʰōm is Mother Earth. Their daughter H₂éwsōs is the goddess of dawn, and the Divine Twins are their sons. A solar goddess, Seh₂ul, also appears. Other deities, such as Perkʷunos, the weather god, or Péh₂usōn, the herding god, are attested only in Western or Graeco-Aryan traditions, so they may be later additions. Many great gods of later traditions, including Thor, Tarhun, Indra and Zeus, appear as lords of the thunderbolt.

Some stories are considered so old that they can be dated to the common period on linguistic and thematic grounds. One describes a figure linked to thunder who slays a many-headed serpent to release water that had been held back. Another is a creation myth involving two brothers, in which one sacrifices the other to form the world. Probably there was also a belief that the Otherworld was guarded by a watchdog and could be reached only by crossing a river.

The brother myth is the most studied. Bruce Lincoln proposes three figures: the Man (Manu), the Twin (Yemos) and the Third (*Trito). The Man sacrifices his twin and creates the world and humanity from the remains. The twin becomes the first king and the Man the first priest. In this reading, social classes arise from the sacrificed body: the priesthood from the head, the warriors from the chest and arms, and the producers from the sexual organs and legs. Later, the Third recovers cattle that a three-headed serpent had stolen from the gods, and returns them to be sacrificed. Echoes of these figures survive in Yama, Yima, Ymir, Manu and Mannus, and possibly in Remus.

Interpretive schools disagree. The naturist school, associated with Friedrich Max Müller, read myths as allegories of celestial phenomena, above all the sun. It lost most of its support in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, although some scholars have revived it. The ritual school, with James George Frazer and Jane Ellen Harrison, sees the stories as inventions meant to explain rites. The functionalist school draws on Georges Dumézil's theory of three functions: sacred sovereignty, warrior force and production. It has been very influential, though critics say it is so broad that it is hard to prove or disprove. The structuralist school, finally, looks for binary oppositions, such as the one proposed by Tamaz Gamkrelidze and Vyacheslav Ivanov around marriage.

The sources carry unequal weight. The Rigveda, the oldest of the Vedas, was for a long time almost equated with Proto-Indo-European myth, although it is now used with more caution. Norse mythology, though recorded late, has a large body of Icelandic material. Baltic mythology is linguistically very conservative, and Latvian folk songs are an important source, though they come late. Greek mythology contributes little, because pre-Greek and Near Eastern influences obscure the Indo-European elements.

In cosmology, the basic opposition is between the immortal gods above and mortal humans on earth, a flat disc surrounded by waters. The stars seem to have been mainly ornamental. According to Martin L. West, the world tree was probably a later borrowing from North Asian cosmologies. Many creation myths describe an earlier time with neither sky nor air, and explain nature from the body of a primordial being: its flesh as earth, its bones as stone, its eyes as the sun. Linguists have reconstructed *h₂ṛtós, meaning what is rightly fitted together, from which Sanskrit ṛtá and Greek artús derive.

A historical document offers a further clue. In 1907 a sworn oath was found at Bogazkoy, the ancient Hattusa, in which the Mitanni king Mattiwaza pledged loyalty to the Hittite emperor Suppiluliuma I around 1340 BCE. The witnesses named include Mitra, Varuna, Indra and the Nasatya twins, deities of the Indo-Iranian branch rather than the Anatolian one. For Dumézil, these gods mirror the priestly, warrior and producer classes of Aryan society, although that interpretation is debated. Claims about an original Babylonian trinity do not hold up either, since those gods belong to different cultures and eras.

Trivia

A shared number. The word for three is one of the few that look alike across the known Indo-European languages, along with words such as mother, night and star.
Religio. In Latin, religio meant scruple or scrupulous respect, especially toward oaths and sworn agreements.
Uranus and Varuna. The Indian god Varuna and the Greek Uranus, the sky, are considered to derive from the same Indo-European root.
