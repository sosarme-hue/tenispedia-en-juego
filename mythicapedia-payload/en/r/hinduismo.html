Myth and religion

A plural tradition of myths, duties and liberation

Hinduism

Hinduism is an umbrella term for a range of Indian religious traditions united by their reference to the Vedas and by the idea of a cosmic order sustained by dharma. It has no single founder and no unified creed, and its myths, preserved in epics and Puranas, coexist with very different philosophies. Today it is followed by about 1.2 billion people, mostly in South Asia.

Hinduism has no single founder and no unified creed. It is better understood as a family of traditions, known as sampradayas, that share a reference to the Vedas and a belief in a cosmic order maintained when people fulfil their duties. Estimates place its followers at around 1.17 billion, roughly 15 per cent of humanity, and most live in South Asia.

The name carries its own history. The word Hindu comes from Sindhu, the Sanskrit name of the Indus River, which Persians pronounced as hindu. For Persian and Arab writers it was a geographical term for people living beyond the river, with no religious meaning. Religious connotations appeared later, in medieval texts, and European merchants and colonists of the seventeenth century used the word for the inhabitants of India as a religious community. The term Hinduism is credited to Ram Mohan Roy around 1816 to 1817, although it became common in the 1840s, when Indians opposed to British rule used it to set themselves apart from Muslims and Christians. Before colonial authorities began sorting people by religion, identity was shaped more by locality, language, caste, occupation and sect.

Believers often speak of sanatana dharma, the eternal religion, a phrase that spread mainly in modern times and points to truths without a human origin. Vaidika dharma, the religion of the Vedas, is another historical name, used by some writers to describe the same tradition.

Texts are grouped into shruti, what is heard or revealed, and smriti, what is remembered and handed down. The most important include the Vedas, the Upanishads, the Puranas, the Mahabharata with the Bhagavad Gita, the Ramayana and the Agamas. The epics and Puranas hold most of the myths that are now widely known: the creation of the world, the avatars of Vishnu, and the deeds of gods and heroes, including the stories of Shiva and the goddess. Puranic chronology, based on these stories, places the tradition thousands of years back. Historians instead see Hinduism taking shape as a synthesis between roughly 500 BCE and 300 CE, after the Vedic period, when the epics and the first Puranas were composed, and flourishing in the medieval era as Buddhism declined in India.

A central idea is the four puruṣārthas, the aims of human life: dharma (ethics and duty), artha (prosperity and work), kama (desire and pleasure) and moksha (liberation). They connect with karma, the link between action and its consequences, and saṃsāra, the cycle of death and rebirth. Non-violence, compassion and patience are among the duties traditionally regarded as universal, and ahiṃsā, non-injury to living beings, is one of the most widely cited.

Practice is extremely varied. It includes daily worship or puja, fire rituals, recitation, chanting, devotion or bhakti, meditation, pilgrimage or yatra, offerings to ancestors and family rites of passage. Some people choose sannyasa, leaving social life for a lifelong search for liberation.

Hindu philosophy recognises six āstika schools that accept the authority of the Vedas: Sankhya, Yoga, Nyaya, Vaisheshika, Mimamsa and Vedanta. Vedanta and Yoga are the most prominent today. The major denominations are Vaishnavism, centred on Vishnu, Shaivism, centred on Shiva, Shaktism, devoted to the goddess, and Smartism, which honours several deities as expressions of a supreme reality.

The range of belief is wide. Hindus may be polytheists, monotheists, pantheists, agnostics or atheists, and there is no required dogma. The Vedas are respected across most traditions, although many people do not follow their ritual rules. Questioning authority also has a long place in the tradition's intellectual history.

In recent times some political movements have drawn on mythological figures such as Rama to shape national identities. Historians and believers continue to debate how these uses relate to the older tradition, and the question remains open.

Today Hinduism is the majority faith in India, Nepal, Mauritius and Bali in Indonesia, and it has significant communities in Southeast Asia, the Caribbean, Guyana, Suriname, North America, Europe and Oceania.

Trivia

A name from a river. The word Hindu comes from Sindhu, the name of the Indus River, which Persians used for the peoples living beyond it.
The eternal religion. Many believers call their tradition sanatana dharma, the eternal religion, because they see its truths as having no beginning or end in human history.
Six schools of philosophy. The six āstika schools accept the authority of the Vedas. Among them, Vedanta and Yoga are today the best known.
