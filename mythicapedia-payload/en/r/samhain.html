The Gaelic festival that closed the harvest and opened winter

Samhain

Samhain is a Gaelic festival on 1 November that closed the harvest season and opened the darker half of the year. In Irish mythology it was a threshold time, when burial mounds opened onto the Otherworld and the dead and supernatural beings could cross into our world. Its legacy runs through to modern Halloween.

Samhain is a Gaelic festival held on 1 November. It marks the end of the harvest and the start of winter, the so-called 'darker half' of the year. Because the Celtic day began and ended at sunset, celebrations opened on the evening of 31 October. The date falls roughly halfway between the autumn equinox and the winter solstice, and it was one of four Gaelic seasonal festivals, alongside Imbolc, Bealtaine and Lughnasa. It was observed across Ireland, Scotland and the Isle of Man, and the Brittonic peoples had their own versions, such as Calan Gaeaf in Wales.

The name comes from the Old and Middle Irish Samain or Samuin. For a long time it was linked to the proto-Indo-European root *semo-, meaning 'summer', but that explanation is now disputed. John T. Koch asks why a festival opening winter would carry a word for summer, and Joseph Vendryes points out that the Celtic summer ended in August. The linguists Xavier Delamarre and Ranko Matasović propose a root meaning 'assembly' or 'reunion', perhaps an 'assembly of the living and the dead'. The Gaulish Coligny calendar, from around the 2nd century CE, names a month SAMON and marks 'the three nights of Samon', which hints at a festival, though its details remain unclear.

In Irish mythology, Samhain was a threshold time when the doorways to the Otherworld opened. Burial mounds were seen as entrances, and the aos sí, the spirits or fairies, could slip into the human world more easily. Most scholars regard them as remnants of pagan deities. People left food and drink to appease them, hoping that the household and its livestock would survive the winter. While Bealtaine was a festival for the living, the early literature describes Samhain as 'essentially a festival for the dead'. The souls of dead relatives were believed to return seeking hospitality, and a place was set for them at the table.

The tales are vivid. Each year the fire-breather Aillen emerged from the Otherworld, lulled the court to sleep with his music and burned the palace of Tara. One Samhain the young Fionn mac Cumhaill stayed awake and killed him with a magic spear, which earned him leadership of the fianna. In another story, the Otherworld being Cúldubh snatched a roast pig from a mound at Slievenamon, and Fionn killed him with a spear throw as he slipped back inside. Fionn's thumb was trapped by the closing door, and because it had been inside the Otherworld, sucking on it granted him great wisdom. In the Acallam na Senórach, three female werewolves emerged from the cave of Cruachan every Samhain to kill livestock, until Caílte destroyed them with the help of Cas Corach's harp. In the Lebor Gabála Érenn, the people of Nemed had to hand over two-thirds of their children, their corn and their milk to the Fomorians each Samhain, monstrous beings who embody darkness, blight and drought.

Some sources are darker. The Dindsenchas and the Annals of the Four Masters, both written by Christian monks, link Samhain with the idol Crom Cruach, to which a firstborn child was supposedly sacrificed. King Tigernmas and three-quarters of his people were said to have died worshipping it on one Samhain. Other legendary kings were fated to die on the feast. Diarmait mac Cerbaill and Muirchertach mac Ercae each suffered a threefold death of wounding, burning and drowning. In Togail Bruidne Dá Derga, King Conaire Mór, having broken his geasa, meets his end on Samhain after three undead riders, messengers of Donn, the god of the dead, warn him. Some academics think such stories may recall real human sacrifice, and they note that several Irish bog bodies appear to have been ritually killed kings, some of them around the time of Samhain.

Samhain also frames major events. The invasion of Ulster that drives the Táin Bó Cúailnge begins on this night, a surprise because cattle raiding was normally a summer activity. The Second Battle of Magh Tuireadh also starts on Samhain, when the Morrígan and the Dagda come together before the battle against the Fomorians. Certain places were linked to the festival too, such as the Hill of Ward in County Meath, where a great gathering and bonfire were thought to have taken place.

Detailed descriptions come late. The festival was not recorded in depth until the early modern period, when herds came down from the summer pastures and livestock were slaughtered for the winter. Bonfires were lit, believed to protect and purify. Mumming and guising, going from door to door in costume and reciting verses for food, were part of the celebrations, and divination with nuts and apples was popular. In the late nineteenth century John Rhys and James Frazer proposed that it had been the Celtic New Year, a view still debated. Frazer, in The Golden Bough (1890), linked the dates of 1 May and 1 November with transhumance, when cattle moved to highland pastures and back again.

After Christianisation, the Western Church set 1 November as All Saints' Day, and 2 November later became All Souls' Day. Over the centuries Samhain and these feasts influenced one another, and much of modern Halloween grew from that mix. Many American customs came with Irish and Scottish immigrants. Since the late twentieth century, Celtic neopagans and Wiccans have observed Samhain as a religious holiday. In Ireland the night was also called oíche na sprideanna, or 'spirit night'.

Trivia

A month named after the festival. The Gaelic names for November come from Samhain, so in Irish and Scottish Gaelic the month takes its name from the festival that opened the dark season.
Tombs aligned with the sunrise. Some Neolithic passage tombs in Ireland, such as the Mound of the Hostages on the Hill of Tara, are aligned with the sunrise around the times of Samhain and Imbolc.
Hop-tu-Naa. On the Isle of Man, the night of 31 October is also called Hop-tu-Naa, one of the local names for the celebration.
