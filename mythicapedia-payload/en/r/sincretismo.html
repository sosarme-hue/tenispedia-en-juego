Myth and religion

The blending of religious traditions and its link to myth

Syncretism

Syncretism refers to the merging of beliefs, deities and rituals from different traditions. Since antiquity, rulers, priests and whole peoples have combined gods and stories to unite communities or to survive the imposition of a new faith. Its history shows that almost no religion is entirely pure.

Syncretism is a term used in cultural anthropology and in the comparative study of religion to describe the blending of two or more traditions. It is usually understood that such unions do not always hold together in a deep coherence, although the word is also used to highlight how a culture or religion has been built through fusion. Plutarch, who used the term in one of his moral essays, described the Cretans setting aside their internal quarrels when war threatened. Erasmus of Rotterdam later revived the word in Latin to refer to the reconciliation of different philosophical systems or beliefs.

Its etymology is more debated than it might seem. The traditional explanation links it to the Greek syn-, meaning "with", and kerannymi, "to mix", related to krasis, "mixture". Another hypothesis connects it with the ethnic adjective for Cretans. What is clear is that the modern sense took hold in the seventeenth century, when the word began to describe the union of religious traditions.

Almost every cultural movement has some syncretic features. Christianity adopted ancient rites and drew on Greek philosophy, but the word is usually reserved for phenomena that arise already as a fusion, such as Afro-American religions, or for thinkers who deliberately tried to synthesise two opposed authors. There is also an important difference between mixing that is accepted and mixing that is imposed. Popular syncretism may show the assimilation of a foreign tradition, but it can also survive in hidden form. Some conversos in Spain, for instance, developed a cult to the victims of the Spanish Inquisition that combined Catholic elements with a quiet resistance to that faith.

One of the best-documented cases is that of the Kushite kings, who ruled Upper Egypt for roughly a century and all of Egypt for about 57 years, from 721 to 664 BCE. In Manetho's list they form the Twenty-fifth Dynasty. These rulers identified their own god Dedun with the Egyptian Osiris, and they kept this worship even after being driven out of Egypt. A temple to this syncretic god, built by the ruler Atlanersa, was excavated at Jebel Barkal.

The Hellenistic period was especially fertile for such mixtures. Rulers often identified local deities in their territories with gods of the Greek pantheon, as a way of strengthening the cohesion of their kingdoms. The practice was accepted in most places, but Jews rejected it firmly, since they regarded identifying Yahweh with the Greek Zeus as the worst blasphemy. Rome followed a similar path. It first merged its traditional gods with Greek ones, producing a single Greco-Roman pantheon, and later linked the members of that pantheon with the local deities of various provinces.

Some examples involve syncretism openly embraced. In Japan, Shinto beliefs were blended into Buddhism, and Christianity, as it spread into Gaul, Ireland, Britain, Germany and Scandinavia, absorbed Germanic and Celtic pagan elements. Centuries later, Christian missionaries in North America identified Manitou, the life force in the traditional beliefs of the Algonquian peoples, with the Christian God. Similar identifications occurred elsewhere in the Americas and Africa, where missionaries met belief in a Supreme God or Supreme Spirit. In Latin America, many baroque churches were built on former indigenous sacred sites, and festivals such as the Day of the Dead show how pre-Hispanic and Catholic traditions overlap.

How syncretism is judged has always depended on who is judging. Many adherents of revealed religions, such as Second Temple Judaism, Islam or much of Protestantism, have seen mixing as a corruption of the original truth. Conversely, syncretism can ease coexistence between different cultures, which is why it has appealed to rulers of multi-ethnic realms. The Mughal emperor Akbar illustrates this tension. In the sixteenth century he proposed the Din-i Ilahi, a faith drawing on Islam, Hinduism, Christianity, Jainism and Zoroastrianism. Sources disagree on whether it was a Sufi order or a new religion, and it attracted very few followers.

In anthropology the term has generated debate. Some researchers argue that it presupposes pure, clearly bounded cultures, something that rarely exists, and prefer to speak of exchange or processes of adaptation. Yet it remains useful for describing how traditions adjust when they meet others. That process often moves through two phases. Accommodation adjusts coexistence on the surface, changing the façade of each tradition while individuals keep their values. Assimilation, slower and largely unconscious, eventually creates a shared history and a common group. Myths and legends often become the place where that shared history is fixed, as with flood stories or gods inherited from earlier traditions. Many religions, including Judaism with its Mesopotamian echoes, show traces of earlier traditions.
