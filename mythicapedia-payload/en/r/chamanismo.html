Myth and religion

Go-betweens of the visible world and the spirits

Shamanism

Shamanism groups together traditional beliefs and practices in which a specialist, the shaman, enters altered states of consciousness to communicate with spirits and gain help with healing, hunting or guidance for the community. The word comes from a Tungusic language of Siberia, but scholars still debate whether it fits the wide range of traditions it is used to describe.

Shamanism refers to a family of beliefs and practices that appear in many parts of the world, and that may go back to prehistoric times. In its most widespread form, the visible world is thought to be filled with invisible forces and spirits that shape the lives of people, animals and the natural world. The shaman is the person who can move between these realms, usually in trance, in order to solve concrete problems for the community.

The key difference from animism, which in principle is shared by everyone in a society, is specialisation. Anyone may recognise that a river or a forest holds spirits, but the shaman has knowledge and abilities of his or her own, often gained through a long apprenticeship. That is why shamans are often described as the experts that animist communities turn to. Unlike organised religions, they usually do not form clergy or formal associations, and traditionally they worked on their own, although there are exceptions, such as the Indian tantric practitioners.

The roles attributed to shamans include healing, divination, dream interpretation, leading sacrifices, preserving tradition through stories and songs, and acting as psychopomp, a guide for the souls of the dead. One person may hold several of these roles at once. According to the accounts, a shaman's soul can leave the body and travel through the upper and lower worlds, or make what are called ascents to the sky. Spirits may be benevolent or malevolent, and many illnesses are explained as the loss of a soul or as the work of a harmful spirit. Treatment then aims to recover the missing soul or drive the spirit out.

Animals hold a central place in this worldview. Shamans call on animal images as guides, omens and messengers. In the Amazon, for instance, the jaguar, which can move across land, water and trees, is linked with the figure of the shaman. Among the Selkup, a sea duck is considered an animal spirit because it flies and dives, belonging to both the upper and the lower world. In some Siberian cultures, certain waterbirds are related to the shaman in a similar way, and it is believed that he takes on their form during trance. The shamanic tree, found among peoples such as the Yakut, Dolgan and Evenki, is another image of mediation: its roots belong to the lower world, its trunk to the human world and its crown to the upper world.

Among some Amazonian peoples, such as the Tukano and Desana, the shaman also plays an ecological role. According to their beliefs, overhunting breaks a balance, and animals have souls that can take offence. The shaman negotiates with mythical beings so that game will allow itself to be hunted, and taboos govern conduct towards prey so that animals are not angered. A similar role is recorded among the Piaroa and in several Inuit groups, where the shaman may bring animal spirits from distant places. In other cultures, infertility or a shortage of game is also understood in terms of souls.

The word shaman comes from the Tungusic languages of Siberia. Several explanations have been proposed. One links it with a root meaning to know, so the shaman would be the one who knows. Another places its origin in Manchu or in Turkic, where terms such as kam or baksı were used. Some specialists, with debate, connect it to the Sanskrit śramana, meaning ascetic, through Chinese and Pali. The word reached Russian and German before entering English, and from there Spanish. The first Europeans who used it applied it to Siberian peoples, but it later spread to practices on other continents, and many of today's disputes arise from that expansion.

Specialists do not agree on a definition. The historian Ronald Hutton distinguished several uses, ranging from anyone who contacts spirits in an altered state to a term limited to the Indigenous religions of Siberia. Mircea Eliade proposed a broad definition, a technique of religious ecstasy, although his work has been widely debated. The anthropologist Alice Kehoe argues that the term can encourage the idea of a single, ancient religion, and that practices such as drumming, chanting or trance also occur in non-shamanic cultures. Piers Vitebsky and others agree that the unity is misleading. Scholars such as Juha Pentikäinen and Judith Hangartner have studied specific traditions from within, and Hoppál proposes the word shamanhood to stress local differences.

The criticism also has a political side. In modern Western forms, shamanism has sometimes been sold as a universal spirituality, which can dilute Indigenous practices and reinforce stereotypes such as the noble savage. In Mongolia, local researchers have pointed out that the very idea of shamanism as a separate religion was partly built from outside, and is now being reconstructed with the participation of shamans themselves. That debate does not stop shamanism from remaining alive in many communities, where its stories, songs and symbols still give meaning to the lives of those who practise it.

Trivia

A word with a long journey. The word is thought to have reached English through Russian and German, and early European uses referred to peoples of Siberia.
The world tree. The shamanic tree appears in Siberian cultures such as the Yakut and Evenki and is read as an axis joining the lower, human and upper worlds.
A duck between two worlds. Among the Selkup, a sea duck is considered an animal spirit because it flies and dives, belonging to both the upper and lower worlds.
