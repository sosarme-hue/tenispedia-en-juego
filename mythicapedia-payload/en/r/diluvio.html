Myth and religion

Floods, arks and divine punishment across the world's traditions

Flood myth

A flood myth tells of a great inundation, sent by one or several gods, that destroys civilization as punishment and clears the way for a new beginning. The oldest known versions come from Mesopotamia, but similar stories appear in the Bible, in India, in Iran, in ancient Greece and among Indigenous peoples of North America. Researchers have also looked for natural explanations behind these tales.

A flood myth is a story in which a great flood, usually sent by one or more gods, destroys civilization as a divine punishment. Many of these tales draw a parallel between the floodwaters and the primordial ocean found in some creation myths. In both cases, water cleanses the world of its corruption and prepares it for a new start. Most flood myths also feature a culture hero, a figure who embodies the human desire to keep on living.

The oldest known account comes from Mesopotamia. The Sumerians recorded it in several texts, and the most extensive Akkadian version is the epic of Atra-Hasis, which dates to the eighteenth century BCE. In it, a group of gods begins turning the steppe into a fertile garden and introduces agriculture. The hard labour provokes a revolt among the lower gods, and to end it, the first humans are created to do the work in their place and to reproduce. Eventually, the humans' noise disturbs the upper gods, and Enlil, the highest of all, decides to wipe them out with a flood. The god Enki, who defies that plan, secretly warns his priest Atrahasis, who builds a boat following his instructions and saves his family.

The Epic of Gilgamesh (c. 2100–1800 BCE) also contains a flood episode in which a single family survives. According to current research, that passage was largely borrowed from Atra-Hasis. Another text, the Eridu Genesis, is known from tablets found in the ruins of Nippur in the late 1890s and was translated by the Assyriologist Arno Poebel. The scholar Yi Samuel Chen argues that the flood story was added to several texts during the Old Babylonian period. Something similar happens with the Sumerian King List. The part that deals with the time before the flood has a different style, and some older copies suggest that the list once existed without any mention of the flood. For Chen, the flood as a turning point dividing the history of the world was an idea that developed in that same period.

In the biblical Genesis, Yahweh, who had created man from the dust of the ground, decides to flood the earth because of humanity's corruption. He instructs Noah to build an ark to preserve human and animal life. When the waters begin, all life outside the ark perishes. Once the waters recede, Yahweh promises never again to judge the earth with a flood, and he sets the rainbow as the sign of that promise. The dates proposed for the writing of this text range from the ninth to the fifth century BCE.

In India, the Shatapatha Brahmana (6th century BCE) and the Puranas tell of a great flood, the manvantara-sandhya. The Matsya avatar of Vishnu warns the first man, Manu, and advises him to build a large boat. In Zoroastrianism, Ahriman tries to destroy the world with a drought, which ends when Mithra shoots an arrow into a rock and water gushes out. One man survives in an ark with his cattle. The German scholar Norbert Oettinger argues that the story of Yima and the Vara was originally a flood myth, and that the harsh winter was added because of the dry climate of eastern Iran.

In ancient Greece, Plato's Timaeus describes a similar story. The Bronze race of humans, constantly at war, angers the high god Zeus, who decides to punish humanity with a flood. Prometheus, the Titan who had created humans from clay, reveals the plan to his son Deucalion, who builds an ark to save himself. After nine nights and days, the waters begin to recede and the ark lands on a mountain.

In North America, the Cheyenne, a people of the Great Plains, preserve a tradition in which a flood changed the course of their history, perhaps in the Missouri River valley. The Blackfeet story called «Language on a Mountain» tells how Napi, known as Old Man, gathers the people on a great mountain after the flood. There he gives each group water of a different colour to drink. Those who receive the black water speak the same language, and they are the ancestors of the Blackfoot, Piegan, Siksika and Kainai. The Hopi, in the southwestern United States, have a tradition of a flood that nearly reached the mountaintops.

Researchers have sought natural explanations for such stories. Mesopotamia, like other early river valley civilizations, was prone to flooding, and in some areas a single inundation could destroy almost the whole known world. At Shuruppak, today's Tell Fara in Iraq, excavations in the 1930s found a layer of yellow alluvial sand and clay separating two occupation levels, indicating a flood around 2900 BCE. In legend, Shuruppak was the city of Uta-napishtim, the king who built a boat to survive the deluge. The historian Adrienne Mayor suggests that global flood stories may have been inspired by seashells and fish fossils found in mountain areas. As for the Deucalion myth, a tsunami caused by the Thera eruption, around 1630–1600 BCE, has been proposed as its basis, though it did not affect the cities of mainland Greece. The Black Sea deluge hypothesis, which posits a massive inflow of Mediterranean water around 5600 BCE, remains highly debated.

The idea that a celestial body could cause a flood also has a history. In 1694, Edmond Halley suggested that a worldwide flood might have resulted from a comet passing very close to the Earth. William Whiston, a protégé and popularizer of Isaac Newton, argued in 1696 that a comet caused Noah's flood. Ignatius Donnelly, in his book Ragnarok (1883), claimed that a comet destroyed the advanced civilization of Atlantis, a proposal now regarded as pseudoarchaeology. The archaeologist Bruce Masse analysed 175 flood myths and proposed that an asteroid impact in the Indian Ocean, around 3000–2800 BCE, produced a giant tsunami. None of these hypotheses has scientific consensus behind it.

Trivia

An eclipse in the myths. According to Bruce Masse's analysis, fourteen of the flood myths he studied mention a total solar eclipse.
Seashells on mountains. Ancient Greeks, Egyptians and Romans recorded seashells and fish fossils in high places, and some Greeks took them as evidence that the Earth had been covered by water several times.
