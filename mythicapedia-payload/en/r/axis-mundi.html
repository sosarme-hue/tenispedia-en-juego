Myth and religion

The axis that links heaven and earth

Axis mundi

The axis mundi is a symbol found in very different cultures: a point where heaven and earth meet and from which all directions are oriented. It can take the form of a mountain, a tree or a tower, and it often marks the most sacred place in a territory. Its story shows how myth has given meaning to the space people inhabit.

The axis mundi, or world axis, is an image that appears in cultures across the globe. It describes a point of contact between heaven and earth, a place through which the blessings of the higher realms can flow down to the lower ones, and through which messages from below can rise upward. All the directions of the compass seem to converge there, so the spot is often understood as the navel of the world and the origin of everything else. The Latin term first belonged to astronomy, where it named the axis of rotation of the celestial sphere. In twentieth-century comparative mythology, however, it was stretched to cover any idea that expresses this link between the high and the low. Mircea Eliade made it widely known in the 1950s.

The forms it takes are remarkably varied. It may be something natural, such as a mountain, a tree, a column of smoke or fire, a vine or a stalk, or something people have built, such as a tower, a ladder, a pillar, a cross, a steeple or a rope. The image is at once feminine and masculine, and it appears in religious and secular settings alike. It is found in shamanic and animist cultures, in the major world religions and in technologically advanced cities, where obelisks and skyscrapers partly carry the same urge to reach toward the sky.

One psychological explanation holds that the symbol grows out of a universal experience: the place a person occupies feels like the centre of the world. That familiar, settled space works as an ordered microcosm, surrounded by unfamiliar foreign lands that stand for chaos, death and night. From the centre, one can still set out in any of the four cardinal directions, make discoveries and found new centres. The name of China, meaning «Middle Kingdom», is often read as a sign of an old belief that the Chinese realm lay at the middle of the world, with other lands arranged around it.

A second, more philosophical reading starts from the idea that the macrocosm is mirrored in the microcosm. Human beings appear as a bridge between worlds: between the earthly and the heavenly, as in some Hindu and Taoist systems, or between three levels that include an underworld, as in the religions of ancient Greece, Egypt, and the Inca and Maya. The axis would then be the path along which one ascends or descends to other realities, and it often expresses human life as a quest for perfection.

Within the known world there is also a special place, the centre of the centre, usually a mountain or a raised site where heaven and earth seem closer. Mount Fuji has long symbolised the world axis in Japanese culture, and Mount Kunlun plays a similar role in China. Mount Kailash is holy to Hinduism and to several religions in Tibet, and Uluru is central to the world and culture of the Pitjantjatjara people of Australia. In Mesopotamia, the Sumerians and Babylonians built ziggurats to raise temples toward the gods, and in Mesoamerica great pyramids had staircases that seemed to lead up to the sky.

The cosmic tree is one of the most widespread images. It links three planes: branches in the sky, a trunk on earth and roots in the underworld. In Norse mythology the ash Yggdrasil fills this role, and it was there that Odin gained knowledge. The Mesoamerican world tree likewise connects the underworld, the earth and the sky. In Genesis, the Tree of Life and the Tree of the Knowledge of Good and Evil stand at the centre of the garden from which four rivers flow. In Yoruba religion, the oil palm is the axis along which Ọrunmila moves between heaven and earth.

The symbol is not tied to a single place. The ancient Greeks regarded several sites as the navel of the world, most notably the oracle at Delphi, while still believing in a cosmic tree and in Mount Olympus as the home of the gods. Judaism has the Temple Mount and Sinai, Christianity has the Mount of Olives and Calvary, and Islam has Mecca. In Christian tradition, Jacob's ladder is also an image of the world axis.

Shamanic traditions often tell of a healer who travels along the axis to bring back knowledge from another world. That pattern appears in stories as different as Odin and the ash tree, the Garden of Eden, Jacob's ladder and the tale of Jack and the Beanstalk. Dante used it in the Divine Comedy, where the poet descends to the depths of the earth and then climbs to paradise.

The image remains alive in modern art. The Endless Column by Constantin Brâncuși, made in 1938, is an abstract sculpture that can be read as a pillar holding up the sky, whose repeating segments invite the eye to climb and suggest the possibility of ascent.

Trivia

A term from astronomy. Before it spread into mythology, the Latin term named the axis of rotation of the celestial sphere in Greco-Roman astronomy.
A name about the centre. The name China is rendered as «Middle Kingdom» and is often read as an expression of the idea of a centre of the world.
A tree that marked an awakening. The bodhi tree is the one under which Siddhartha Gautama sat on the night he attained enlightenment, according to Buddhist tradition.
A shared mountain. Mount Kailash is holy to both Hinduism and several religions in Tibet, showing that one place can be a centre for different traditions.
