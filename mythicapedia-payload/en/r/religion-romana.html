Myth and religion

A web of cults, pacts and traditions

Religion in ancient Rome

Roman religion was less a body of doctrine than a set of cults, held together by pietas and by the principle of do ut des. It was lived in the home, in sacred groves and in public ceremonies, and it opened up to foreign gods as Rome expanded. Its myths about the city's origins blend Trojan, Greek and Latin heroes.

Ancient Roman religion did not rest on a book of doctrines. It was a matter of practice: prayers, rites, sacrifices and festivals that had to be carried out correctly. The Romans saw themselves as a deeply religious people, and they credited their rise to power to pietas, a collective sense of duty that kept them in good standing with the gods. The relationship was contractual, summed up in the phrase do ut des, 'I give so that you may give'. An offering was made in the hope of a favour, and if a deity failed to respond, the worshipper could stop honouring it.

According to tradition, most religious institutions went back to the city's founders. Ancient accounts say that Aeneas, son of Venus, carried the Palladium and the lares and penates from Troy to Italy. King Evander, a Greek exile from Arcadia, was said to have founded the Ara Maxima to Hercules and to have started the Lupercalia. This story was later joined to that of Romulus and Remus, twin sons of Mars, whose lives include divine intervention, consultation of the gods through augury, and a fratricide that became part of Rome's origin story. The sources do not agree on every detail of these genealogies, and modern historians find it hard to separate legend from history.

Within this tradition, Numa Pompilius, the second king, appears as the great organiser. He is credited with the calendar, with priesthoods such as the Salii, the flamines and the Vestals, and with the cults of Jupiter, Mars and Quirinus. According to legend, the doors of the Temple of Janus stood open in wartime and closed in peacetime. Roman historians believed that the essentials of Republican religion were complete by the end of his reign.

Daily religious life worked on several levels. In the home, the pater familias acted as priest. Near the household hearth, in the lararium, wine, milk or honey was poured out for the lares, the manes and the penates. There were also deities for each stage of life, such as Cunina, who watched over the cradle, or Abeona and Domiduca, who taught a child to walk and escorted them to and from school. Farmers and herders had their own gods. The dead were cremated, and their ashes placed in urns, while speeches praising the deceased were given.

Sacred groves, known as luci, came before temples as places of worship. On festival days people visited them, hung offerings on the branches and might share a meal there. Cutting down a tree was considered sacrilege, and the groves, like temples, could serve as places of refuge. The most important included the grove of Diana near Aricia, the groves of Egeria and the Muses along the Via Appia, and the grove of Vesta at the foot of the Palatine.

Public worship was a matter of state. Consuls held religious duties, and priests were officials of the cult who possessed no divine power of their own. The pontifex maximus stood at the head, responsible for the calendar and for recording memorable events. From Augustus onwards, every emperor held this title. Augurs read signs such as the entrails of animals or the flight of birds, a practice of Etruscan origin, and they could postpone political business by declaring the omens unfavourable. The fetiales kept treaties with other cities alive through oral tradition. The Vestal Virgins, who tended the sacred fire of Vesta, formed the most famous female priesthood. Their chastity was treated as a community concern, and a sexual relationship with a citizen was regarded as incest.

Rome also absorbed foreign deities through interpretatio graeca, identifying its gods with Greek ones and adapting their myths and imagery. Jupiter, Juno and Minerva formed the Capitoline Triad, and Mars was widely venerated during the Republic. Tolerance was generally the rule: foreign gods were folded in rather than eliminated. Mystery cults and secret gatherings, however, aroused suspicion, as with the Bacchanalia, which the Senate tried to curb in 186 BC. The hardest case was Judaism, whose strict monotheism clashed with the Roman habit of adding gods to the existing collection.

Under the Empire, the cult of the emperors became a political bond. Augustus justified one-man rule through a broad programme of religious renewal, and vows once made for the safety of the Republic now concerned the well-being of the emperor. Refusing the official cult could be read as treason. Christians were at first seen as atheists or as followers of a new superstition, until in 380 their faith became the official religion of the state.

Festivals set the rhythm of the calendar. Roughly forty annual celebrations were held, some lasting days and others a single day, and some included games such as chariot races and theatrical performances. Victory parades for generals were understood as the fulfilment of religious vows, although their political weight often overshadowed their religious meaning.

Trivia

Vestals under the flame. The Vestal Virgins tended the sacred fire of Vesta for centuries. They were disbanded once Christianity had come to dominate.
Gods of the cradle and the schoolroom. Cunina watched over babies in their cradles, while Abeona and Domiduca escorted children to and from school. Romans had a deity for almost every stage of life.
A ban in 186 BC. In 186 BC the Senate tried to restrict the Bacchanalia, gatherings with secret oaths that were seen as a threat to public order.
