The religion of the lwa in Haiti and its diaspora

Haitian Vodou

Haitian Vodou developed between the 16th and 19th centuries from the blending of West and Central African traditional religions with Catholicism, in a context shaped by slavery. Its practitioners, known as Vodouists, honour the lwa, spirits linked to African deities and often equated with Catholic saints. It is one of the world's most misunderstood religions.

Haitian Vodou, spelled Vodou in Haitian Creole, took shape on the island of Hispaniola, where enslaved Africans brought traditions from peoples such as the Fon and the Bakongo. In the French colony of Saint-Domingue, those beliefs mixed with Roman Catholic imagery and Freemasonic elements that reached the island through the colonists. The result was not a copy of either African religions or Christianity but a new, creolized tradition, which scholars describe in various ways: a neo-African religion, an African-derived tradition, or an Afro-Latin American religion.

Vodou has no central authority, no single leader, no formal creed and no fixed body of doctrine. Nor does it have a single liturgy. Practice differs between regions, communities and congregations, and between Haiti and its diaspora. It is practised at home, within family groups on their own land, and also in communal temples run by a priest or priestess. Many Haitians follow both Vodou and Catholicism at once and see no contradiction. Practitioners often describe themselves as devotees who serve the spirits.

At the centre of belief stands Bondye, a name derived from the French bon Dieu, or «good God». For Vodouists, Bondye is the ultimate source of power, the creator of the universe and the guarantor of cosmic order. Yet this God is seen as remote, uninvolved in daily human affairs, so people turn to intermediaries called lwa. There are over a thousand of them, each with a distinct personality and with associated colours, days of the week and objects. They reach people through dreams or through possession during ceremonies, where drums, singing and dancing continue until a spirit descends on a dancer. Offerings include fruit, liquor and sacrificed animals, and healing rituals and herbal remedies play a prominent part.

The lwa are grouped into «nations». The two largest are the Rada and the Petwo. The Rada, rooted mainly in Dahomeyan and Yoruba influences, are described as «cool» and generally benevolent and righteous. The Petwo, with a stronger Kongo background, are called «hot»: more forceful, sometimes morally ambiguous, and linked to matters such as money. In Rada ceremonies the first to be saluted is usually Papa Legba, guardian of gates and crossroads, while Petwo rites usually begin with Mèt Kalfou. The sacred twins, the marasa, have a place of their own because twins are believed to hold special powers. Other popular lwa include Agwe, lord of the sea who protects ships and fishermen; Ezili Freda, goddess of love and luxury; Ogou, a warrior associated with weapons; and Danbala, a serpent linked to water and springs. The gede, headed by Baron Samedi, are tied to the realm of the dead and, according to tradition, bring humour and mischief to ceremonies.

In the diaspora Vodou also became a space of religious translation. Many lwa are identified with Catholic saints by analogy of function. Papa Legba is paired with Saint Peter, who holds the keys; Ezili Freda with Our Lady of Sorrows; the marasa with the twin saints Cosmas and Damian; and Danbala with Saint Patrick or with Moses, because of the serpents associated with them. Scholars note that this equivalence may originally have helped conceal worship when Catholicism was imposed by the colonists, although many Vodouists today hold these devotions sincerely.

Vodou also has a place in Haitian history. The ceremony led by the priest Boukman on 14 August 1791 at Bois-Caïman is often regarded as one of the precursors of the revolution that, between 1791 and 1801, overthrew the French colonial government, abolished slavery and gave rise to the Haitian republic. Later, the Duvalier government made Vodou a national symbol and used it to reinforce its political power, an example of how a religious tradition can be turned to propaganda. In the following decades, movements inspired by Négritude led some practitioners to try to remove Catholic influences from their practice.

Much of the popular image of Vodou comes from literature and film rather than from the religion itself. The zombie, a person supposedly revived by a bokor to be made to work, and the voodoo dolls used in curses have become familiar clichés. Scholars point out that these ideas have distorted and demonised practices that, for their followers, form part of an ethic of service and respect toward the spirits.

Finally, Vodou's view of the person distinguishes two parts of the soul: the ti bon ange, or «little good angel», which is linked to conscience and self-criticism, and the gros bon ange, or «big good angel», which holds memory, intelligence and personhood. At death, the soul is said to join the Ginen, the ancestral spirits, while the person faces judgement before Bondye. This idea of judgement is more common in cities than in the mountains, where people often believe the soul dissolves into the navel of the earth nine days after death.

Trivia

A name with history. The word vodou comes from Fon, where it means «spirit». It was already recorded in the 1658 Doctrina Christiana.
Twins with powers. The marasa, or sacred twins, receive special attention because twins are believed to have particular powers. Every lwa has its own marasa.
Saints and spirits. Danbala is sometimes identified with Saint Patrick, who is linked to snakes, and sometimes with Moses, whose staff becomes a serpent in the Book of Exodus.
