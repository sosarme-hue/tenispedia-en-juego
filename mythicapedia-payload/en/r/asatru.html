Myth and religion

The faith of the Æsir and the modern reconstruction of Germanic paganism

Asatru

Asatru, a name meaning roughly loyalty to the Æsir, is one of the modern Heathen religions that seek to revive the pre-Christian beliefs of the Germanic peoples. Its practice rests mainly on medieval Scandinavian texts such as the Eddas, and several European countries officially recognise it. Its link to myth is direct, yet the religion as practised today is a modern reconstruction rather than an unbroken survival.

Asatru, which translates as loyalty to the Æsir, is one of the contemporary forms of Heathenry, the umbrella term for religions that try to revive the pagan faiths of the Germanic peoples of central and northern Europe. Its followers also use names such as forn sed (the old custom), vor sed (our custom) or Odinism, while close relatives include Theodism, rooted in Anglo-Saxon sources, and Vanatru, which centres on the Vanir. Iceland recognised the religion officially in 1973, Norway in 1994, Denmark in 2003, and Sweden and Spain in 2007.

The roots of the belief system reach back to the Stone Age in Scandinavia, the Baltic and northern Germany, although the beliefs of that period did not differ greatly from those found elsewhere in Europe. It was during the Nordic Bronze Age that the Germanic peoples began to set themselves apart from their neighbours. The Roman writer Tacitus described some of their customs, but he framed them in terms drawn from Roman religion. As many Germanic groups converted to Christianity during the migration period, the Scandinavians, far from the empire, kept their faith for centuries. The Arab traveller Ibn Fadlan recorded some practices among the Rus, and once Scandinavian kings converted, the old religion declined, sometimes through persecution.

The word Ásatrú was coined in the nationalist Romanticism of the nineteenth century, and Edvard Grieg used it for the first time in his 1870 opera Olaf Trygvason. The modern practice should therefore be understood as a reconstruction. The Old Norse material is the most fully preserved, thanks to the Prose and Poetic Eddas and the Voluspa, whereas evidence for the Anglo-Saxon, Lombard or Visigothic traditions is scarce and reaches us through Christian authors. Many practitioners treat these sources as a source of inspiration rather than a fixed rulebook, and they accept a good deal of personal interpretation.

The religion is generally described as non-dogmatic, so personal practice differs widely. Among the gods, Thor owns the hammer Mjolnir, which the dwarves Sindri and Brokkr forged. Odin, the one-eyed father of the Æsir, gave an eye to the giant Mimir in exchange for wisdom, and according to the myth he hung for nine days and nights to win the runes. Njord, father of the Vanir and lord of the sea, sits beside Freyr and Freyja, who are linked to fertility, while Frigg is Odin's wife. Fenrir, the giant wolf, is Loki's son. Tyr, Baldr, Heimdallr, Sif, Bragi and Skadi are also honoured.

The central ceremony is the blot. In earlier times it was a communal feast with offerings and libations. Modern blots are often held four times a year, with particular attention to the solstices, and usually include a shared meal and a ritual toast. A typical rite begins by consecrating the space with a hammer, after which a horn or cup is passed around the circle for three toasts: one to the gods, one to the elves and dises, and one to the ancestors. Only the person holding the vessel may address the deities, while the others wait their turn.

According to the Edda, Odin, Lodur and Hoenir found two trees, Ask and Embla, and gave them breath, movement, health and soul. From them came the first man and the first woman. Beliefs also include the fylgja, a family guardian spirit that often takes an animal shape, and the hamingja, an inner strength that protects against harm. Many communities hold a naming ceremony for newborn children, through which the child is given a name and recognised as a member of the family.

The nine noble virtues are the best known moral framework: courage, truth, honour, fidelity, discipline, hospitality, industriousness, trust and perseverance. They were set out in the 1970s by John Yeowell and John Gibbs-Bailey of the British group Odinic Rite, who drew on texts such as the Havamal and the Icelandic sagas, and their historical validity is disputed. Sacred places are usually natural ones, including groves, rivers and lakes, though stone mounds, enclosures marked with hazel staves and communal halls are also used.

Internal currents differ considerably. Traditionalists treat the sources as a guide and welcome people of any origin. Folkish Heathens, by contrast, hold that the faith belongs to the descendants of the Germanic peoples, and some of their groups have links to far-right and white supremacist politics. It is worth remembering that völkisch groups in Germany and Austria venerated these deities in the early twentieth century, and that the Nazis used Norse myth as propaganda and as a tool of exclusion and violence. This history helps explain the suspicion the movement still meets today.

Scholars estimate that there are no more than around twenty thousand Heathens worldwide, although official counts differ. Iceland registered 2,173 practitioners, a high share for a country of roughly 300,000 people. In a few countries, openly Asatru or Odinist politicians have won public office, among them Sigurjon Thordarson, who sat in the Icelandic parliament, and Dan Halloran, elected to the New York City Council.
