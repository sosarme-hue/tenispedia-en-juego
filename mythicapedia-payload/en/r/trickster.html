The trickster figure in world myth

Trickster

The trickster is a character who combines great cleverness, sometimes with secret knowledge, with a habit of playing pranks, deceiving others and breaking the rules. Found in the myths of many cultures, from Hermes to the Coyote of Native American traditions, the figure has lived on in literature, Jungian psychology and online culture.

A trickster is a character in a story who shows great intelligence or secret knowledge and uses it to play tricks, deceive others or break the normal rules and conventional behaviour. The figure may be a god, a goddess, a spirit, a person or an animal with human traits. Scholars sometimes call it the divine rogue. Lewis Hyde described the trickster as a boundary-crosser, someone who moves across physical and social lines and often breaks them.

What sets the trickster apart is that it upsets the order of things and then restores that order on a new footing. Its transgressions often take the form of scams, pranks or theft. The trickster may be clever, foolish or both, and it has no trouble questioning, disrupting or mocking authority. Because it is an archetype, it turns up in the myths of very different peoples, and it often changes shape or even gender from one tale to another.

In Greek myth, Hermes plays the trickster. According to the stories, he is the patron of thieves and the inventor of lying, a gift he passed on to Autolycus, who in turn gave it to Odysseus. In Slavic folktales, the trickster and the culture hero are often one and the same. In Norse myth, the troublemaker is Loki, a shapeshifter. In The Song of Hyndla, from the Poetic Edda, Loki turns into a mare and gives birth to Sleipnir, Odin's eight-legged horse, a story that shows how the figure slips past the boundaries of sex and body.

In many African-language communities the hare or the rabbit is the protagonist of these tales. In West Africa the spider Anansi is the classic trickster, and the figure travelled to the Caribbean with the slave trade. In southern Africa, the being known as Kaggen often takes the form of a praying mantis. In African-American folklore the main figure is Brer Rabbit.

In Native American traditions the trickster has traits of its own that set it apart from the picaro of European fiction. Many native peoples held that clowns and tricksters were essential to any contact with the sacred, since laughter opens the mind and frees it from rigid ideas. The trickster can be wise in one story and foolish in another, a hero in one tale and a villain in the next. Coyote, in the southwestern United States, and Raven, in the Pacific Northwest, appear in many myths as thieves who stole fire from the gods, and in some stories they teach people how to catch salmon.

Coyote shows the ambiguity especially well. According to Crow tradition, Old Man Coyote poses as the Creator, takes a handful of mud and makes people from it. For A. Hultkrantz, this impersonation works as a mythic substitute for a Great Spirit whose name was considered too sacred to use outside special ceremonies. In other stories Coyote is a noble trickster who takes water from the Frog people because it is not right for one people to have all of it, while in others he is malicious and harms Duck. In Wasco tradition, he defeats Thunderbird, the killer of people, with the help of the Spirit Chief.

In analytical psychology, Carl Gustav Jung devoted his 1954 work On the Psychology of the Trickster Figure to the character. For Jung, the trickster represents an undifferentiated psyche that has barely left the animal level, and in ordinary people it appears as tendencies in the unconscious that surface whenever we feel that setbacks are chasing us with malice. Jung called this the shadow. In his view the trickster myth survives because of its therapeutic effect, reminding the developed consciousness of a past it has not fully left behind.

In modern literature the trickster survives as a character archetype, not necessarily supernatural. It often acts as a catalyst: its antics cause trouble for others, yet it remains untouched. Puck in Shakespeare's plays is a clear example. Many European fairy tales also tell of a humble peasant who, through wit, passes the trials a king sets for his daughter's suitors where proud knights fail. Bugs Bunny and the Cheshire Cat are more recent heirs of the same tradition.

In digital culture, the trickster has been linked to internet trolling. The anthropologist James Cuffe proposed the Chinese internet character Grass Mud Horse, whose name sounds like a rude phrase, as a trickster candidate because of its double meaning. According to Cuffe, the character allows people to skirt censorship while commenting on the experience of living under it, offering new ways to reassess one's own experience against that of one's community. In this way the trickster continues to help us think about limits.
