The hero's journey in world storytelling

Monomyth

The monomyth describes a narrative pattern in which a hero leaves the familiar world, faces the unknown and returns changed, bringing a gift for others. Joseph Campbell laid it out in 1949 and compared it with stories from very different cultures. Its reach is still debated, but it remains a useful lens for reading myths.

The monomyth, also called the hero's journey or hero's quest, is the template Joseph Campbell set out in The Hero with a Thousand Faces (1949). The American mythologist described a familiar shape: a hero leaves the ordinary world for a realm of supernatural wonder, wins a decisive victory there and comes home with a gift that can benefit others. Campbell borrowed the word from Finnegans Wake, the James Joyce novel he admired, and later co-wrote a study of that book.

The idea had earlier roots. In 1871 the anthropologist Edward Burnett Tylor noted recurring features in the plots of heroes, and in 1876 the German philologist Johann Georg von Hahn listed motifs that turned up in Indo-European hero tales. In the twentieth century, Otto Rank (1909) and Lord Raglan (1936) drew up their own lists of traits, both reading them through Freudian psychoanalysis and ritualism. Campbell, shaped by Carl Jung's analytical psychology, gave the model its best-known form and applied it to comparing religions as well as stories.

Campbell lists seventeen stages, though few myths contain every one. Some stories dwell on only a handful, and others arrange them in a different order. To make them easier to follow, they are often grouped into three parts: departure, initiation and return.

In the departure, the hero lives an ordinary life until news or an event breaks in and calls him toward the unknown. That other world might be a forest, an underground kingdom, the depths of the sea, the sky or a secret island, always a place of shifting beings, unbearable torments and impossible delights. Some heroes set out of their own accord, like Theseus hearing about the Minotaur in Athens. Others are swept away, like Odysseus, driven across the Mediterranean by the winds of Poseidon. Often, though, the hero first refuses. Campbell reads this refusal as a way of getting stuck in routine or fear, and he warns that a life built on it ends up as a maze.

A guide or protective figure usually appears, offering a talisman or advice. Crossing the first threshold, the hero meets a guardian who marks the edge of the known. Campbell describes the next move as a kind of symbolic death: the hero is swallowed by the unknown, as the biblical Jonah was by a great fish, and is transformed there before being reborn. He compares this to a worshipper entering a temple, guarded by colossal stone figures.

Initiation is built around trials, which often come in threes and in which the hero can fail. The supernatural aid he met at the start sustains him, and along the way he wins partial victories. The climax, in Campbell's account, is the meeting with the goddess, a love that feels almost absolute and that he presents as a mystical marriage. If the traveller is a woman, she may be the one who finds a celestial partner. Before reaching that goal the hero must resist temptation, often physical or material, which Campbell links to the figure of woman as tempter. The central moment is reconciliation with the father, the one who holds power over life and death. In fuller versions, the steps that follow include apotheosis and the winning of a reward or elixir, which point toward the return.

In the return, the hero goes back to the ordinary world with his prize. He may be chased by the guardians of the other realm, or need outside help to get home. He crosses the threshold again and, if he succeeds, uses what he has learned for the good of others. He is changed by the journey and gains a wisdom that lets him move between both worlds.

The model has drawn criticism. Some folklorists have dismissed it as unscholarly, pointing to bias in the sources selected. More recent analysis treats it as an example of the sympathetic plot, a common narrative structure in which a goal-driven protagonist meets obstacles, overcomes them and is rewarded. Its appeal lies in that simplicity, though it is worth remembering that the pattern was identified and named by Campbell and earlier theorists, and is not a fixed law followed by every myth.

Trivia

A borrowed word. Campbell took the term «monomyth» from Finnegans Wake. Years later, in 1944, he co-wrote A Skeleton Key to Finnegans Wake, an analysis of that novel.
Jonah in the belly. The Book of Jonah is cited as an example of the belly of the whale. After three days Jonah is vomited onto shore, a scene some read as symbolic death and rebirth in Jungian terms.
From myth to cinema. George Lucas has said that Star Wars was deliberately structured with this archetype in mind. In The Power of Myth, Campbell agrees with Bill Moyers that the trash-compactor scene is a strong example.
Ivanov's monomyth. The scholar Omry Ronen called Vyacheslav Ivanov's 1904 reading of Dionysus as an «avatar of Christ» «Ivanov's monomyth».
