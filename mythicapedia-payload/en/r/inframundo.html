Myth and religion

The world of the dead in religious traditions and myths

Underworld

The underworld is the supernatural world of the dead in many religious traditions and myths, usually placed below the world of the living. Its image changes from culture to culture, but it shares common features: journeys made by living heroes, rites needed to cross over, and figures who rule or guard it.

The underworld, also known as the netherworld or hell, is a general term for the place where the souls of the dead go in many religious traditions and myths. In more secular language it is sometimes called the Other World, a phrase that refers to any dwelling of the dead without specifying its form. The technical adjective for anything belonging to this realm is chthonic. The idea appears in almost every civilization and, according to some scholars, may be as old as humanity itself. Although each culture imagines it in its own way, the most widespread conception places it in the depths of the Earth, beneath the world of the living.

This underground location has also shaped imagery. In many works of art the underworld appears as a place of fire and lava, a picture that recurs in paintings, engravings and visual narratives from very different periods. That image is not universal, however. In other traditions the realm of the dead is described as a shadowy region or a land of shades rather than a burning place. It is common for the underworld to be ruled by a personification of death, a deity who presides over the space and over the boundary between the two worlds.

One of the most frequent features of these myths is the journey of living people into the underworld, usually for some heroic purpose: to bring someone back, to gain knowledge or to overcome a trial. In many stories, the heroes who go down there return changed by what they have seen. Other myths reinforce the belief that the souls of the dead can enter the underworld only after the proper ceremonies have been performed. A well known example comes from ancient Greek tradition, where the recently dead Patroclus haunts Achilles until his body receives a proper burial. Along similar lines, people of high social status were often dressed and equipped with objects so that they could find their way and survive in the afterlife, as if the journey required its own gear.

Another family of myths follows the soul of the deceased on its own journey. In these stories the dead must be carried across a defining obstacle, such as a lake or a river, to reach their destination. Images of such crossings are among the oldest and most persistent in human art, appearing in ancient works as well as modern ones, where they still serve to represent the passage from one state of being to another. The descent to the underworld has also had a remarkable life in literature. Some scholars have described it as the single most important myth for Modernist authors, who returned to it again and again in their writing.

Religious traditions include a great variety of underworlds, each with its own names, geographies and rules, and numerous figures rule or guard these spaces, from sovereigns of the realm of the dead to guardians who watch over its entrances. For religions that are still practised today, these beliefs are part of the faith of their followers and of how they understand life and death, so they are best described as they are lived. In the end, studying the underworld means studying how each society imagines what happens after death and what role it assigns to the living in that process.

Trivia

A technical adjective. Scholars of mythology and religion use the adjective chthonic for anything that belongs to the underworld.
Fire and lava. The picture of the underworld as a place of fire and lava is typical of art, although it does not appear in every tradition.
Patroclus and Achilles. In the Greek story, the dead Patroclus appears to Achilles and gives him no rest until his body is properly buried.
A myth for the Modernists. Some scholars have described the descent to the underworld as the single most important myth for Modernist authors.
