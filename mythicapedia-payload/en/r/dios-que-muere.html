Gods of life, death and rebirth in mythology

Dying-and-rising deity

Many myths tell of gods who die and return to life, or who vanish and come back. James Frazer turned this motif into a category in the late nineteenth century, and scholars have argued over it ever since. Today a sharper distinction is drawn between gods who truly rise again and those who only die or depart.

A dying-and-rising god is a figure in myth who dies and then returns to existence. The most cited examples come from the ancient Near East and from the traditions that drew on it, including Greco-Roman mythology. The idea entered comparative mythology through James Frazer's The Golden Bough, first published in 1890. Frazer linked the motif to fertility rites tied to the yearly cycle of vegetation, and he grouped Osiris, Tammuz, Adonis, Attis, Zagreus, Dionysus and Jesus under the same heading.

Later scholarship refined that picture. Many of the figures Frazer filed under "dying and rising" only die and never come back. By the end of the twentieth century, the prevailing view was that a god who genuinely returns to life is a distinctive feature of the ancient Near Eastern myths and of the mystery cults of late antiquity. Folklorists had already drawn a related line in Stith Thompson's Motif-Index of Folk-Literature (1932), where motif A192 covers the death or departure of gods and motif A193 covers their resurrection.

Deaths of divine beings turn up in many cultures, perhaps because the traits of gods were shaped by everyday experience, and the conflicts that surrounded them often ended in death. Balder in Norse myth, the feathered serpent Quetzalcoatl in Aztec tradition and the Japanese Izanami all belong to this pattern. What differs is how they die and what follows.

Balder's story, probably first written down in the twelfth century, ends when his blind brother Höðr, tricked into it, fires a mistletoe-tipped arrow at him by accident. Balder's body is set alight on a ship that drifts out to sea, and he does not return, because not every living thing weeps for him. His death sets off the doom of the gods. In most versions of Quetzalcoatl's story, recorded in the sixteenth century, Tezcatlipoca tricks him into drinking too much, and, ashamed of his own wrongdoing, he burns himself to death. Some accounts say a flock of birds flew up from his ashes, but he does not come back as himself. In other variants he sails away over the ocean and never returns.

Hawaiian deities can also die and leave the world in several ways. Some gods killed on Lanai by Lanikuala departed for the skies, whereas Kaili leaves by canoe and is never seen again. In the Japanese account, Izanami dies giving birth to Kagu-tsuchi, the fire deity. Izanagi journeys to Yomi, the land of gloom, to bring her back, but she has already decayed, and he cannot restore her. She pursues him, and he manages to escape.

Some traditions tie the seasonal round of life and death to gods who themselves pass through a cycle of death and renewal. Such figures act as vegetation deities. Ishtar and Persephone are two who die each year. Ishtar's descent underground stands for the barren season, and her return for the renewal of the farming year. Still, most scholars hold that the gods of this motif die without generally returning as the same deity, though Tryggve Mettinger argues that in some cases they do.

The concept took new shape in the twentieth century. Early on, Gerald Massey argued for parallels between Egyptian dying-and-rising myths and Jesus, but his factual mistakes undermine much of his work. Carl Jung read death and rebirth as archetypes of the collective unconscious, useful for psychological integration, and set aside the question of whether the historical events were true. Jung gave special attention to Osiris. In 1950 he wrote that those who take part in the Osiris myth experience a continuity of life that outlasts every change of form. He held that rebirth applied to the father, Osiris, and not to his son Horus. These ideas have drawn criticism, because the link between Osiris and agriculture rests on rituals tied to the Nile floods and the harvest, and agricultural analogies are generally seen as the weakest part of the comparison.

In Greek mythology, Dionysus, son of Zeus, was a horned child whom the Titans lured with toys before tearing him apart, boiling him and eating him. Zeus struck down the Titans with a thunderbolt, and according to the story humans were formed from their ashes. His grandmother Rhea pieced some of him back together, principally his spared heart, and brought him back to life. In Orphic tales Zagreus is the son of Hades and Persephone and a god of rebirth. The scholar Barry Powell has proposed Dionysus as an example of resurrection.

The oldest known example of the dying-and-rising myth is the Sumerian story of Inanna's Descent to the Underworld. The goddess travels below to visit her sister Ereshkigal, is struck down there and becomes a corpse. She stays dead for three days and three nights until her father Enki sends two galla to bring her back. The galla offer her food and water, and she returns to the world.

During the twentieth century most scholars questioned how universal the motif really was. Kurt Rudolph argued in 1986 that the common link between the mystery religions and dying and rising divinities is faulty. Gerald O'Collins calls the habit of stretching surface resemblances "parallelomania". Against this, Mettinger (2001) holds that many gods of the mystery cults do die, descend to the underworld, are mourned and retrieved by a woman, and restored to life. Roland de Vaux attacked the category in 1933, and Jonathan Z. Smith, in his 1987 entry for Mircea Eliade's Encyclopedia of Religion, called it largely a misnomer built on imaginative reconstructions and late or ambiguous texts. Smith proposed separating gods who die and stay dead from gods who disappear and return. His view won wide acceptance in the 1990s, although the term kept being used in studies of the ancient Near East.

Marcel Detienne, in The Gardens of Adonis, argues that the herbs grown and withered at the Athenian festival do not simply stand for crops in general. He links them instead to a cluster of associations around spices, including seduction, trickery, gluttony and the dangers of childbirth. For him, Adonis's death is only one fact to be weighed. Another objection is that the category lumps together very different myths and places death and resurrection at the centre, so that Christianity becomes the yardstick for all religion. Dag Øistein Endsjø notes that several figures in Greek religion often cited as dying-and-rising gods died as ordinary mortals and became gods only afterwards, so they do not fit the definition.

Today the Encyclopedia of Psychology and Religion describes the debate as unsettled. Some scholars reject Frazer's broad universal category and stress the differences between traditions, while others still find it useful. Paola Corrente, in an extensive survey from the 2010s and 2020s, accepts that much of Frazer's evidence was faulty but defends the category with revised criteria. She focuses on Mesopotamian and Near Eastern figures such as Inanna in Sumerian texts and Baal in the Ugaritic texts, whose myths she sees as clear cases of death and resurrection. On Dionysus she judges the connection more complicated and argues that it has often been overlooked or misread.

Trivia

A title by Frazer. The Golden Bough first appeared in 1890 and grew through several editions into a multi-volume work.
A catalogued motif. Stith Thompson's 1932 index lists the death of gods as motif A192 and their resurrection as A193.
Ashes and birds. In some versions of Quetzalcoatl's story, a flock of birds rises from his ashes, though he does not return as himself.
Three days dead. In the Sumerian story, Inanna stays dead for three days and three nights before being restored to life.
