The theory that looks for real people behind the gods

Euhemerism

Euhemerism, sometimes spelled evemerism, proposes that many mythological gods began as real people, such as rulers, founders or heroes, whose deeds grew in the retelling until they became divine. The approach takes its name from Euhemerus, the author of the lost work Sacred Inscription. Known mostly through summaries, his ideas shaped thinkers from antiquity to the modern era.

Euhemerism is a way of reading myths built on a simple premise: behind many stories about gods there may have been real people, kings, founders or heroes of a distant past, whose deeds were exaggerated as they were retold until they became divine. The name comes from Euhemerus, a Greek writer of the late fourth century BC. His main work, Hierá anagraphé (Sacred Inscription), has been lost, and we know it chiefly through summaries, especially those preserved by Diodorus of Sicily.

According to those summaries, Euhemerus described an imaginary island called Panchaea, where sacred inscriptions recorded the lives of men who had benefited their people. After death, these kings or inventors were said to have been worshipped as gods. For him, the hidden meaning of myths was historical and social: remembering the deification of ancestors preserved models of good conduct. Centuries later, Plutarch attacked this foundation, since the island did not exist and so the inscriptions could not be checked.

Euhemerus was not the first to think this way. The sophist Prodicus of Ceos had argued that the gods were things or people of importance in the past who were later deified. Other authors, including Xenophanes, Herodotus and Hecataeus of Abdera, also wrote in related terms, according to the sources. Even in Plato's Phaedrus, Socrates offers a rationalising reading of the abduction of Oreithyia by Boreas: the north wind, he suggests, blew the girl off the rocks. Yet Socrates also doubts that every myth can be explained this way, since creatures such as centaurs or the Chimera do not fit the scheme easily.

The influence of Euhemerus lasted a long time. The Roman poet Ennius was among the later writers who followed his ideas. Cicero, in On the Nature of the Gods and On Divination, combined allegorical interpretation with euhemerism, treating deified men as gods. Much later, Voltaire wrote Dialogues with Euhemerus, and David Hume also accepted the theory.

The idea of humans being deified was not unusual in the ancient world. In many cultures, rulers were honoured as divine, sometimes even while still alive. The tyrant Dion of Syracuse was deified during his lifetime, and modern scholars think his case may have influenced Euhemerus's thinking. Euhemerus also lived through the deification of the Seleucid rulers and the Egyptian-style presentation of the Ptolemies, a blend of Greek and Egyptian traditions.

The tomb of Zeus shows how the argument worked. Euhemerus claimed that Zeus had been a mortal king who died on Crete, where his tomb, bearing his name, could still be seen. The claim was not new, however. Epimenides of Knossos, around 600 BC, already reported the belief that Zeus lay buried somewhere on Crete, and for that reason the Cretans were called liars. Callimachus, an opponent of Euhemerus, argued that the tomb was invented and that Zeus was eternal. A later Latin commentary explained that the original inscription read 'the tomb of Minos, son of Jupiter', and that over time the words 'Minos, the son' wore away. Porphyry, influenced by Euhemerus, claimed that Pythagoras had found the tomb and inscribed it.

Early Christians found the method useful. Hostile to paganism, the Church Fathers used euhemerism to undermine the pagan gods, though they did not apply it to their own beliefs. Clement of Alexandria wrote that those whom pagans bowed to had once been men like themselves. Cyprian, a North African convert, took the argument for granted in his short essay On the Vanity of Idols, written in 247 AD. Eusebius applied it to the Babylonian god Baal and to Belus, and Isidore of Seville, in his Etymologies, devoted a chapter to deified men and elaborate genealogies of gods.

In the Prose Edda, composed around 1220, the Icelandic writer Snorri Sturluson presents Odin as a historical leader who came from Asia Minor, tracing his ancestry back to Priam, king of Troy. According to this account, Odin travelled north and founded the royal houses of Denmark, Sweden and Norway. Snorri thus continued the euhemerist tradition of the early Christians.

In the modern period the method remained alive. In 1711 Antoine Banier published The Mythology and Fables of the Ancients, Explained, which argued strongly for a euhemerist reading of Greek myth. Jacob Bryant in 1744 and George Stanley Faber in 1816 applied the approach on a Biblical basis. The archaeologist Heinrich Schliemann excavated Troy and claimed to have found objects linked to figures such as Agamemnon and Priam. In the twentieth century, Robert Graves offered euhemerist readings of Greek myths, but most scholars reject them. Richard Carrier has used the term 'euhemerization' for the reverse process, in which a cosmic god is placed in history as a real person, which turns the usual sense of euhemerism on its head.

Trivia

An island that does not exist. Plutarch criticised Euhemerus because his claims rested on inscriptions from the island of Panchaea, a place that, in Plutarch's view, did not exist.
Liars over a tomb. Epimenides of Knossos called all Cretans liars because they claimed that Zeus was buried on their island.
Troy and Priam. Heinrich Schliemann excavated Troy and claimed to have found objects linked to figures from the Homeric poems, such as Agamemnon and Priam.
