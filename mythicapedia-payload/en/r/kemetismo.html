Myth and religion

The modern religion that revives the gods of ancient Egypt

Kemetism

Kemetism is a family of neopagan religions that emerged in the late twentieth century and seek to revive the beliefs and practices of ancient Egypt. Its name comes from Kemet, the "black land" by which the Egyptians called their country. Its groups range from strict reconstruction to eclectic blends with other traditions.

The movement takes its name from Kemet, an Egyptian word meaning "black land". It referred to the dark silt that the Nile left after its annual floods, in contrast to the surrounding desert, the "red land". Followers, known as Kemetics, call the gods netjeru, the Egyptian term for divinities, and prefer the conventional Egyptological spelling of ancient names, speaking of Heru rather than Horus or Anpu rather than Anubis.

Kemetism is not an ancient religion that has survived unbroken, but a modern movement. Some practitioners claim a direct line of continuity with secret societies supposedly active since the ban on paganism by Theodosius I in 392 CE, or since the closure of the last working temple of Isis on the island of Philae, traditionally dated to around 535. Such claims cannot be proven historically. What can be documented is the long influence of Egyptian religion on other traditions. Hermeticism rests on Hermes Trismegistus, a figure combining the Greek god Hermes with the Egyptian Thoth, and early and medieval Christianity absorbed legends about Jesus's stay in Egypt and myths associated with the goddess Isis.

The key difference lies in approach. Esoteric currents and Hermeticism usually read the Egyptian gods symbolically. Kemetic groups, by contrast, aim at a more or less rigorous restoration of the religion in its historical form, though most accept that an exact copy of ancient practice is not always possible or even desirable. The starting problem is that Egyptian religion changed over three thousand years and varied from place to place: one god could have different myths and forms of worship in each nome or even each temple. A single, original form is therefore largely a reconstruction.

A central principle is maat, usually translated as order, truth or justice. In ancient Egypt it was the foundation of the world's stability, and the pharaoh, regarded as divine, had the main duty of sustaining it. The goddess Maat and her emblem, the ostrich feather, embodied the idea. Each person contributed by keeping the rules, and breaking them opened the way to chaos. Kemetics try to live by maat through ethical ideas and good manners, yet no binding text sets out a fixed moral code.

Practice centres on worship: prayer, offerings and altars. The altar usually holds a statue or flat image of one or more deities, surrounded by candles, votive gifts, prayer beads, incense burners and dishes for food. Many groups try to offer what ancient Egyptians offered. Commonly honoured gods include Ra, Amun, Isis and Osiris, Thoth, Sekhmet, Bastet and Hathor.

The movement has several branches. The most influential is the House of Netjer, known as Kemetic Orthodoxy, founded in Chicago in 1988 by Tamara Siuda. It seeks worship as close to the Egyptian tradition as possible, its leader takes some titles and attributes of the old pharaohs, and it moves toward monolatry, the worship of one god without denying others. Other branches include Kemetic Wicca, which combines a Wiccan structure with Egyptian elements, the Temple of the Eternal Source, founded in the United States in 1975, Neo-Atonism, inspired by the monotheistic reform of the pharaoh Akhenaten, and the Temple of Set, an initiatory school focused almost entirely on the god Set and founded by Michael Aquino in the 1970s.

Some groups take a looser path. The Fellowship of Isis, formed in Ireland, centres on the cult of Isis adapted to Greek and Roman settings and blended with other traditions. The Ausar Auset Society, founded in 1973 by Ra Un Nefer Amen and based in Brooklyn, uses Egyptian aesthetics as the basis of a Pan-African spirituality. The group has been criticised for using the Egyptian past to support ideas of racial superiority, an issue that generates debate inside and outside the movement. Kemetism thus shows how an ancient tradition can be reinterpreted today from very different perspectives.

Trivia

Black land. The dark colour of Kemet came from the silt the Nile deposited during its yearly floods, making the name a direct reference to fertility.
A feather as symbol. The goddess Maat was shown with an ostrich feather, which served as the moral measure in the judgment of the dead.
Hermes and Thoth. Hermes Trismegistus joins the Greek god Hermes with the Egyptian Thoth, an early example of blending between Mediterranean religions.
