The Gaelic festival that opened the summer

Beltane

Beltane is the great Gaelic festival of 1 May, marking the start of the summer pasture season with bonfires, protective rituals and feasting. Its name appears in the oldest Irish literature, where it is linked to episodes of Irish myth. Today it survives in local customs, returns as a cultural festival and is celebrated by neopagans and Wiccans.

Beltane, also spelled Bealtaine or Beltain, is the Gaelic May Day festival, the day that marked the arrival of summer. It was traditionally held on 1 May, roughly halfway between the spring equinox and the summer solstice, and was widely observed in Ireland, Scotland and the Isle of Man. Together with Samhain, Imbolc and Lughnasadh, it formed one of the four great seasonal festivals of the Gaelic world, and it has close relatives in other Celtic lands, such as the Welsh celebrations held on the same day. The name is already found in the earliest Irish literature, which links the festival to important episodes of Irish mythology.

The name is old, and its exact meaning is not entirely clear. In modern Irish the day is called Lá Bealtaine, and the month of May is Mí na Bealtaine, the month of Beltane. In Scottish Gaelic the phrase Lá Buidhe Bealtainn, the bright or yellow day of Beltane, was used for the first of May. Another old name, probably meaning something like first of summer, survives in the Scottish Gaelic name for May and matches related words in other Celtic languages. Two modern etymologies have been proposed for Beltane, and neither is certain.

According to the traditions recorded, the day was built around rituals meant to protect cattle, people and crops, and to encourage growth. Special bonfires were lit, and their flames, smoke and ashes were believed to have protective power. People and livestock walked around or between the fires, and sometimes leapt over the flames or the embers. Household fires were put out beforehand and then relit from the communal bonfire, a gesture that tied each home to the wider community. The gatherings were followed by a feast, and some of the food and drink was set aside as an offering to the fairy folk.

Doors, windows, byres and livestock were decorated with yellow May flowers, perhaps because they recalled fire. In parts of Ireland people made a May Bush, usually a hawthorn or a branch dressed with flowers, ribbons, bright shells and small rushlights. Holy wells were also visited. People walked sunwise around the well, prayed for health and left offerings such as coins or strips of cloth tied to nearby trees. The first water drawn from a well on Beltane was thought especially powerful, and the morning dew was believed to bring beauty and youth, so young women rolled in the wet grass or washed their faces with it at dawn.

Popular belief added many rules of its own. Nobody should light a fire on May morning until they saw smoke rising from a neighbour's house. It was unlucky to hand out ashes or clothing on that day, and giving away coal or ash was thought to bring trouble in lighting fires the following year. A birth or a wedding on 1 May was generally considered ill-fated. On the night before, a cake and a jug were left on the table, because it was believed that Irish people who had died abroad returned to their old homes that day. A robin that flew into the house was taken as a sign that someone in the household would die.

In Irish mythology, the hill of Uisneach, in what is now County Westmeath, was known as the navel of Ireland and was a central place of ritual; the main Beltane fire was lit there. Early sources do not give one fixed version of the stories in which the festival appears, so they are best read as tales from a tradition rather than as historical events. The hilltop fires also carried a political meaning, though the sources mention this without spelling out the details.

Over the centuries the festival lost its force. In Ireland, Beltane fires were common until the mid-twentieth century, and today they survive mainly in the city of Limerick, which holds an annual bonfire night. Since 2009 a festival has been held again on the Hill of Uisneach, with a torchlit procession and a large bonfire lit at dusk; in 2017 the fire was lit by the President of Ireland, Michael D. Higgins. In Edinburgh, since 1988, a fire festival on Calton Hill has marked the night of 30 April. It draws on the tradition but is a modern event in form. In Peebles, in the Scottish Borders, a Beltane Queen is crowned every June. The festival has also reached popular music: the 1970 single Ride a White Swan by Marc Bolan and T. Rex refers in its lyrics to 'the people of the Beltane'.

Today Neopagans and Wiccans celebrate Beltane, though in very different ways. Neopagans usually mark it from 30 April to 1 May in the Northern Hemisphere, and from 31 October to 1 November in the Southern Hemisphere. Celtic Reconstructionists try to rebuild the ancient religion from research and historical sources, avoiding a mixture with unrelated traditions; they often celebrate Beltane when the local hawthorns are in bloom, and may lead people and animals between two fires. Wicca, by contrast, is syncretic, and its Beltane has more in common with the Germanic and English May Day, focusing on fertility and on maypole dancing.

Beyond the British Isles, the Iberian Peninsula keeps early-spring celebrations on these dates. In Galicia, people once gathered crops at night by torchlight, using torches called fachucos, and scattered their ashes over the fields; today the festival has regained followers, partly through the romantic and reconstructionist interest it shares with Ireland and Brittany. In Cantabria, the first of May has long been marked by the Maya festival, in which a tall trunk dressed with ribbons and branches is planted in the middle of the villages, and a Mayadama is chosen, dressed in white and adorned with flowers; the custom is read as a celebration of spring fertility. In Castile, especially in the highlands of Segovia, Burgos and Soria, the pingado del mayo, the raising of a May pole, was traditional. On 15 May, Saint Isidro, patron of farmers, is celebrated in many Castilian villages as a festival of abundance and prayers for good harvests, and he is also the patron saint of Madrid.

Trivia

The navel of Ireland. The hill of Uisneach, in Westmeath, was regarded as the ritual centre of ancient Ireland, and the main Beltane bonfire was lit there.
Dew and youth. The dew of Beltane morning was believed to bring beauty and youth, and some young women washed their faces with it at dawn.
