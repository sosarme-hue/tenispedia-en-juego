Myth and religion

An Afro-Cuban religion born from the meeting of Yoruba faith and Catholicism

Santería

Santería is an African diaspora religion that took shape in Cuba in the late nineteenth century. It combines the traditional Yoruba religion brought by enslaved people with the Catholicism imposed under Spanish rule and, in some urban areas, with Spiritist ideas. Its focus is on the orishas, deities linked to Catholic saints and to their own myths.

Santería, also known as the Lucumí cult, Regla de Ocha or Regla Lucumí, is a religion of the African diaspora that developed in Cuba in the late nineteenth century. Its roots lie in the beliefs carried to the island by enslaved West Africans, most of them Yoruba, which blended with Catholicism, the only religion legally permitted under Spanish colonial rule. In the cities of western Cuba, Spiritist ideas were added to the mix. No central authority governs it, and its practitioners, known as creyentes or believers, differ widely in how deeply they are involved and how they practice.

The word santería was first used in a mocking tone, as a way of poking fun at the devotion that followers showed to their saints. During the colonial period the religion was often called the Lucumí cult, a name taken from the Yoruba phrase oloku mi, meaning "my friend". Other names, such as Regla de Ocha, refer to ocha, the term used for its deities. Today santería is the most widely known name, although some practitioners find it offensive.

At the centre of the religion are the orishas, also called ochas or santos. They inherit many of their names and attributes from traditional Yoruba divinities, and believers hold that they can take part in human affairs if they receive offerings and their wishes are honoured. They are linked to a supreme creator, Olodumare, who is understood in different ways: some see this figure as the essence of everything that exists, while others describe it as having three aspects. From Olodumare comes aché, a supernatural force that fills the universe and is called upon through ritual and offerings. Stories about the orishas are known as patakís, and the orishas dwell in orún, the spiritual realm, in contrast to ayé, the world of humans.

Some orishas hold a particularly prominent place. Eleguá, the messenger and guardian of crossroads, is usually the first to be asked for permission when a ceremony begins. Changó is linked with lightning and fire, Ochún with rivers and love, and Yemayá with motherhood and the sea. Oyá is associated with wind and death, Obatalá with truth and justice, and Orula with divination. Ogún, Ochosi and others complete a pantheon that was reshaped as it formed. Scholars note that fewer than twenty orishas became truly prominent, and that many myths changed as local West African cults merged.

The link with Catholic saints is central. Changó is identified with Saint Barbara because both are shown wearing red, and Babalú Ayé is often associated with Saint Lazarus. Cuba's patroness, Our Lady of Charity, is equated with Ochún. Some scholars think these correspondences allowed enslaved people to keep honouring their deities under the eyes of colonial authorities, while others stress that they also helped people fit into Catholic social life.

Each person is believed to be linked to a particular orisha, often called the "owner of the head". This bond is thought to be set before birth and to shape one's personality and destiny. If the orisha is male, it is described as a "father"; if female, as a "mother". Joining a religious house, or ilé, usually requires initiation, which makes a person a santero or santera, although not everyone who takes part in the religion is initiated.

A characteristic ritual is the toque de santo, a celebration with drums, songs and dance meant to draw an orisha down to possess one of those present. Offerings include candles, fruit, flowers, liquor and, in certain cases, animals that are eaten afterwards. Several forms of divination are used to consult the orishas: the diloggún, handled by oloshas, and the Ifá oracle, which belongs to the babalawos. The ancestors also receive attention, especially the egún, the spirits of the dead, to whom offerings such as seven glasses of water may be given.

The history of santería is tied to the slave trade. After the arrival of Europeans, the indigenous population of the island declined sharply, and colonists turned to enslaved Africans for their plantations. The first arrived in 1511, and it is estimated that close to a million people were brought to Cuba in total. On the island they were grouped by port of embarkation, and Yoruba speakers were classed as the "Lucumí nation". In cabildos de nación, mutual-aid associations, traditions were maintained and later blended. In the twentieth century, after independence in 1898, the new constitution recognised freedom of worship, yet santería remained marginalised and was often regarded as witchcraft.

Today its practitioners are concentrated mainly in the provinces of La Habana and Matanzas, although communities exist across the country and abroad. Emigration in the 1960s spread it to Spain, the United States, Italy and other countries. Several hundred thousand people are estimated to be initiates, but the total number of those involved is hard to pin down, since many also consider themselves Catholic.

Trivia

A name born as mockery. The word santería began as a mocking term for the devotion of its followers to their saints. Today it is the most common name, although some practitioners reject it.
Patroness and orisha. Our Lady of Charity, Cuba's patroness, is equated with Ochún, so one figure can receive both Catholic and Afro-Cuban devotion.
A reshaped pantheon. Although hundreds of orishas were venerated in West Africa, fewer than twenty became prominent in santería, according to scholars.
