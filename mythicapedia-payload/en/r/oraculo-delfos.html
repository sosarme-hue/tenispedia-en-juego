Apollo's sanctuary and the voice of the Pythia

Oracle of Delphi

The Delphic oracle was, for centuries, the most prestigious in ancient Greece. Its sanctuary, dedicated to Apollo, stood on the slopes of Mount Parnassus, and there a priestess known as the Pythia delivered replies that the Greeks took to be the god's own words. Its myths, rites and famous ambiguities still fascinate readers today.

The Delphic oracle occupied a large sacred precinct in the valley of the Pleistos, beside Mount Parnassus in Phocis, roughly 700 metres above sea level. According to tradition, the Muses once gathered on the mountain to sing, while the nymphs of the springs listened and Apollo played the lyre. One of its oldest springs, the Castalian, was surrounded by laurels sacred to the god. For more than a thousand years, people seeking advice came here, from kings to ordinary farmers.

The name of the place carries traces of its mythical past. Homer calls it Pytho, and that name is linked to Python, the great serpent or dragon which, according to myth, guarded the earliest oracle. The ancient Greeks offered several word games to explain it. One connected it with the verb pythomai, meaning to rot, because Apollo was said to have left the serpent to decay after killing it. Another linked it with pynthanomai, to inquire, a reference to the oracle's purpose. The word pythia, the name of the priestess, comes from the same root. A different explanation links Delphi to the dolphin. In the Homeric Hymn to Apollo, the god takes the form of a dolphin to lead a ship of Cretan sailors to the site, and they become his priests.

Excavations show that people lived here long before. A small Mycenaean settlement existed around 1400 BC, and it was abandoned sometime between 1100 and 800 BC. Later an altar appeared, followed by a first temple. The origins of the oracle are told in several ways. Diodorus Siculus describes a goatherd who noticed his animals behaving oddly near a crack in the ground that gave off vapours. When he approached, he began to prophesy. As word spread, many visitors came, and some fell into the chasm while in a trance. It was therefore decided that one woman would deliver the answers on behalf of everyone, and she was seated on a tripod for safety.

The Pythia was chosen regardless of social class, provided her life and conduct were beyond reproach. The post was for life, and she was expected to live within the sanctuary. At the height of the oracle's fame up to three priestesses served, taking turns; in periods of decline, one was enough. According to Diodorus, the Pythia was originally a young virgin, but after a young woman was abducted and assaulted by a man from Thessaly, it was decided that only women over fifty would be chosen. They were still to dress as maidens.

The oracle met once a month, on the seventh day, which was regarded as the birthday of Apollo. In winter there were no consultations, because the god was believed to have gone to the land of the Hyperboreans. Before each session the Pythia purified herself in the Castalian spring and made offerings. Then the priests poured cold water over a goat. If it shivered, the god was ready to answer, and the animal was sacrificed on the altar. Visitors washed in water from the springs, paid their fees, offered a sacrifice before the temple and finally put their questions to the Pythia, probably out loud.

The exact ritual is poorly documented. The Pythia sat on a tripod in the innermost chamber of the temple, called the adyton. Later writers such as Plutarch, Strabo and Pausanias describe her trance in different ways. Some speak of vapours rising from a deep fissure, others of waters from the Kassotis spring, or of laurel leaves she chewed. Excavators in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries did not find the deep crack described in the legends. However, a geological review found that two faults cross beneath the temple, through which gases such as ethane, methane and ethylene might seep. This is a modern hypothesis rather than an ancient fact, but it offers one possible explanation.

The replies were often ambiguous. The best-known case concerns Croesus, king of Lydia, who asked whether he should attack the Persians. According to Herodotus, the answer was that if he crossed the river Halys he would destroy a great empire. Croesus took this as a promise of victory, but it was his own kingdom that fell. Xenophon records another answer given to the same king: «If you know yourself, Croesus, you will make your journey happily». Many scholars think the oracle offered sensible guidance as often as it made predictions.

In the precinct, the Sacred Way wound uphill past treasuries, small buildings where cities and rulers stored their gifts, to the temple of Apollo and, higher still, the stadium. The Pythian Games were held there. The altar of sacrifice stood on the terrace in front of the temple. Myth held that Zeus released two eagles from opposite ends of the world, and they met at Delphi, where a stone called the omphalos marked the navel of the earth. Inscribed in the temple were maxims such as «Know thyself», which later became one of the best-known sayings of Greek wisdom.

Trivia

A shepherd's sanctuary. According to Diodorus Siculus, the oracle's origin is linked to a goatherd whose animals reacted strangely to vapours from a crack in the ground. The story resembles other tales in which animals reveal hidden sacred places.
A prophecy that cut both ways. When Croesus misread his answer, the «great empire» that fell was his own. The anecdote is often cited as an example of oracular ambiguity.
The navel of the world. The omphalos of Delphi was a half-egg-shaped stone that symbolised the centre of the world. According to myth, two eagles sent by Zeus from opposite ends of the earth met at this spot.
