Myth and religion

Gods, cosmic order and life after death

Ancient Egyptian religion

Ancient Egyptian religion was a polytheistic system that shaped social, political and funerary life for roughly 3,500 years. Believers dealt with hundreds of gods through offerings, prayers and rituals, aiming to keep the cosmos in order against chaos. Myths about Ra, Osiris, Horus and Anubis, along with ideas about judgement after death, left a lasting mark on later cultures.

Ancient Egyptian religion was a system of polytheistic beliefs and rituals that ran through nearly every part of life. About 1,500 deities are known. Egyptians saw them as real presences that controlled the forces of nature, from the sun to the Nile. Their language had no word equivalent to the modern European idea of religion, because belief and practice were woven into politics, work and family life.

The gods were grouped in many different ways. Some groups were of indefinite size and gathered minor deities with similar roles, often with little individual identity. Others were pairs that expressed the duality of opposing forces, or family triads of father, mother and child who were worshipped together. One of the most important groups was the Ennead of Heliopolis, nine gods within a theological system that covered creation, kingship and the afterlife. Gods could also merge through syncretism. Amun, the power hidden behind things, was linked with Ra, the sun god, to form Amun-Ra, which joined invisible power with the visible force of nature. These links were fluid and did not make two gods permanently one.

At the centre of this order stood Ma'at, a word that covered truth, justice and the balance of the universe. Ma'at was a fixed order that had existed since creation, and it was constantly threatened by Isfet, chaos. Humans were expected to cooperate and live together to uphold it in society. On a cosmic level, the gods had to keep functioning in balance, and offerings and rituals were made to them to hold back disorder. Time itself was understood as cyclical. The annual flooding of the Nile, the succession from one king to another and, above all, the daily journey of Ra renewed the order established at creation.

The cosmos was imagined as a flat expanse of earth, personified by the god Geb, arched over by the sky goddess Nut, with Shu, the god of air, standing between them. Beneath the earth lay a parallel underworld, and beyond the heavens stretched Nu, the chaos that had existed before creation. According to the myth, Ra crossed the sky by day and at night passed through the Duat, a mysterious realm linked to death and rebirth, so that he could be born again at dawn. The sources do not settle whether the Duat lay underground or in the sky.

Gods were shown through symbolic images that indicated their role in nature, rather than as literal portraits. Anubis, the funerary god, appeared as a jackal, an animal that scavenged and could threaten the body. Egyptians turned that threat into protection. His black skin recalled mummified flesh and the rich black soil of the Nile, a symbol of resurrection. This iconography was not fixed, and many gods could appear in more than one form. Many deities also had regional centres of worship, though these associations shifted over time. Montu was the patron of Thebes, but in the Middle Kingdom he was displaced by Amun, who may have originated elsewhere.

The pharaoh stood at the centre of religious life. Royal authority was believed to be a divine force embodied in the king, even though he was recognised as human. He acted as an intermediary between the people and the gods, and sustained Ma'at through justice, temples and offerings. He was identified with Horus and was regarded as a son of Ra. After death he became fully divine and was associated with Ra and with Osiris, the god of death and rebirth. Many mortuary temples were dedicated to the worship of dead kings. Yet the real prestige of the monarch could differ from what official texts and images showed, and his religious importance declined sharply from the end of the New Kingdom. Meanwhile, popular religion grew in weight.

Egyptians distinguished several parts of a person. The ka was a life force that left the body at death and had to keep receiving food through offerings. The ba was the set of unique spiritual traits of each individual, and it stayed with the body. Funerary rituals aimed to free the ba and reunite it with the ka, so that the person could live on as an akh, a transfigured being in the afterlife. At first, only the pharaoh seemed to have a ba and be able to join the gods, while ordinary people passed into a dark, desolate realm. Over time, the hope of a favourable afterlife was extended to everyone. This explains the effort put into preserving bodies, building tombs and placing grave goods.

In the New Kingdom, according to the most developed beliefs, the soul had to avoid dangers in the Duat before facing a final judgement, the so-called Weighing of the Heart. The gods compared the heart of the deceased with Ma'at to determine whether the person had lived in accordance with it. Those who passed the test were said to dwell in the realm of Osiris, a fertile and pleasant land.

The religion changed over time. After the Roman conquest of Egypt in 30 BC, official temples declined and the cult of Isis spread widely across the Mediterranean. During the Ptolemaic period, the Greek rulers kept the traditions alive and created Serapis, a deity who combined features of Osiris and Apis with Greek attributes. Christianity later spread through Egypt, and the end of the religion is traditionally placed in the 6th century, when Justinian ordered the temples at Philae closed. Recent scholarship argues that organised paganism at Philae had already ended in the 5th century, which suggests that the decline was gradual rather than a single event.

Trivia

Ta netjer. Some scholars have linked the land of Punt with the origin of Egyptian deities, partly because it was called Ta netjer in Egyptian, meaning God's Land.
Statues of Min. At the temple of Koptos, three monumental statues of Min were found, dated to around 3300 BC. They show a bearded man and bear inscriptions with his name.
Serapis. In the Ptolemaic period, the god Serapis emerged from a blend of Osiris and Apis with Greek features, and became very popular among Egypt's Greek population.
