Myth and religion

The learned class of the Iron Age Celts

Druid

Druids were the learned class of the ancient Celtic peoples of Gaul and Britain, and possibly of other Celtic-speaking lands. They left no writings of their own, so nearly everything known about them comes from Greek and Roman observers and from later Irish medieval stories. Their modern image has been shaped as much by centuries of reinterpretation as by the scant evidence itself.

The druids were the learned class of the Celtic peoples of Gaul and Britain during the Iron Age, and perhaps of other Celtic-speaking lands as well. Greek and Roman writers described them as priests, teachers, judges and guardians of religious and legal tradition, though the word priest covers only part of their role. Classical authors placed them only in Gaul and Britain, never among the other Celtic peoples. In Ireland their function was mainly priestly, while in Wales it was more prophetic, and there they were said to be filled with awen, an inspiration that also animated the bards.

Almost everything we know comes from outsiders, because the druids committed nothing of their teaching to writing. Greek and Roman authors describe them, and Irish medieval tales preserve many more, but those tales are now generally read as literary creations of Christian writers rather than as trustworthy evidence for pre-Christian religion. Archaeology reveals the religious life of the Iron Age, yet no object dug up has been securely linked to the druids. The historian Ronald Hutton put it bluntly: they certainly existed, but we can know almost nothing about them for certain, so they function largely as legendary figures.

The earliest notices, from the 4th and 3rd centuries BCE, treat them as barbarian philosophers, somewhat like the Persian magi or the Indian gymnosophists. The fullest account comes from Julius Caesar, whose reliability has long been debated. Caesar described the druids as one of the two most influential groups in Gallic society, alongside the nobility. They organised worship, sacrifices, divination and legal judgements. They were exempt from military service and taxes, and they could exclude a person from religious festivals, which in practice meant social exile. Diodorus Siculus and Strabo added that they were held in such awe that they could halt a battle by stepping between two armies.

Training was long and entirely oral. Pomponius Mela was the first to say it took place in secret, in caves and forests. Students memorised large bodies of verse, and Caesar claimed that completing the course could take more than twenty years. There is no evidence that the profession was reserved for men, and some myths, such as that of Ceridwen, hint that women may have practised it. Beyond that, what we know about how novices were taught is largely guesswork, since no ancient verse has survived.

Several ancient writers linked them with philosophy. Alexander Polyhistor called them philosophers and saw their belief in the immortality of the soul and reincarnation as Pythagorean in origin. Diodorus Siculus, around 36 BCE, described a similar doctrine: souls are immortal and, after a set number of years, begin a new life in a new body. Caesar also wrote of a common ancestor of the tribe, whom he called Dispater, meaning the father Hades. Later speculation, such as a 1928 folklorist's suggestion of Buddhist missionary influence, lacks direct support.

The most famous passage comes from Pliny the Elder, who described a ritual involving oak and mistletoe. Much of the modern picture of druids, with white robes, sacred groves, mistletoe and a golden sickle, derives from that single account. Pliny also offered a folk etymology, linking the name to the Greek word for oak. Most scholars today reconstruct it from Proto-Celtic roots meaning roughly oak-knower, drawing on an Indo-European root for oak and another for seeing or knowing, while some argue for a sense of strong insight. An older reading, which took the first element as an intensifier meaning very wise, was proposed in 1893 and withdrawn by its author in 1927, but it still turns up in popular books.

Greek and Roman writers presented the druids as practitioners of human sacrifice, which helped them label the Celts as barbarians. According to Caesar, the victims were usually criminals, though sometimes innocent people were used too, and they were burned inside a huge wicker figure. A later manuscript, the Commenta Bernensia, links three gods to different deaths: drowning for Teutates, hanging for Esus and fire for Taranis. Iron Age mass graves found in Gaul have been read as sacrifices by the excavator Jean-Louis Brunaux, while the archaeologist Martin Brown has argued that they may hold honoured warriors buried in a sanctuary.

The debate remains open. The historian J. Rives warned that Greeks and Romans routinely projected barbarian traits onto outsiders, so these accusations need careful handling. Ronald Hutton concluded that the classical sources, as they have come down to us, are not good enough to settle whether human sacrifice was truly part of druidic belief. Peter Berresford Ellis, in his 1994 book on the druids, compared them to the Brahmin caste of India and considered the sacrifice charges unproven. Nora Chadwick, a scholar of medieval Welsh and Irish literature, argued firmly that they took no part in human sacrifice and that the charges were Roman imperial propaganda.

After the Roman conquest of Gaul, the druids declined. Emperors Tiberius and Claudius placed druidism under prohibition in the 1st century CE, and it soon vanished from the continental record. Ireland was never reached by Rome, and there medieval literature preserves many druids, such as the sorcerers who oppose Christianity in the Táin Bó Cúailnge. Around 750, the Irish monk Blathmac used the word again, describing Jesus as wiser than any druid.

From the 16th century onward, the druids became a screen for changing ideas: monotheist philosophers in Renaissance humanism, builders of Stonehenge, and bards in the Romantic imagination. During the so-called Celtic Revival, fraternal and neopagan groups took up the name, in a movement known as neo-druidism. These modern groups draw inspiration from the druids but have no genuine continuity with the ancient priesthood. Academic scholarship accepts the druids as a real priestly and learned order of the Iron Age Celts, while stressing how little about them can be established with confidence.

Trivia

A name that sounds like oak. Pliny the Elder linked druid to the Greek word for oak. The modern reconstruction, based on Indo-European roots, suggests something like oak-knower.
A bird with a wise name. In Irish and Welsh, druí and dryw could also denote the wren, a bird regarded as prophetic in those traditions.
An interpretation that refuses to die. In 1893 Rudolf Thurneysen proposed reading the name as very wise. He withdrew it in 1927, yet the reading still appears in popular books.
