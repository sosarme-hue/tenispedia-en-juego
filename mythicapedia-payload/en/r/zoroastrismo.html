The faith of Zoroaster and the worship of Ahura Mazda

Zoroastrianism

Zoroastrianism, also called Mazdayasna, is one of the oldest organised faiths still practised today. It is built around the teachings of the prophet Zoroaster and the supreme deity Ahura Mazda, and its picture of a choice between truth and falsehood left a lasting mark on Iranian religious thought and on later traditions.

Zoroastrianism, also known as Mazdayasna or Mazdaism, is one of the oldest organised religions still practised today. Its name comes from Zoroaster, the Greek form of the Avestan name Zarathustra, the prophet on whose teachings the faith rests. The word Mazdayasna combines the name of Ahura Mazda with the Avestan yasna, meaning worship or devotion, so it can be rendered as devotion to Ahura Mazda. Believers also call themselves Behdin, meaning people of the good religion.

The faith grew out of a reform of beliefs shared by Iranian-speaking peoples who settled in western Turkestan. These peoples were related to the Indo-Aryans of India, which is why Zoroastrianism is often studied alongside Vedic religion. The parallels are striking. The Iranian ahura corresponds to the Vedic asura, and the Iranian daeva to the Indian deva. The god Mitra, who in India split into several figures, kept his unity in Iran. According to the tradition, Zoroaster divided the older divinities into two groups: the ahuras, who were to be revered, and the daevas, who were to be rejected. The oldest compositions, the Gathas, are attributed to Zoroaster himself and form the core of the Yasna, the liturgical part of the Avesta, the sacred collection of texts.

The ethical heart of the teaching is the choice between asha, truth and cosmic order, and druj, falsehood and deceit. The texts describe two twin spirits, Spenta Mainyu, the bountiful spirit, and Angra Mainyu, the destructive one, both of whom exercised free will at the start of creation. In the oldest texts, however, Angra Mainyu is not presented as an equal rival to Ahura Mazda. The figure of a primordial adversary known as Ahriman developed later, in Middle Persian writings such as the ninth-century Bundahishn. Nor is the end of the world imagined as God alone destroying evil. The frashokereti, the renovation of the world, is a shared process in which humanity takes part through good thoughts, good words and good deeds, the three moral pillars repeated in its prayers.

The faith became the official religion of successive Iranian empires. The Achaemenids gave it a more formal shape, although in the time of Darius I Mitra was still honoured, and the kings declared their devotion to Ahura Mazda in inscriptions that sat alongside other cults. Herodotus, describing Persian customs, reports that they offered sacrifices on high mountains to Zeus, whom the Greeks linked with the Iranian sky god. The same author mentions the Magi as one of the tribes of Media and describes funeral practices such as the exposure of the dead. For centuries the Avesta was passed on orally, and under the Sasanians the religion was revitalised and its teachings standardised in writing.

Was Zoroastrianism monotheistic? Scholars do not agree. Some speak of ethical dualism, others of henotheism, in which a supreme god coexists with other divinities. Almut Hintze has proposed a distinct form of monotheism that blends dualist and polytheist features, while Prods Oktor Skjærvø describes a dualistic and polytheistic religion with one supreme god. The picture of strict monotheism took hold in the nineteenth century, when the orientalist Martin Haug read the Avesta as reducing the lesser divinities to angels. That reading also spread among the Parsis themselves, and today it is being reconsidered both in scholarship and in the community.

The lesser divinities are known as yazatas, a word meaning beings worthy of worship. Among them Mithra stands out. He was originally a god of covenant, agreement and promise, and Anahita, an ancient goddess of the waters, is also prominent. Rashnu is one of the judges who weigh souls after death. The tradition also recognises the Amesha Spentas, a group of entities that embody aspects of the universe and of moral goodness. Fire, atar, is the most sacred symbol of Ahura Mazda, and that is why places of worship are called fire temples, where the flame is kept burning and ceremonies are performed.

After the Muslim conquest of Persia the faith lost much of its strength, and many followers migrated to India. They settled in Gujarat, and their descendants are known as the Parsis. Today the community is small. India's 2011 census counted 57,264 Parsis and Zoroastrians, and a 2012 survey estimated between about 111,000 and 122,000 believers worldwide. The Iranian Zoroastrians, who remained in Iran, keep the tradition with doctrinal and ritual differences from the Parsis. In recent years some young Kurds have turned to Zoroastrianism, partly as an expression of ethnic identity.

Because of its importance in the history of ideas, Zoroastrianism is often studied in relation to Judaism and Christianity. The scholar Mary Boyce argued that Zoroaster was the first to teach individual judgement, heaven and hell, and the future resurrection of the body. Other researchers have proposed that the figure of a divine adversary, the hope of a saviour and parts of Jewish eschatology absorbed Iranian influences during the Babylonian exile. Other specialists prefer to speak of parallels rather than direct borrowing, and they stress that the belief systems differ in important ways.

Trivia

A name of Greek origin. The name Zoroaster is the Greek form of Zarathustra, the prophet's Avestan name. In modern Persian he is known as Zartosht or Zardosht.
Fire as a symbol. Zoroastrians regard fire as especially sacred because it represents Ahura Mazda. That is why their places of worship are called fire temples.
Behdin, the good religion. Believers call themselves Behdin, meaning of the good religion. In the liturgy the term refers to a lay member formally initiated in a ceremony called the navjote.
