The way of the kami: Japan's traditional religion

Shinto

Shinto is the traditional religion of Japan, centred on kami, supernatural beings believed to inhabit nature and community life. Its myths, recorded in eighth-century texts, explain the origin of the Japanese islands and the imperial line. Its history shows how a tradition without fixed dogma was reshaped by Buddhism and later by modern nationalism.

Shinto, or shinto, is the traditional religion of Japan. For centuries it has coexisted with Buddhism, the country's other major faith, and many people take part in rituals from both without feeling any contradiction. It has no single founder, no one sacred book and no fixed doctrine, so describing it takes more care than describing many other traditions.

The name means "the way of the gods", yet it was not used as a name of its own until Buddhism arrived. To tell the native religion apart from the imported one, people began using this label, and they chose a Chinese term because Chinese was then the only written language in Japan. Scholars still debate when it is fair to speak of Shinto as a distinct religion, since many of its practices were local and varied.

At its heart are the kami, supernatural beings or forces that dwell in nature: mountains, rocks, trees, rivers, wind and fire. There are also kami linked to deified ancestors, outstanding people and abstract ideals. Some are local guardians of a particular place, while others have a much wider reach, such as Amaterasu, the sun goddess. The phrase yaoyorozu no kami, literally "eight million gods", is usually taken to mean countless rather than an exact number.

The oldest myths are known mainly from two eighth-century texts, the Kojiki (712) and the Nihon shoki (720), which were produced by the ruling elite to legitimise its power. According to these stories, the primordial pair Izanagi and Izanami created the Japanese islands and many other kami. Izanami died giving birth to the fire kami, and Izanagi travelled to the land of the dead to bring her back. When he saw her body decaying, he fled and sealed the entrance. Purifying himself in the sea, he produced Amaterasu from his left eye, Tsukuyomi from his right and Susanoo from his nose. After a quarrel with Susanoo, Amaterasu hid in a cave and the world fell into darkness until other kami coaxed her out. The tradition also says she later sent her grandson Ninigi to rule Japan, equipped with a curved jewel, a mirror and a sword, which became symbols of imperial authority. Today these narratives are read as tradition rather than history, though their link to the imperial family became central to later interpretations of Shinto.

Rituals take place mostly at shrines, known as jinja, which are staffed by priests who offer food, drink and objects to the kami. Shrines aim to cultivate harmony between people and the kami and to ask for their protection. Purity is a central concern: before worship it is customary to wash and cleanse oneself, because impurity was associated with death and sickness. There are also household shrines, local festivals, divination and charms. Japan has around 100,000 public shrines, and Ise holds a special position because of its privileged relationship with the court.

The story changed when Buddhism arrived from China and Korea at the end of the Kofun period. For centuries many shrines were looked after by Buddhist monks, and the kami were understood as manifestations of buddhas and bodhisattvas. The honji suijaku theory, or "original form and traces", explained this relationship. Over time, schools emerged that wanted to present Shinto as an independent teaching, and in the Edo period the Kokugaku movement sought to strip it of foreign influences.

With the Meiji Restoration in 1868, Shinto became an ideological tool of the state. The kami and the buddhas were legally separated, and shrines were presented as places of devotion to the emperor and the nation. Visiting a shrine was framed as a patriotic duty rather than a religious act. During the militarism of the Shōwa period, this system was used to reinforce nationalism and colonial expansion, and shrines were built in occupied parts of China and Korea. After defeat in 1945, State Shinto was dismantled, and in 1946 the emperor renounced any claim to divinity. Some observers see this as a clear example of how myths can serve propaganda and exclusion, and the Yasukuni Shrine in Tokyo still provokes debate because of its links to that past.

Today many people describe Shinto as a nature religion, a framing that some critics see as a way of distancing it from its military history. It is a plural tradition, with local variations and no detailed moral code. Its main legacy is a set of practical values about respect, purity and living alongside the natural world, which remain part of everyday life in Japan.

Trivia

Eight million. The phrase yaoyorozu no kami is often rendered as "eight million gods", but it really means something like "countless". It reflects an open rather than closed system of deities.
A borrowed name. Before Buddhism arrived, the native religion had no name. The Japanese adopted a Chinese expression to distinguish it from the imported teaching.
A goddess in a cave. According to the myth, Amaterasu hid in a cave and left the world in darkness. Her story is one of the best-known in the eighth-century texts.
