Myth and religion

Translating foreign gods into the Greek language

Interpretatio graeca

Interpretatio graeca is the habit of ancient Greek writers of identifying foreign gods with members of their own pantheon, based on similarities of function or character. It was a way of making sense of other religions and, at the same time, a tool for living alongside them. The Roman equivalent, interpretatio romana, followed a similar logic.

The Latin phrase interpretatio graeca, meaning "Greek interpretation" or "Greek translation", describes the tendency of the ancient Greeks to match foreign gods with members of their own pantheon. The criterion was resemblance: if a foreign deity shared traits or functions with a Greek god, it was treated as, in a sense, that same god under another name. It was a comparative method, used to interpret or try to understand the myths and religion of other peoples through familiar concepts.

The most cited case is Herodotus. When describing Egyptian religion, he linked Amon with Zeus, Osiris with Dionysus and Ptah with Hephaestus. For him, Egyptians and Greeks worshipped the same deities under different names. He used the same approach with the Scythians, whose queen of the gods, Tabiti, he matched with Hestia, and whose main gods he associated with Zeus, Gaia, Aphrodite Urania and, without naming them, with equivalents of Heracles and Ares. Other Greek writers did something similar with Roman cults: Dionysius of Halicarnassus and Plutarch described Roman temples and practices under the names of equivalent Greek gods.

The underlying idea, in ancient polytheism, is that each deity is defined by its character, that is, by what it does and how it appears in myths, hymns and rites. Jan Assmann calls this "intercultural translation": because gods resemble one another, their names can be translated from one to another. Pliny the Elder summed it up as nomina alia aliis gentibus, "different names for different peoples". This capacity for translation made possible the religious syncretism of the Hellenistic age and of the Roman Empire before Christianity.

Not all the equivalences were equally solid. Some seem to share a common origin: Zeus and Jupiter are thought to derive from the same Indo-European archetype, Dyeus, the supreme sky god, so they share functions by nature. Others demanded much more theological and poetic work. Ares and Mars are both war gods, but Ares was a minor figure in Greek religious practice and was held in low regard by the poets, while Mars was a father of the Roman people and a central figure in archaic Roman religion. There were also Roman deities with no Greek equivalent, such as Janus and Terminus, which kept a tradition of their own.

Etruscan culture played an intermediary role in this transmission. The name of Heracles passed into Etruscan as Hercle and from there into Latin as Hercules. Later, when Rome conquered the Hellenistic world, it adopted new Greek beliefs and rites. Apollo is a good example: he entered Roman culture under his Greek name but developed distinctly Roman features, such as the role Augustus gave him as one of his patron deities.

The method was also applied to the gods of other peoples. The Romans interpreted Celtic and Near Eastern deities with equal ease: Lugus was identified with Mercury, Sulis with Minerva, and the Anatolian storm god, with his double-headed axe, became Jupiter Dolichenus, a cult figure especially popular among soldiers. The Jewish case was more complicated. Depending on the author, Yahweh was identified with Jupiter, Saturn, Dionysus, Typhon or Helios, according to how each writer viewed Jews and Christians. Jews, for their part, rejected such identifications outright, and this divergence contributed to the tensions with Rome.

When the Romans had no equivalent, they simply absorbed foreign deities. Cybele, for instance, arrived from Phrygia under the name Magna Mater deorum Idaea, although the Greeks had earlier interpreted her as Rhea. Finally, the same principle reached the Germanic peoples. Tacitus, in the Germania, used the phrase interpretatio romana for the gods of the Naharvali, identified with Castor and Pollux, and for the principal Germanic gods, which he rendered as Hercules and Mercury, alluding to Thor and Odin.

Trivia

Herodotus and Egypt. Herodotus identified Amon with Zeus, Osiris with Dionysus and Ptah with Hephaestus, equivalences that lasted into the Hellenistic period.
Hercle, Hercules. The name of Heracles reached Latin through Etruscan, where it appears as Hercle before becoming Hercules.
