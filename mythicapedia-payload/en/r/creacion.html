Stories about the origin of the world and humankind

Creation myth

A creation myth is a symbolic narrative that explains how the world began and how the first people came to live in it. Such stories appear in nearly every known religious tradition, and most of them exist in several versions. For those who tell them, they often carry deep truths, expressed symbolically, metaphorically or, depending on the case, historically or literally.

A creation myth is a symbolic narrative that explains how the world began and how people first came to inhabit it. In everyday speech the word 'myth' tends to suggest something false or fanciful, but within each culture these stories carry a very different weight. They are told as sacred accounts and shape how people relate to nature, to the spiritual world and to one another. They appear in nearly every known religious tradition and, since they were passed on orally, most of them survive in several versions.

Many of these myths are set in a vague past that the historian of religion Mircea Eliade called in illo tempore, 'at that time'. They feature characters who are deities, human-like figures or animals, and they often speak and transform with ease. Scholars have tried to sort them into types. Eliade and Charles Long identified five basic kinds: creation ex nihilo, in which the world arises from the thought, word, dream or bodily secretions of a divine being; earth-diver creation, in which an animal brings mud up from the bottom of the primordial waters; emergence myths, in which peoples pass through several worlds before reaching the present one; creation by the dismemberment of a primordial being; and creation by the division of an original unity, such as a cosmic egg that cracks open or order emerging from chaos. Marta Weigle later refined this scheme, and Raymond Van Over proposed another one based on six recurring themes. These categories are tools for study rather than sealed boxes, and many stories combine features of several types.

In ancient Egypt, according to the Heliopolitan account, there was at first only Nun, an endless ocean of dark waters that held all the elements of the cosmos. From Nun arose the god Ra, who became aware of himself and gave himself a name. Ra made Shu, the air, from his breath and Tefnut, moisture, from his spittle. Their children were Geb, the earth, and Nut, the sky. Because Geb and Nut lay so tightly joined that nothing could come between them, Shu lifted Nut over his head and held her apart from Geb. Another version tells of an eye of the sun sent to find Shu and Tefnut; when it returned to find another eye had taken its place, it wept, and its tears became the first men and women. Each day Ra crossed the sky in a boat, and each night he travelled through the Duat, the underworld, passing twelve gates and facing the serpent Apep, the enemy of the sun. Each dawn the sun was reborn, and cosmic order, Ma'at, was preserved.

In classical Greece, Hesiod's Theogony begins with Chaos, a deep void from which emerged Gaia, the Earth, and other primordial beings such as Eros, Tartarus and Erebus. Gaia bore Uranus, the Sky, who then fertilised her. Their offspring included the Titans, the Cyclopes and the Hundred-Handed ones. Cronus overthrew his father, and he was later challenged by his son Zeus, who, with help from the Cyclopes, won and imprisoned the Titans in Tartarus. Other texts offer different genealogies. An Orphic poem, whose fragments survive in the Derveni papyrus, places Nyx, Night, as an origin that comes before Uranus, Cronus and Zeus. Homer, for his part, described the Earth as a flat disc floating in the Ocean, with the Sun, Helios, crossing the sky by day and sailing back around the Earth at night in a golden cup.

In Mesoamerica, the Maya Popol Vuh tells how the gods tried twice to create humanity. First they shaped beings from mud, but the figures could not hold together and had no souls; then they made them from wood, but these beings had no will and forgot their makers. Finally they formed people from maize dough mixed with the blood of the gods. In the Aztec tradition, Tezcatlipoca and Quetzalcoatl lure a monster of the earth by offering a foot as bait; they use its body to shape the land, and from its eyes, tears and openings come lakes, rivers and caves. The 'Legend of the Fifth Sun' says that the gods gathered at Teotihuacan and chose one of their number to throw himself into a fire to create the world. The strongest hesitated, and a small, humble god leapt in without hesitation and became the Sun; the first, ashamed, then threw himself in too and became the Moon.

In West Africa, the Yoruba account tells how Oduduwa came down over the primordial waters by a chain carrying a handful of earth, a five-toed hen and a seed. He scattered the earth on the water, forming the kingdom of Ife; the hen scratched the ground and buried the seed, from which grew a great tree with sixteen branches, the ancestors of the sixteen Yoruba peoples. The Zulu story speaks of Unkulunkulu, who emerged from the void, made the earth from two rocks and, with the help of herbs, brought forth a man and a woman.

In the Andes, the myth of Viracocha tells how the god created the universe and then tried to make a first humanity from stone. Those giants refused to acknowledge him as their creator and were destroyed by a flood. The Incas, for their part, placed the origin of their people at Lake Titicaca, with Manco Capac and Mama Ocllo. In Mapuche tradition, the sea serpent Kai Kai Vilú tried to drown humanity, while Treng Treng Vilú sheltered people in the mountains and made the land rise as the sea rose. The ancestors of the Mapuche survived and appeased Kai Kai through a nguillatun, a ceremony. Among the Inuit, the first woman drew every animal out of a hole in the ice, and the caribou, the last one, became Kaila's finest gift to the people.

Read through the lens of mythology, these stories show how each people understands its place in the world. For their believers, many of them remain a living truth rather than old tales.

Trivia

A myth in papyrus. A fragment of the Derveni papyrus, found in Greece, shows that a theogonic poem attributed to Orpheus existed, in which Night appears as an origin prior to Uranus, Cronus and Zeus.
A pattern across continents. The earth-diver type, in which an animal brings mud from the depths, appears in North America, Eastern Europe and also in the Yoruba myth of Oduduwa in West Africa.
A late debate. The idea that God created the world from nothing does not appear in the Hebrew Bible, and in the early second century CE Christian thinkers began to debate it, until by the early third century it had become a basic tenet of Christian theology.
