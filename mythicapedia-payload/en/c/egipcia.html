Gods, myths and the order of the world in ancient Egypt

Egyptian mythology

Egyptian mythology gathered the stories through which the ancient Egyptians explained the origin of the world, the actions of their gods and the fate of the dead over more than three thousand years. Its myths are fragmentary and changing, and they are best understood as metaphors rather than fixed tales. They were tied to maat, the cosmic order, and to the authority of the pharaoh.

Egyptian mythology is the body of stories through which the ancient Egyptians made sense of the world by describing what their gods did. These beliefs formed a central part of Egyptian religion, which was practised from the predynastic period until the emperor Justinian I closed the last active temple, that of Isis at Philae, in 535. Over more than three thousand years, the myths changed a great deal, so no short account can cover every god or version.

The Egyptians lived in a fertile strip beside the Nile, surrounded by desert. The river's yearly floods renewed the land, while a weak flood could bring famine. This experience shaped how they saw time, as a series of natural cycles: the sun rises and sets, the river rises and falls, the seasons return. The order of this world, called maat, was always under threat from chaos, which they associated with the desert and with peoples beyond the valley. Many myths revolve around this tension between order, chaos and renewal.

In art, many gods appear with a human body and an animal head, which sets them apart from Greek or Roman images. The habit goes back to the predynastic period, when people linked the forces that troubled them to particular animals. The falcon became Horus, associated with the sky; the desert jackal, guardian of the cemeteries, became Anubis; and the Nile crocodile, dangerous and feared, was revered in the region of the Fayum. Human traits were added to these figures, such as family ties, so that triads formed of a god, his wife and their son.

When the country was unified near the end of the predynastic period, around 3100 BC, local gods gained national importance and began to be linked with one another. Each province, or nome, kept its own protectors, though. In Heliopolis Ra was the chief god, in Thebes Amun, and in Memphis Ptah. Temple priests started to arrange this crowd of deities and to explain how they related, how the world was made and why the Nile flooded. These local theologies are known today mainly through texts such as the Pyramid Texts and the Book of the Dead.

Egyptian cosmology divided the universe into three realms. Above was the sky, home of the gods, represented by the goddess Nut arched over the earth, described as the one who gave birth to the gods. Below was the earth, home of humans, which was the body of Geb, the god of the earth, lying beneath Nut. Beyond lay the Duat, the realm of the dead, a dark space that Ra crossed in his boat at night and where the deceased had to avoid many dangers. Osiris ruled there first, and later Horus.

Priests grouped the gods into families. The Ennead of Heliopolis brought together nine deities, among them Atum, Shu, Tefnut, Nut, Geb, Osiris, Isis, Nephthys and Seth, and gave primacy to Atum and Ra. The Ogdoad of Hermopolis grouped eight primeval beings, pairs representing principles such as the primordial waters of Nun, and in this version the god Ra emerged from it. Local triads also existed: Khnum, Satet and Anuket at Elephantine; Amun, Mut and Khonsu at Thebes; and Ptah, Sekhmet and Nefertum at Memphis, where the three gods had no connection before the triad was formalised.

The versions do not always agree. According to the Heliopolitan tradition, Atum created the world, while in the Memphite account that task belongs to Ptah, whose commands the Ennead carry out. This story raises Ptah above the gods of Heliopolis, and many scholars read it as a political claim for the supremacy of Memphis. In the same way, Hathor could be called the mother, wife or daughter of Ra depending on the text. The Egyptians did not treat such differences as mistakes, but as different ways of naming a reality they saw as beyond human reach.

The merging of cults was constant. Ra was joined with Atum as Ra-Atum, then with the Aten as Aten-Ra, and later with Horus as Ra-Horakhty. Ptah combined with Seker and then with Osiris. Hathor was largely absorbed into Isis. Seth, who first appeared as a protective god, came to gather the traits of evil forces. Egyptian tradition connects this change with the Hyksos period, when rulers of Asian origin chose Seth as their patron and were later expelled. After their fall, Seth was reviled.

The best-known myth is that of Osiris, Isis and Horus against Seth. In the story, Seth killed Osiris and scattered his body, and Isis is said to have gathered it together and conceived Horus. Horus, once grown, fought Seth for the throne. This cycle explained the legitimacy of kingship, since the pharaoh was identified with Horus, and it also gave meaning to death and rebirth. In the Ptolemaic and Roman periods, the triad of Osiris, Isis and Horus lasted longest, and each god had a major cult centre: Osiris at Abydos, Isis at Dendera and Horus at Edfu.

A singular episode was that of Akhenaten, who in the fourteenth century BC tried to give priority to the Aten, the sun disc, and moved the capital to Akhetaten, the site now called Amarna. Some Egyptologists describe this as henotheism, the worship of one chief god without denying others, while others argue that calling it monotheism is inaccurate, since the royal family remained part of the pantheon and drew its power from the Aten. After his death the traditional cults returned strongly, and the names of Akhenaten and Tutankhamun were erased from many monuments.

Life after death stood at the heart of the religion. At first it was a privilege of gods and pharaohs, but it later spread to the wider population. This gave rise to mummification, meant to preserve the body, and to the belief that a missing part of the body could prevent entry into the afterlife. The Egyptians thought of the person as made of several elements, among them the ba, the ka and the akh, which separated and came together according to the funerary account.

The judgement of the dead is perhaps the most famous scene. Anubis led the deceased to the Hall of Two Truths, where the heart, the seat of moral conduct, was weighed on a balance against a feather representing maat. If the result was favourable, the deceased was brought before Osiris in the Field of Reeds. If not, Ammit, the devourer of hearts, a creature combining crocodile, lion and hippopotamus, destroyed the heart and the person lost any hope of immortality. Before forty-two judges of the Duat, the deceased had to speak the right words, formulas later gathered in the Book of the Dead.

The Book of the Dead was not a single text but a collection of about 190 magical spells, adapted to each person, which were placed beside the dead or carved on tomb walls. One of its best-known examples is the Papyrus of Ani, made around 1240 BC, with texts and images of Ani and his wife on their journey through the realm of the dead.

Mythology influenced Egyptian life deeply. It inspired rituals, supported the ideology of kingship and appeared in tombs, temples and amulets. Festivals followed the agricultural calendar. The most solemn was the lamentation of Isis, which marked the death of Osiris and was followed by the search for the god, the reappearance of Osiris when the plants sprouted, and the birth of Horus. Plutarch's account shows how these celebrations joined myth, calendar and harvest.

Today the myths are read in many ways. Some specialists regard them as fragmentary accounts, while others hold that complete narratives existed but were rarely written down. What seems clear is that the Egyptians accepted different versions without demanding absolute consistency, because what mattered was the deeper meaning of each story. That flexibility is one of the features that most strikes modern readers of this ancient mythology.

Trivia

Ammit, the devourer. Ammit was a creature made of crocodile, lion and hippopotamus parts who waited beside the scales of judgement. If the heart of the deceased weighed more than the feather of maat, she devoured it.
A goddess with a cow's head. In a minor myth, Horus grew angry with his mother Isis and cut off her head. Isis replaced it with a cow's head, which explains why she is sometimes shown wearing cow horns in her headdress.
The last temple. The temple of Isis at Philae was, according to the sources, the last active centre of Egyptian religion. Justinian I closed it in 535.
