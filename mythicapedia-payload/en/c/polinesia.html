Stories, lineages and oral traditions of the Pacific

Polynesian mythology

Polynesian mythology gathers the oral traditions of the peoples of the island groups of the central and southern Pacific, along with those of some scattered communities beyond that region. These were living traditions, passed on by word of mouth and reshaped by each teller. When writing arrived, some versions were fixed on paper and gained a political and legal weight that can still be felt today.

Polynesian mythology covers the stories, myths, legends and oral traditions of the peoples of Polynesia, a vast triangle of island groups in the central and southern Pacific, together with the tales of several scattered communities that lie outside that triangle and are known as the Polynesian outliers. There is no single sacred book and no closed canon. These stories live in the voice of the person telling them, in chants, in the names of places and in the shapes of rocks.

The Polynesian languages descend from a reconstructed ancestor called Proto-Polynesian, probably spoken around 1000 BC in the Tonga and Samoa area. From there, voyagers fanned out eastward, first to the Cook Islands and from there to places such as Tahiti and the Marquesas. Their descendants later reached the islands stretching from Tahiti to Rapa Nui, and later still Hawai‘i and New Zealand. The most recent research puts the settlement of New Zealand at about 1300 AD. All these languages belong to the Austronesian family, and they are so close in vocabulary and grammar that speakers from different islands can often understand one another.

The peoples also share much of their culture, from social organisation and the raising of children to horticulture, building and textile making. Their myths show the same pattern: a common stock of tales that each community reworks in its own way. One widespread theme is the marriage of Sky and Earth. In New Zealand, the Māori version tells of Rangi and Papa, whose union gives birth to the world and everything in it. In some island groups, Tangaroa is the god of the sea and of fishing. There are also stories of islands hauled up from the bottom of the ocean with a magic fishhook, or thrown down from the heavens, as well as tales of voyages, migrations, seductions and battles.

Among the most widely told figures is Māui, a cunning trickster whose exploits are known across much of the region. In some versions he faces a demon called Ulupoka. Another famous figure is Hina, or Sina, a beautiful goddess and ancestress. Alongside these shared themes, each island group has its own demigods and culture heroes, whose stories gradually shade into the firmer outlines of remembered history. Many of these tales are tied to geographical or ecological features such as rocks, mountains or reefs, which the myths present as the petrified remains of supernatural beings.

A central idea is Hawaiki, a mythical island where Polynesians place their origins and where several myths unfold. The word has two meanings. On the one hand, it refers to a life to come, where the souls of the dead go. On the other, it names an ancestral land lying to the west. That westward direction makes sense, since migration routes ran eastward from Southeast Asia, so the homeland was always behind the travellers. Its real location is still debated. The anthropologist R. C. Suggs proposed Tonga and Samoa, at the western edge of the region, as the original Hawaiki. More recent work drawing on linguistics and genetics has also suggested Taiwan.

Before writing, these stories lived in the spoken word, and speech has a flexibility that paper does not allow. The myths recount the ancient time known as pō, and the adventures of the gods, or atua, and of deified ancestors. They make heavy use of allegory, metaphor, parable, hyperbole and personification. There is no fixed version of any tale. A story could change within certain limits depending on the setting and on the needs of the teller and the listeners. Oral literature, moreover, was less concerned with understanding the past for its own sake than with justifying the present order of things.

Genealogies are a good example. They exist in several versions, often contradicting one another. Their purpose was not to give a true account of events but to stress the seniority of the ruling chiefly line, and with it that line's political legitimacy and its right to use the land and its resources. When a new line rose to power, it needed a genealogy of equal prestige, so it might add generations or borrow a few ancestors from the previous dynasty. A cautious narrator would often prefer allusion to direct statement. Each island, tribe or clan therefore kept its own version of the same narrative cycle.

Writing changed this dynamic. When missionaries, officials, anthropologists and ethnologists collected and published these accounts, they inevitably altered their nature. By fixing on paper what had once varied almost endlessly, they turned the words of a single narrator, at a single moment, into the authoritative version. In New Zealand, the writings of one chief, Wiremu Te Rangikāheke, formed the basis of much of Governor George Grey's Polynesian Mythology, a book that still provides the de facto official versions of many of the best-known Māori legends.

Some Polynesians understood the power of the new medium early. From the mid-19th century, a number of them wrote down their genealogies and the history and origins of their tribes. These documents, called pukapuka whakapapa in Māori, or puta tumu and puta tūpuna in tropical Polynesia, were jealously guarded by heads of households, and many were lost or destroyed. In the 1890s, Makea Takau, a chief of Rarotonga, ordered his people to burn all their family books except his own. His version thus became the official history of the chiefly line, and the possibility of dissent disappeared. At his request, extracts were published in the Journal of the Polynesian Society.

Today these stories serve very different purposes. Some reconstructions are made for tourists, while others are part of efforts to renew cultural identity. Genealogies also have practical consequences. In the Cook Islands, and to a lesser extent in New Zealand, much of current land law rests on them. Later, New Zealand's authorities set up land courts to determine ownership and inheritance rights, drawing on systematic collections of traditional histories. Because these sources are numerous and sometimes incompatible, they have been endlessly contested and have given rise to many lawsuits. Studying Polynesian mythology therefore means distinguishing between the story, the written version and the uses to which both are put today.
