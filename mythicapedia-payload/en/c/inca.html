Viracocha, Inti and the layered worlds of the Inca Empire

Inca mythology

Inca mythology gathered the stories and beliefs of the people who ruled from Cusco over a vast part of the Andes. It drew on older traditions such as Chavín, Paracas, Moche, Nazca and the Huarochirí Manuscript, and it explained the origin of the world, the order of the cosmos and the links between people, earth and sky. Today it is known mainly through chronicles, art and the myths that have survived among Andean peoples.

Inca mythology is the body of stories, beliefs and collective memory of the Inca civilization, which came to span lands that today belong to Colombia, Ecuador, Peru, Bolivia, Chile and Argentina. The empire first absorbed the central highlands of Peru and then pushed southward, and its success rested as much on political, military and commercial power as on a religious framework that gave very different peoples something in common. It was less a closed doctrine than a patchwork of traditions organized around Cusco, the capital from which worship was coordinated.

The Andean religious world is far older than the empire itself. The Huarochirí Manuscript, a seventeenth-century text that records the myths and beliefs of a province in the western Andes, shows how many stories came from much earlier periods. Other pre-Inca cultures, including Chavín, Paracas, Moche and Nazca, left images and objects that help us trace that inheritance. Among their most important figures was Viracocha, a creator deity who, even in Inca times, was regarded as the origin of all things and was closely linked with the sea.

The Incas did not leave a writing system in the usual sense, and that shapes what we can know about their myths. After the Spanish conquest, colonial officials burned many of the records that had been kept, so what survives comes mainly from what priests and chroniclers wrote down, from the iconography of pottery and architecture, and from oral traditions. The quipus, cords with knots used for counting and recording, are another piece of the puzzle. The researcher Gary Urton has proposed that they could have encoded phonological or logographic information, a hypothesis that is still debated.

The best-known story is the founding of the Inca dynasty, centred on Manco Cápac. The sources do not agree about his origins. In one version he was the son of Viracocha; in another, he was raised from the depths of Lake Titicaca by the sun god Inti. A third tradition makes Manco and his brother Pacha Kamaq sons of the sun god. Commoners were reportedly forbidden to say the name of Viracocha, which may help explain why there are three founding legends instead of one.

One of the most detailed versions places Manco Cápac and his siblings at Paqariq Tampu, south of Cusco. According to the myth, they came out of the cave of Puma Orco carrying a golden staff, the Topayauri. They were to build a temple to the Sun wherever the staff sank into the ground. On the journey, one brother, Ayar Cachi, was tricked into going back and was sealed inside a huaca, or, in another tradition, turned to stone or ice, because his reckless and cruel conduct had angered the peoples they were trying to rule. Some versions add that the young Manco, out of jealousy, betrayed and killed his older brothers before seizing power in Cusco.

The chronicler Pedro Sarmiento de Gamboa recorded another account, in which eight men and women emerged from a hill called Tambotoco, about 33 kilometres from Cusco. A later variant placed the origin in the waters of Lake Titicaca. Some scholars have suggested that this story may have been used to draw the powerful Aymara peoples into the Tawantinsuyu, although that is a modern interpretation rather than something stated in the sources.

Andean cosmology divided space on two planes. Horizontally, the world was split into upper and lower halves, called Hanan and Hurin, and each half was split again, producing a fourfold pattern understood as complementarity, opposition and reciprocity. Vertically, the world was organized into three pachas. The Hanan Pacha was the upper, celestial world, home to Viracocha, Inti, Mama Quilla and Illapa. Only the just could enter it, crossing a bridge made of hair. The Kay Pacha was the world of the present, where humans live and where deities such as Pachamama, lady of the earth, and Mama Sara, lady of fruits and plants, are found. The Hurin Pacha was the world below, of the dead and of whatever lies beneath the surface of the earth or the sea. There dwelt Supay, Mama Cocha and Pachacámac, among others. Spanish chronicles often present it as a hell, but the traditions describe it more as another dwelling place, connected to the living world through springs, caves and other openings.

The texts also mention further levels. The Uku Pacha, the world within, of eternal darkness, was often confused with the Hurin Pacha in the chronicles. The Hawa Pacha, the outer world, exists but cannot be perceived by the senses. The Hakaq Pacha, the beyond or the world of spirits, is also mentioned.

Landscape carried great weight in these stories. Lake Titicaca appears as the place where the world began, and many Andean peaks were considered apus, great lords or spirits of the mountains, to whom offerings were made. One myth links the mountain Paxil with the origin of human beings, created from maize kernels scattered by the gods. The sky was also part of this sacred geography. The Milky Way was read as a river with animals along its banks, and constellations such as the Great Llama and the Fox fitted into that reading. Myths were passed on by word of mouth, so sound had a special value: in the creation story, the voice of Viracocha is decisive.

In the realm of the gods, pre-Hispanic Andean mythology tended to personify the stars and great natural phenomena. Viracocha was the only god in the full sense, a creator and lord of the world. His cult was reserved largely for the nobility and for the wise men known as amautas. According to some oral traditions, he had four sons: Kon or Wakon, Mallku, Vichama and Pachacámac. Inti, the Sun, was regarded as Viracocha's son and as a patron of the empire. He received many offerings, and the Sapa Inca claimed descent from him. According to the reference text, his prominence grew after the Incas defeated the Chancas during the reign of Pachacútec, to whom that victory was credited. Mama Quilla, the Moon, protected women in particular, and Pachamama brought fertility to the fields.

Illapa deserves separate attention. His name means lightning, but he was much more than a thunder god. He governed the atmospheric sky, rain and weather, and he was also lord of war. He sent storms, hail and floods as punishment to those who failed their duties, yet his rain fertilized the land and made crops flourish. For this reason he was linked to life, agriculture and fertility. In some traditions he was identified with other figures, such as Catequil, god of thunder and lightning in the northern Peruvian highlands.

The empire allowed the peoples it absorbed to keep their own religions, so the pantheon was layered. There were official gods, gods of the ayllus, the kin groups, and local divinities of mountains, lakes and springs. Among them were Amaru, a sacred serpent associated with wisdom, water and the underworld; Huallallo Carhuincho, a fire god of the Wankas who, in one myth, was punished for trying to devour a boy who turned out to be Inti; and Ekeko, a protector of the household and of wealth.

Today the Quechua peoples of Peru and Bolivia and the Kichwa of Ecuador share a devotion to the Sun and a view of the landscape as a place filled with living presences. For their communities these beliefs are not only a past history but a living part of their relationship with the earth and the sky.

Trivia

A golden staff. Some versions of the Manco Cápac myth say the founders carried a golden staff, the Topayauri, which sank into the earth and marked the spot where a temple to the Sun was to be built.
Three origin stories. Because commoners could not say the name of Viracocha, there are three different foundation legends instead of one, each with its own account of the origin of the Incas.
A sky full of animals. The Milky Way was read as a river, and among its stars people saw animals such as the Great Llama and the Fox, which featured in their stories and in the way they read the sky.
