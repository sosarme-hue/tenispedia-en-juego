Spiritual traditions, revival and resistance

Native American religions

Native American religions are a varied set of beliefs, practices and ceremonies that were passed down orally for centuries. They share ideas such as a supernatural world full of beings and powers, and the world tree as a central symbol, although each people expresses them in its own way. Their history has been shaped by persecution and forced conversion, but also by revival movements that remain alive today.

Native American religions, also called Indigenous American faiths, are a family of spiritual practices that differ considerably from one people to another. Ceremonies share a sense of the sacred, yet they vary across the continent, and early European explorers described communities that each had their own religious customs. Their theologies may be monotheistic, polytheistic, henotheistic, animistic, shamanistic or pantheistic, or combine several of these. Most of these beliefs were transmitted by word of mouth, in the form of myths, oral histories, stories, allegories and principles of conduct.

A feature shared by many of these traditions is the belief in a supernatural world inhabited by deities, spirits and wonders. Concepts such as the Great Spirit, the Fifth World, the world tree and the red road, which guides a person’s life, appear among many peoples. Before Christianization, most of these religions recognized a supreme being, a creator or teacher, or a sky deity from whom all other spiritual beings emanated. Sources cite the Inca god Viracocha and Tirawa, the god of the Pawnee, as examples. This supreme being is closely linked to the world tree or to a central cosmic pole, which served as the main ritual symbol in the great ceremonies, especially in northern North America.

Before contact with Europeans, the most widespread rites were connected with hunting, gathering, planting and war. Many of these ceremonies aimed to secure the success of an activity or to ask the beings of the natural world for permission to hunt or to plant. Today, many practitioners do not see religion as a separate sphere of life. For them, spirituality extends into politics, law, health, education and environmental care, so community decisions and beliefs form a single whole.

From the sixteenth century to the present, Native spirituality has given rise to many movements of renewal. Scholars often distinguish between fundamentalist movements, which favoured a return to traditional ways and rejected the changes imposed by modern states, and reform or syncretic movements, which blended Indigenous and Christian elements. Among the first group are the Pueblo Revolt of the 1680s, the Shawnee Prophet Movement, White Path’s Rebellion, and the two Ghost Dances, the first from 1869 to 1870 and the second from 1889 to 1890. Among the second, sources mention the Longhouse Religion, the Indian Shaker Church, the Native American Church and the Wocekiye religion.

From the 1600s, Catholic and Protestant missionaries sought to convert Native peoples to Christianity. After the United States became independent, its government kept suppressing Indigenous practices and promoted forcible conversion. Government agencies and religious organizations in the United States and Canada often cooperated in these efforts, and in many cases violence was used, as in the suppression of the Ghost Dance and the Sun Dance in the 1890s. Canada did not lift its ban on the full Sun Dance ceremony until 1951. Around the start of the twentieth century, the American government turned to less violent methods, although a series of federal laws banned practices such as feasts, Sun Dance ceremonies and the use of the sweat lodge. This persecution officially lasted until 1978, when the American Indian Religious Freedom Act was passed, though some argue that it had little real effect on protecting Native beliefs.

Another tool of suppression was the removal of Native children from their families and their placement in government-funded boarding schools run by Christian churches. There, children were forced, often violently, to adopt Christian beliefs, the values of mainstream white culture and English, while being forbidden to speak their languages or practise their own beliefs. This system continued into the 1970s.

The number of practitioners is hard to pin down. Some non-Native anthropologists estimate that around 9000 people belonged to traditional Native American religions in the twenty-first century. Because these communities rarely have public organizations or membership lists, that figure is probably far below the real number of participants. Native spiritual leaders also point out that a century of persecution led many believers to practise in secret, and that many also attend Christian services, which complicates any count.

Traditions vary a great deal by region. In Alaska and the far north, the peoples of the Arctic and subarctic practised cults linked to hunting and gathering, and shamanism mediated between people and spirits, souls and other immortal beings. The west includes cultural areas such as the Northwest Coast, the Plateau, the Great Basin and California. There the Earth Lodge Religion emerged, spreading among peoples such as the Wintun and the Shasta and predicting events similar to those of the Ghost Dance, such as the return of ancestors or the end of the world. Around 1850, the Wanapum prophet Smohalla founded the Washat Dreamers Religion in the Columbia River region. Its followers held the Waashat Dance, with seven drummers, a salmon feast and sacred songs. Smohalla urged a return to a hunting and gathering way of life, the rejection of alcohol and of imposed farming, and the sources record his famous words against ploughing the earth. Smohalla was jailed by authorities who wanted to prevent a possible uprising, but his religion has continued.

The Ghost Dance of the late nineteenth century was a revitalization movement in the western United States. It began as a local ceremony in Nevada, founded by the Paiute prophet Wodziwob, but it gained wide popularity between 1889 and 1890 through Wovoka, also a Northern Paiute. According to his account, after falling ill during an eclipse of the sun on 1 January 1889, he visited the spirit world and announced that the world would end and be restored to a pure state, where Native people would live forever, including the dead. He asked people to live honestly, avoid alcohol and the ways of white people, and pray, sing and dance instead of mourning. His followers came to see him as a kind of messiah. The movement spread among the Shoshone, Arapaho, Cheyenne and Sioux, and some Lakota and Dakota bands, living through forced relocation, became more militant. In December 1890, a gathering of dancers at Wounded Knee was invaded by the Seventh Cavalry, and many Lakota and Dakota people, mostly women, children and elders, were killed. Some communities, such as the Caddo Nation, still practise the Ghost Dance today, though often in private.

In the Great Plains, the traditions of more than fifty nations are extremely varied and complex. Wocekiye, practised among the Dakota and Lakota, combines traditional religion with forms of Christianity. In the northeast, the Algonquian, Iroquoian and Siouan-speaking peoples played a prominent role. The Midewiwin, or Grand Medicine Society, and the Wabunowin, or Dawn Society, are traditional religious organizations of the Anishinaabe, or Ojibwe, and other peoples of the Great Lakes region. The Longhouse Religion, known as the Code of Handsome Lake, was founded in 1797 or 1799 by the Seneca prophet Handsome Lake. It blended traditional Seneca beliefs with rules that forbade drinking, the sale of land, intensive animal farming and witchcraft.

In the southeast, peoples of the southern woodlands practised mainly the Green Corn Ceremony and other agricultural cults. In 1858 the Cherokee Keetoowah Society was formed as a religious and political organization that aimed to preserve traditional ways of life, and around 1895 the Four Mothers Society was founded, bringing together Muscogee, Cherokee, Choctaw and Chickasaw people. In the southwestern United States and in Mexico, peoples practised maize-centred cults. Mexicayotl, a Nahuatl term meaning the essence of being Mexican, is a movement that seeks to revive the religion, philosophy and traditions of ancient Mexico, including Aztec religion. It emerged in the 1950s among intellectuals in Mexico City and has spread in recent years among urban populations and among Chicanos in North America.

Today, many communities work to keep their languages, ceremonies and sacred places. Since 1978, some of those sites have been protected by law in the United States, although the AIRFA has not always translated into strong protection for beliefs. The legacy of these religions can be seen in the defence of land, in community health and environmental care, and in the growing interest of younger generations in their own traditions.

Trivia

An eclipse and a vision. Wovoka, leader of the 1889 Ghost Dance, said he fell ill during a solar eclipse on 1 January 1889 and that, after recovering, he had visited the spirit world.
A dance with seven drummers. The Waashat Dance of the Washat Dreamers Religion required seven drummers and was held every seventh day, alongside a salmon feast.
A key year for legal protection. The American Indian Religious Freedom Act was passed in 1978, the year that officially ended federal bans on many traditional practices.
