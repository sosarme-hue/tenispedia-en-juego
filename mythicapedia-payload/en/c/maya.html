Gods, heroes and the underworld of Xibalba in Mesoamerican tradition

Maya mythology

Maya mythology is a body of stories about gods, heroes and personified forces of nature that were told for centuries across Mesoamerica. Its best-known written source is the Popol Vuh, while most other myths have to be reconstructed from images, hieroglyphs and the accounts of Spanish chroniclers. Many of these tales explain the origin of the world, of humankind and of the heavenly bodies, and set out how people should relate to their surroundings.

Maya mythology is part of the wider mythology of Mesoamerica, and it gathers the stories in which personified natural forces, gods and heroes interact with one another. Much of what we know comes from texts written after the arrival of the Spanish, such as the Popol Vuh of the K'iche' people and the books known as the Chilam Balam. Earlier myths must be pieced together mainly from the images on temple walls, stone monuments and painted pottery, along with a few hieroglyphic inscriptions. Other parts of the oral tradition, such as animal tales and moral stories, fall outside this overview.

Many myths explain the origins of natural and cultural phenomena: the Sun, the Moon, Venus, the Pleiades and the Milky Way; mountains, clouds, rain, thunder and lightning; wild and domestic animals; the colours of maize; illnesses and the herbs that cure them; farming tools; and the steam bath. They often carry a moral lesson, since they define the proper ritual relationship between people and the natural world.

The Popol Vuh describes creation as a series of attempts. According to this text, animals came first, but they could neither speak nor honour their makers. Humans were then shaped from wet clay, which dissolved in the rain, and then from wood. The wooden beings could talk and reproduce, but they had no soul and no memory, so they were destroyed. The tradition holds that monkeys descend from this wooden race. Finally the gods made the first people from maize dough: yellow maize formed the flesh and red maize the blood. These beings were so wise that they resembled the gods, who felt threatened and sent a mist to dim their sight.

Tepeu and Kukulkán appear as the creators, the makers and the ancestors, described as among the first beings to exist and as very wise. Kukulkán is the name of the feathered serpent, the same figure the Aztecs called Quetzalcoatl. Huracán, the 'heart of the sky', is less personified and acts as the storm itself. In some versions, Huracán caused a great flood after the first people angered the gods, and he kept repeating the word 'earth' until land rose out of the waters. The sources do not agree on who took part in each attempt or on the names of the gods, and scholars have noted differences between one text and another.

The best-known cycle is that of the Hero Twins, Hunahpú and Ixbalanqué. According to the Popol Vuh, their father and uncle had played ball on the court of the lords of Xibalba, the underworld, and had been defeated and killed. The head of one of them was hung in a tree, and a maiden from the underworld who passed by was spat on by it and became pregnant. She gave birth to the twins, who suffered the scorn of their older half brothers. After a series of tricks, the twins outwitted the lords of the underworld in tests such as the burning pit, where they died, only for their ashes, thrown into the river, to bring them back to life. They later returned to Xibalba in disguise and overcame its inhabitants. In the end they became the Sun and the Moon. In the same story the older brothers are turned into monkeys.

Other texts preserve different themes. The Book of Chilam Balam of Chumayel speaks of the collapse of the sky and a deluge, followed by the killing of the earth crocodile, the raising of the sky and the setting up of five World Trees. A crocodile named Itzam Cab Ain, linked to flooding, appears in the Chilam Balam books of Maní and Tizimín, where it is defeated when its throat is cut. The Dresden Codex, on page 74, shows a water-spewing celestial dragon that is generally thought to be causing a deluge.

According to the Spanish priests, Xibalba was a dangerous realm of sickness and death. It was said to lie beneath the surface, its road steep and thorny and forbidden to outsiders. Tradition placed its entrance in a cave in Alta Verapaz, near Cobán, though no one has ever firmly located it. Some descriptions link it to the roots of the cosmic tree Yaxché, whose four roots reach toward the four directions. It was divided into nine levels ruled by the Bolontikú, the 'Nine Lords of the Night'. Above them stood the kings Hun-Camé and Vucub-Camé, and under their command were ten princes whose task was to bring illness, misfortune and death. Sources do not always agree on how many of these lords there were or what they were called.

Among the death deities, Āh Puch is the god and king of Xibalba, shown with a skull for a head, bare ribs and a spine that juts out, and ornaments of gold or copper bells. His name also appears as Yum Kimil or Cizin, and the latter has been connected with stench and decay. Studies of the hieroglyphs have made it possible to separate the so-called God A, who seems to stand for bodily decay and for a lord of death, from God A1, or Lajiin, linked with self-sacrifice by decapitation. These figures reveal what the ancient Maya thought about life after death and where they might end up.

Other deities hold important places in the pantheon. Itzamná is the creator god and god of the heavens, credited with teaching people language. Chaac is the god of rain, fertility and farming, while Ixchel is the Moon goddess and wife of the solar god. Ah Mun is the maize god, shown as a young man carrying an ear of corn. The Bacabs hold up the sky and are associated with beekeeping and honey. Names and roles shift from one region and period to another, and it is not always easy to know what each god did in each place.

The Tonsured Maize God is one of the most studied figures. Some researchers see him as an earlier form of the Hero Twins' father, Hun-Hunahpú; others treat him as a hero in his own right. His link with water and rain, and scenes in which he appears among naked women, have led to different readings. Another group of stories, which appear older in the images, shows heroes killing jaguars, animals that could stand for hostile groups.

These readings call for caution. In the 1970s Michael D. Coe identified several characters of the Popol Vuh on ceramics, and many scholars then began to read almost every scene through that book. Linda Schele, using fragments of inscriptions, composed a cosmogonic myth about a First Father that still awaits visual confirmation. More recent work by Oswaldo Chinchilla Mazariegos, published in 2011 and 2017, has widened the analysis to other Mesoamerican tales. Today Maya peoples are very much alive, and many of their stories survive in communities that pass them on to new generations, while their religious life is lived by believers as faith.

Trivia

The four Chacs. In Chan Kom, in Yucatán, the four pillars of the sky are called the four Chacs, a reference to the rain god.
Bells in the wells. Large quantities of gold or copper bells have been found in wells where victims were thought to have been thrown, alongside images linked to Āh Puch.
A turtle in the weaving. Among the Kaweq families of Rabinal, in Baja Verapaz, the turtle Piyakok appears in women's woven bands, and it also gives its name to a local species of pond turtle.
