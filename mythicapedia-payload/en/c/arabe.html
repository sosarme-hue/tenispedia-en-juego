Gods, shrines and religious communities before Islam

Pre-Islamic Arabian religion

Before Islam, most people in Arabia followed polytheistic religions centred on local shrines and on gods such as Hubal and al-Lāt. From the fourth century, Judaism and Christianity spread through the region, with Zoroastrianism present to a lesser degree, while many inscriptions show a growing invocation of a single god. The sources are scarce and partly external, so what we know of its myths is limited.

Until about the fourth century, nearly all inhabitants of the Arabian Peninsula followed polytheistic faiths. There was no single religion, however, and no shared body of doctrine. What existed was a patchwork of local and regional cults, each with its own gods and spirits, sacred places, objects, ritual specialists such as diviners, and practices such as pilgrimage.

Worship centred on local shrines and temples, such as the Kaaba in Mecca or the Temple of Awwam in Yemen. Believers invoked their deities through pilgrimages, divination and ritual sacrifice. Many of the physical descriptions of gods and goddesses come from idols, especially those near the Kaaba, which is said to have held as many as 360.

Our knowledge comes from several kinds of source. There are inscriptions in scripts such as Safaitic, Sabaic and Paleo-Arabic, pre-Islamic poetry, and accounts by Greek and Jewish writers. There is also the Muslim tradition, including the Qur'an and later Islamic writings. Even so, the information is limited. The Annals of Esarhaddon, from ancient Assyria, mention names such as Atarsamain, Nukhay, Ruldaiu and Atarquruma. Herodotus wrote that the Arabs worshipped Orotalt, whom he identified with Dionysus, and Alilat, whom he identified with Aphrodite. Strabo spoke of Dionysus and Zeus, and Origen of Dionysus and Urania. These accounts do not agree, which makes it hard to reconstruct a single pantheon.

One of the most substantial sources is the Book of Idols by Hisham ibn al-Kalbi, written in the eighth century. The scholar F. E. Peters regarded it as the fullest treatment of pre-Islamic Arabian religious practice. According to al-Kalbi, descendants of Ishmael who had settled in Mecca took holy stones from the Kaaba to other lands, set them up and circled them as they did the Kaaba. In his account, this custom gave rise to idol worship. Some scholars infer from this that Arabs first venerated stones and only later adopted images of gods under foreign influence. A third-century Syriac homily attributed to Pseudo-Meliton describes the pagan beliefs of Syriac speakers in northern Mesopotamia, mostly Arabs, and shows the link between a god and the stone that represented him. The full myths and the meaning of the divine epithets, however, remain poorly understood.

The kingdoms of southern Arabia built large pantheons. The god ʿAthtar often held the highest rank, while each people had its own patron: Almaqah among the Sabaeans, Wadd among the Minaeans, ʿAmm among the Qatabanians and Sayin among the Hadramites. These patron gods were closely tied to politics and social organization. In less complex societies pantheons were smaller and centred on a local patron, such as Dhu Ghaba at Dedan or Dushara among the Nabataeans. Other deities, including al-ʿUzzā, Manāt, Allāt, Hubal, Shayʿ al-Qawm and the Egyptian goddess Isis, appear in particular regional, political or trading contexts.

The Safaitic inscriptions, left by nomadic groups in the north, reveal a very practical religious life. Worshippers asked their gods for safety, help, reunion with family or companions, relief, vengeance, healing, protection on journeys and success in risky ventures. Animal sacrifice appears often, in raids, travels and uncertain situations. The inscriptions also mention cult stones, offerings, vows, oaths, pilgrimage, ritual shelters and sacred water. Because the texts are short, they do not allow historians to reconstruct the theology or the myths of these religions in detail.

In the fourth century, as Arabia moved into late antiquity, belief in and invocation of a single god became increasingly common. In the south, almost all gods stop being mentioned, except Rahmanan in the second half of the fourth century. In the north and west, inscriptions begin to invoke only Allah in the fifth and sixth centuries. These shifts coincided with the rise of Christianity and Judaism, though not all monotheists were Christian or Jewish. Islamic sources use the term hanif for monotheists who were neither Jews nor Christians. Some recent researchers argue that henotheism or monotheism was dominant from the fourth century, in contrast to the older view that polytheism remained the norm until much later.

Jewish communities had been in Arabia for some time. From Roman times, Jewish migrants settled and formed both sedentary and nomadic tribes. They spoke Arabic as well as Hebrew and Aramaic, and kept in touch with religious centres in Babylonia and Palestine. The Himyarites of Yemen converted to Judaism in the fourth century, and some of the Kinda did so in the fourth and fifth centuries. Jewish tribes lived in the main towns, including Tayma, Khaybar and Medina, and sources count twenty Jewish tribes in the peninsula in Muhammad's time. Some Arab women in Yathrib reportedly vowed that their child would be a Jew if it survived, since they saw Jews as a people of knowledge and the book. The weekly market day, called aruba, fell the day before the Jewish Sabbath, and some scholars think this may have influenced the choice of Friday for the Muslim congregational prayer.

Christianity was also present, mainly on the northeastern, northwestern and southern edges of the peninsula. The Ghassanids, a client kingdom of Rome, converted to Christianity. In the south, Najran became a Christian centre under the influence of the Kingdom of Aksum, across the Red Sea. A family from the Banu Harith built a large church there, Deir Najran, known as the Ka'ba of Najran. The Ghassanids and the southern Christians adopted Monophysitism, while the Lakhmids, clients of the Sasanians, followed Nestorianism. In the Persian Gulf region, Christianity gained ground from the fourth century, with monasteries and a diocesan structure. The northeast was called Beth Qatraye, and Oman with what is now the United Arab Emirates formed the diocese of Beth Mazunaye, with Sohar as its central city.

Iranian religions also left traces. There is no clear surviving physical evidence, but Persian military and commercial presence may have brought Iranian ideas, and the Qur'an contains Persian loanwords such as firdaws, meaning paradise. Zoroastrianism was practised in Bahrain, in Persian-ruled Oman and in Persian-ruled Yemen, where the descendants of the Abna, the Persian conquerors, kept the faith. The historian al-Baladhuri mentions Yemeni Zoroastrians who had to pay the jizya after the Muslim conquest. Other accounts, such as that of al-Yaʿqubi, say the Sasanian king Kavad I persuaded the ruler of Kinda to promote Mazdakism. References to Manichaeism are doubtful, and recent research has not been able to prove that it was widespread in Mecca in the sixth and seventh centuries.

Islamic sources also mention Buddhist idols in the Kaaba and Arabs and Persians who considered themselves students of the Buddha. Rashid al-Din Hamadani, in the fourteenth century, and al-Masudi report such claims, and a thirteenth-century Islamic miniature shows Muhammad destroying Hindu and Buddhist idols. Mostafa Vaziri has suggested that Buddhism may have reached Arabia through Indian merchants, although these references are hard to confirm.

Trivia

A number of idols. According to Islamic sources, the Kaaba was said to hold up to 360 idols, a figure that recurs in the tradition and hints at how varied local worship once was.
From stones to idols. In al-Kalbi's Book of Idols, holy stones carried from Mecca were set up and circled like the Kaaba. Some scholars read this story as a possible step from stone veneration to images of gods.
An early written mention. The Annals of Esarhaddon, from ancient Assyria, are among the earliest written references to Arabian deities, naming figures such as Atarsamain and Nukhay.
