The stories of the Celtic peoples, from the Iron Age to medieval literature

Celtic mythology

Celtic mythology gathers the religious stories of peoples who shared a polytheistic faith during the Iron Age, although they never formed a single political state. Much of what we know comes from Roman writers, inscriptions and Christian monks who, in the Middle Ages, wrote down Irish and Welsh oral traditions. Today it is studied through fragmentary remains, with careful interpretation.

Celtic mythology is the body of myths and sacred figures belonging to the Celtic peoples. During the Iron Age, like other Europeans of the time, the Celts followed a polytheistic religion with many gods and goddesses. They were never politically united, though. Their world stretched across much of western and central Europe, and religious practice varied greatly from one region to another. That is why it is more accurate to speak of related mythologies than of a single system.

The main difficulty in studying them is the lack of sources written by the Celts themselves. The continental Celts, such as the Gauls and the Celtiberians, left few written texts. The Gauls did know writing, and objects with inscriptions in Gaulish have survived, along with the Coligny calendar. Julius Caesar says that the druids refused to commit certain sacred verses to writing, yet he also notes that other Gauls used writing for administrative matters. After the Roman conquest, the continental Celtic languages disappeared and their religion stopped being practised, so what remains comes mostly from Greco-Roman authors, inscriptions dating from after the conquest and archaeology.

In Ireland and parts of Wales, the Ogham alphabet was used for short inscriptions, usually personal names. More sophisticated literacy did not reach the Celtic areas that Rome had never conquered until Christianity arrived. Many Gaelic stories were recorded by Christian monks, who passed them on without most of their original religious meaning and adapted them to their own worldview.

Continental sources mention many deities, but nearly all seem to have been local or tribal gods, known only in one area. Inscriptions name more than three hundred of them, many linked to springs, forests or particular places. Some names come from classical writers. The Roman poet Lucan mentions Taranis, Teutates and Esus, although there is little Celtic evidence that they were major gods. The Syrian rhetorician Lucian describes Ogmios, who was said to lead a band of men chained by their ears to his tongue, a symbol of the power of eloquence. Among the images with no known name are a three-headed god, a squatting god and a god with a serpent. Some have been found in Late Bronze Age peat bogs in Britain, which shows they predate Roman rule and were widely spread.

The most cited account of the gods of Gaul comes from Julius Caesar's history of his campaigns there. He names five principal gods, giving Roman equivalents. Mercury was the most revered, the inventor of all the arts and the patron of travellers and traders, and many scholars link him to the god Lugus. Then came Apollo, who drove away illness; Mars, who governed war; Jupiter, who ruled the sky; and Minerva, who encouraged skills. Caesar adds that the Gauls claimed descent from a god he compares to Dis Pater. The scholar MacBain proposed correspondences with Irish gods: Apollo with Lugh, Mercury with Manannán mac Lir, Jupiter with the Dagda, Mars with Neit and Minerva with Brigit. These are hypotheses, not certainties.

The best preserved mythology belongs to the Gaelic languages, covering Ireland, Scotland and the Isle of Man, and to the Brittonic languages, covering Wales, Cornwall and Brittany. The Irish body of myth is the largest. Its stories are traditionally grouped into cycles. The Mythological Cycle, or Cycle of the Gods, tells of the Túatha Dé Danann, a supernatural people often identified with the Irish deities. They appear as kings, queens, druids, bards, warriors, healers and craftsmen with supernatural powers. Among them are the Dagda, 'the great god' and probably the chief deity; the Morrígan, a triple goddess linked to war, fate and sovereignty; Lugh; Nuada; Aengus; Brigid; Manannán; Dian Cecht the healer; and Goibniu the smith, one of the Trí Dé Dána, or 'three gods of craft'. Their traditional enemies were the monstrous Fomorians, defeated at the battle of Mag Tuired. The Lebor Gabála Érenn, or 'Book of Invasions', offers a legendary history of Ireland, while Aided Chlainne Lir, 'The Death of the Children of Lir', tells the tragic fate of the children of a sea god.

The Ulster Cycle gathers heroic legends about the Ulaid. It centres on King Conchobar mac Nessa, his court at Emain Macha and the hero Cú Chulainn, who fights the Connachta and their queen Medb. Its longest and most important work is the epic Táin Bó Cúailnge, or 'The Cattle Raid of Cooley'. The Fenian Cycle, by contrast, follows Fionn and his band of warriors, the Fianna, and includes Acallam na Senórach, 'Tales of the Elders'. The Cycle of the Kings concerns historical or semi-historical rulers and the origins of dynasties and peoples, as in Buile Shuibhne, 'The Madness of King Sweeny'. Outside the cycles sit the echtrai, tales of journeys to the Otherworld such as the voyage of Bran, and the Dindsenchas, or 'lore of places', which explains the names of particular sites.

In Wales, British mythology survives mainly in the Four Branches of the Mabinogi, in names such as Rhiannon, Teyrnon and Brân the Blessed. Other episodes, such as the appearance of Arawn, king of the Otherworld, who asks a mortal to help him in his feuds, or the hero who can only be killed under seemingly contradictory conditions, have parallels across Proto-Indo-European mythology. The children of Llŷr and the children of Dôn, the latter linked to the Irish Danu, are major figures, though these tales are not primary mythology. The tale of Culhwch and Olwen names Mabon ap Modron, 'Divine Son of the Divine Mother'. Even so, too little is known of the British background to rebuild a creation narrative or a coherent pantheon, and there may never have been a single British mythological tradition.

Several motifs run through the insular Celtic world. The Otherworld is a parallel realm inhabited by supernatural beings, which some heroes visit. The sovereignty goddess embodies the land and grants kingship to a ruler by marrying him. Celtic myth also shaped the medieval Arthurian legend, which includes magic cauldrons, otherworldly feasts and realms beyond death. Such parallels, together with the links between Irish, Welsh and continental names, such as Lugh and Lugus, Goibniu and Gobannos, or Mabon and Maponos, suggest shared roots, though they do not allow us to reconstruct one unified religion.
