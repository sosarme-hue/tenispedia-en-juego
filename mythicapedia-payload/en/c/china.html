Gods, heroes and worlds of ancient China

Chinese mythology

Chinese mythology is a body of stories passed down orally or recorded in literature across the area now known as China. Its myths appear scattered through works of history, philosophy and poetry, and many gods were later reinterpreted as virtuous rulers. Among the best-known tales are Pangu's creation of the world, Nüwa's repair of the sky and Yu's struggle against the floods.

Chinese mythology has no single book devoted to it. Ancient China did not produce works dedicated solely to myths. Instead, the stories surface in texts of history, philosophy and poetry, usually as passing mentions of characters and of the deeds attributed to them. This scattered transmission has consequences. The stories come from different periods and dynasties, and they belong to different schools of thought, among them Daoism, Confucianism, Legalism and Mohism. As a result, the same figure may appear with quite different traits depending on who is telling the story. Much of what survives comes from texts that date mainly to the Han dynasty.

A notable feature of these sources is that many major deities were reinterpreted by their authors in line with philosophical ideas. Figures with supernatural powers were turned into virtuous sovereigns who had reigned in remote times. This tendency sits alongside a more openly mythological view, so that the same character can be both an exemplary ruler and a being capable of reshaping the world. Ancient texts also linked the gods to the five directions: the four cardinal points and the centre, which was the place of the emperor. This cosmological scheme took shape in early antiquity.

Myths about creation arrive late in Chinese literature, after Confucianism, Daoism and popular religion had already taken form. The versions do not agree and sometimes contradict one another. Depending on the source, the creation of the first beings is credited to Shangdi, to Heaven (Tian), to Nüwa, to Pangu, to the Three Pure Ones or to the Jade Emperor. Shangdi appears in literature around 700 BCE or earlier, depending on how the Shujing, the Classic of History, is dated. For a long time he is described in personal terms without being considered a creator, a role he does not clearly take on until the Han dynasty. Over time Shangdi and Heaven came to be worshipped as a single entity, as at the Temple of Heaven in Beijing, although scholars have not settled exactly where one ends and the other begins.

The story of Pangu is perhaps the most famous. It does not appear in literature before 200 CE. According to this myth, at first there was only a shapeless chaos, out of which came an egg that had taken 18,000 years to form. When the yin and yang forces were in balance, Pangu emerged from the egg and set about creating the world. With an axe he divided the two principles: the heavy yin sank to form the earth, while the light yang rose to form the sky. Pangu stayed between them for another 18,000 years, holding the sky up, and then rested. His body became the rest of the world. His breath turned into the wind, his voice into thunder, his left eye into the sun and his right eye into the moon. Mountains, rivers, fertile fields, forests and precious minerals came from other parts of his body. His sweat fell as rain, and the tiny creatures living on his body, fleas in some versions, were carried off by the wind and became human beings.

Nüwa appears around 350 BCE and is often paired with Fuxi, her companion, and at times the two are honoured as the last ancestors of humanity. In different accounts Nüwa made human beings and, in another story, mended the sky when it was torn, using stones of seven colours. The patch left in the sky became the rainbow. Fuxi is credited with inventing writing, fishing and hunting. Nüwa also appears alongside Yu, who built the channels that brought a great flood under control. The flood motif is shared with other traditions, including Sumerian, Greek, Maya and Jewish ones, and in the Chinese case Yu is presented above all as an organiser of the land.

Another group of myths concerns the sun. According to one account, the sun lived in a tree called Fusang or Kongsang. Each morning it rose from there to rest in another tree in the west. In antiquity there were ten suns, which one day rose together and scorched the people below. The archer Yi shot down nine of them with his arrows. According to most texts, it was Yao who asked that they be cut down rather than shot, though this version may be the result of blending with Indo-European mythology, since Yi is a hero of that tradition. Chang'e, Yi's wife and goddess of the Moon, is the heroine of another popular tale. Motifs close to the ten suns appear among other peoples of the Far East, in Siberia and in some Native American stories.

Chinese mythology also holds a cosmic dualism found across much of East Asia and Oceania. One principle, linked to light, the sun and fire, is set against another linked to darkness, the moon and water. The first was represented by a bird, which in China was a crow, a motif important in the Shang dynasty, the first whose existence is confirmed by archaeology. The second was represented by a serpent or another water creature. A myth about Shun, one of the mythical rulers, illustrates the idea: his mother belonged to the serpent clan and his father to the bird clan, so that Shun united both principles. The tale also reflects the totemism of early society, in which each clan had an ancestral animal, and exogamy, which required spouses to come from different clans.

Traditional cosmology pictures a square earth beneath a round sky, held apart by pillars that might be mountains or trees. Above lies the realm of Heaven, imagined as a court with a hierarchical government headed by a supreme emperor. Below the earth stretches the land of the dead, called Diyu, the Yellow Springs or Hell. In later versions this underworld has its own bureaucracy, with judges who weigh the merits of each soul, and officials such as Yanluo and the ox-headed and horse-faced attendants. According to some traditions, souls drink the Drink of Forgetfulness, served by Meng Po, before being reborn. The sources also place remote lands at the edges of the world: the Kunlun mountains in the west, the island of Fusang in the east and the sea paradise of Penglai. One myth explains why the land of China slopes down towards the east. Gonggong damaged the world pillar on Mount Buzhou, and that is why the rivers flow from west to east.

Myth is also intertwined with religion and politics. The Jade Emperor appears in literature after Daoism had taken shape, and both Daoism and Buddhism absorbed mythical figures and places into their own beliefs. The Daoist paradise, for instance, became the dwelling place of the immortals. Myth was also used to legitimise dynasties, which often claimed divine descent, and the notion of the Mandate of Heaven offered an explanation for why one dynasty fell and another replaced it. Today myth remains alive in popular religion and in Daoism. One example is the story of the herdsman and the weaver girl, lovers separated by the Milky Way, who meet once a year on a bridge of birds called the Queqiao during the Qixi festival. These traditions carry meaning for those who practise them, and studying them helps us understand the variety of Chinese thought.

Trivia

A mythology without its own book. Ancient China produced no works devoted only to myths. The stories appear within texts of history, philosophy and poetry.
The rainbow as a patch. According to one story, Nüwa mended the torn sky with stones of seven colours, and the patch left behind became the rainbow.
Carp that become dragons. Tradition speaks of the Dragon Gate, a set of rapids where certain carp that swim upstream and leap the falls are transformed into dragons.
