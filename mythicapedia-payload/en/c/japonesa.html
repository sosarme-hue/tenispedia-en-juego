Japanese mythology

Japanese mythology is a body of traditional stories, folktales and beliefs that grew up across the islands of the archipelago. Its Shinto roots are tied to the landscape and to farming life. Its best-known tales are recorded in the Kojiki and the Nihonshoki, and they explain everything from the making of the islands to the origin of the imperial family.

Japanese mythology is a body of traditional stories, folktales and beliefs that developed across the islands of the archipelago. Its foundation is Shinto, although over the centuries it absorbed influences from China, Buddhism and popular folk practice. For Shinto believers, a living faith, these narratives are not simply myths. They are part of a religious life centred on the kami, a word usually rendered as “gods” or “spirits” and used for an almost countless range of sacred beings.

A particular feature deserves attention. Unlike Greek or Norse mythology, it is hard in Japan to say neatly which stories count as myth and which belong to other categories. The tales are closely tied to the landscape, to mountains, rivers and islands, and to popular farming beliefs. Much of what is known today comes from two key works. The Kojiki, often translated as “Record of Ancient Matters”, is the oldest surviving account of the country’s myths, legends and history. The Nihonshoki is the second oldest. The two texts approach the material differently and sometimes disagree. Alongside them stand complementary works. The Shintōshū explains the origins of the deities from a Buddhist viewpoint, while the Hotsuma Tsutae offers a markedly different version of Japan’s mythology.

According to the story, the world began when heaven and earth separated. In Takamagahara, the high plain of heaven, the first gods were born, among them the Kotoamatsukami and the seven gods of Kamiyonanayo. They entrusted the making of the first land to the pair Izanagi and Izanami, and gave them a jewelled spear. The two stood on the Amenoukihashi, the floating bridge of heaven, and stirred the ocean. Salt drops falling from the spear’s tip formed the island of Inojoro. They came down to live there, raised a pillar called Amenomihashira and built a palace around it.

The myth also explains the proper way to come together. The couple circled the pillar in opposite directions. When they met, Izanami spoke first, and the result was two malformed children, Hiruko and Awashima. They were not counted as gods and were set adrift at sea. The other deities, when consulted, said the male should have spoken first. The pair repeated the rite, this time with Izanagi speaking first, and their union succeeded. It produced the eight great islands, the Ōyashima, among them Awaji, Shikoku, Kyushu and Honshu, which in early times was called Yamato. Further islands and many deities followed.

The most dramatic episode comes with the birth of the fire god Kagutsuchi. Izanami died giving birth to him and was buried on Mount Hiba, on the border of the old provinces of Izumo and Hōki. Furious, Izanagi killed the infant, and new deities emerged from the death. Episodes like this one are the kind that myths use to account for natural phenomena and aspects of culture.

Grief-stricken, Izanagi travelled to Yomi, the dark land of the dead. There he found Izanami, but she told him she had already eaten the food of the underworld and could not return. She asked for time to rest and begged him not to look. He lit the comb from her hair as a torch and saw her body decaying and crawling with maggots. Terrified, he fled, and Izanami pursued him with the shikome, wild female beings of Yomi. Izanagi escaped by throwing his cap, which turned into clusters of grapes, and his comb, which became bamboo shoots. He urinated on a tree to make a river and threw peaches that drove off the pursuers. At last he sealed the cave entrance with a huge boulder. From behind it, Izanami vowed to kill a thousand living people each day. Izanagi answered that he would bring fifteen hundred into being. In this telling, death entered the world on that day.

After purifying himself in water, Izanagi’s body gave birth to new deities. From his left eye came Amaterasu, the sun; from his right eye, Tsukuyomi, the moon; and from his nose, Susanoo, the god of wind or storm. He divided the world among them. Amaterasu took the heavens, Tsukuyomi the night, and Susanoo the seas.

Susanoo proved hard to manage. After his excesses, he was banished to Yomi, but first he wanted to bid farewell to his sister in heaven. Suspicious of his intentions, Amaterasu set a test in which each would produce deities from their belongings. She made three women from his sword, and he made five men from her necklace. Both claimed victory. His violence peaked when he hurled a half-flayed pony, a creature sacred to Amaterasu, into the room where she was weaving, and one of her attendants died. She retreated into the Iwayado cave, and the world was plunged into darkness.

The gods tried in vain to coax her out. Then Ame-no-Uzume, the goddess of festivity, devised a plan. She set a large bronze mirror in a tree facing the cave, wrapped herself in leaves and flowers, overturned a tub and danced on it until she was naked. The male gods roared with laughter. Curious, Amaterasu peeped out, saw her reflection in the mirror, and was drawn out by the god Ame-no-Tajikarawo. The cave was then closed with a sacred rope, the shirukume. Uzume is remembered from then on as goddess of dawn and of festivity.

Another cycle tells of the defeat of Yamata-no-orochi, a serpent with eight heads and eight tails. Susanoo reached the province of Izumo and met an elderly couple mourning their daughter, the last of eight whom the dragon had devoured, one each year. He offered to save her in exchange for her hand. He turned her into a comb, hid her in his hair, and set out eight enclosures with barrels of rice liquor. The dragon drank until it fell asleep, and Susanoo killed it. In its tail he found an exceptional sword, which he gave to Amaterasu. It later became known as Kusanagi and appears in many later tales.

The Izumo tradition also features Ōnamuji, also called Ōkuninushi, a descendant of Susanoo. His brothers killed him twice, and his mother restored him both times. After fleeing to Susanoo’s realm, he passed the god’s tests and won his approval. In this version, Ōnamuji and the dwarf deity Sukunabiko helped create the islands. The Yamato tradition credits Izanagi and Izanami instead. It is a good example of how regional versions coexist and sometimes contradict one another.

The myth of Ninigi, grandson of Amaterasu, explains the origin of the imperial family. Ninigi fell in love with Konohana-sakuya, daughter of Yamatsumi, lord of the mountains. Her father offered him her elder sister, Iwanaga, too. Ninigi married only Sakuya. According to the story, Iwanaga embodied eternity, which Ninigi declined, while Sakuya stood for the brief prosperity of flowers. This divine descent lies behind the word tennō, “heavenly sovereign”, used for the emperor. For centuries, the link to Amaterasu helped legitimise imperial authority, and in modern Japan until 1945 the emperor was officially regarded as divine. That political use of the myth left a painful legacy.

Today, the Kojiki and the Nihonshoki remain present in shrines, festivals, art, literature and popular culture. Their stories show how a set of narratives can shape an identity, a landscape and a living religion.
