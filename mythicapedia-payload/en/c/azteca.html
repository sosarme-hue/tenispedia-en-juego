Gods, suns and the origins of the world in central Mexico

Aztec mythology

Aztec mythology belongs to the wider Nahua religious world of Mesoamerica and gathers stories about creator gods, patron deities and four earlier worlds that came before our own. Its myths explain how the sky, the earth, humanity and the Sun came to be, and they tie ritual offerings to the survival of the cosmos.

Aztec mythology is the body of myths and religious traditions of the Mexica, the people who built Tenochtitlan on Lake Texcoco. They belonged to the wider Nahua cultural world of Mesoamerica, and much of what they believed was shared with neighbouring peoples. According to tradition, the groups who became the Aztecs came from the north, and the sources do not agree on where they started. Some accounts name Aztlan, others Chicomoztoc, 'the place of the seven caves', and others Tamoanchan, a legendary origin of all civilizations. When they reached the valley of Anahuac, they were seen by their neighbours as the least civilized newcomers. They decided to learn, and they absorbed a great deal from the older Toltec tradition, which they regarded as the source of all culture, and from Teotihuacan, which they often confused with Tollan. The vision of an eagle perched on a cactus, holding a serpent, marked the place where they founded Tenochtitlan, and that image still appears on the coat of arms of Mexico.

At the heart of Nahua thought was the idea of creative duality. The wise men known as tlamatinime spoke of a force they called Ipalnemohuani, 'the one through whom we live'. Ometeotl, the 'two god', was the principle that contained both the masculine and the feminine within itself. It appeared as Ometecuhtli, lord of duality, and Omecihuatl, lady of duality. From this couple came four principal creator gods: Xipe Totec, Tezcatlipoca, Quetzalcoatl and Huitzilopochtli. Because the Nahuatl language used a plural only for animate beings, some scholars have debated whether the Mexica moved towards monotheism. Most specialists now stress that this reading comes from Western ideas, and that the key concept in Nahua thought was duality rather than a single supreme god.

Below the creators stood the patron gods, who watched over each people. A legend says that when the Nahua groups left Chicomoztoc, each carried a sacred bundle, a tlaquimilolli, holding relics of its patron. Huitzilopochtli was the patron of the Mexica, but they also respected the gods of the peoples they conquered. Next to the Templo Mayor they built a special shrine called the Coateocalli, where the patron deities of subject peoples were honoured, a practice sometimes compared to the Roman Pantheon. Beyond these figures there were gods for crafts and for many aspects of daily life. Xipe Totec was linked to goldsmiths, Nanahuatzin to skin illnesses and humility, and Tlazolteotl to physical love and to prostitutes. Many of these gods were older than the Mexica and were shared with other Nahua and neighbouring peoples.

The myth of the five suns describes four worlds that came before the present one. Each was ruled by a god, inhabited by a different kind of humanity and destroyed by a natural force linked to one of the elements: earth, water, air and fire. The best-known source is the Historia de Mexico by its pictures, and the order of the suns changes from one account to another, so it is best read as a tradition rather than a fixed chronology. In the most common version, the first sun, Nahui Ocelotl, belonged to Tezcatlipoca, and its people were giants who ate acorns. Quetzalcoatl knocked Tezcatlipoca from the sky, and jaguars devoured the giants. The second sun, Nahui Ehecatl, belonged to Quetzalcoatl, and ended in a great wind that carried away the monkey-men and turned them into monkeys. The third sun, Nahui Quiahuitl, was ruled by Tlaloc, and ended in a rain of fire. The fourth, Nahui Atl, was ruled by Chalchiuhtlicue, and ended in a flood that turned the fish-men into fish. The fifth sun, Nahui Ollin, is our age, and it depended on a divine sacrifice.

Stories of how the earth was made also vary. In one telling, Quetzalcoatl and Tezcatlipoca come down from the sky and find the monster Cipactli, also called Tlaltecuhtli, 'lord of the earth'. They change into great serpents, seize the monster and tear it apart. Its upper half becomes the earth and its lower half the sky. The other gods are angry about the violence, so they decide to console the earth: plants and flowers grow from its hair and skin, its eyes become springs and lakes, its mouth becomes rivers and caves, and its nose becomes mountains and valleys. Other versions describe Tezcatlipoca losing a foot when he offers it as bait. In all of them the monster demands blood and food, and sacrifice becomes the way to keep the earth fruitful. When the gods wanted to people the world, Quetzalcoatl went down to Mictlan, the dangerous underworld ruled by Mictlantecuhtli and Mictecacihuatl, to fetch the bones of earlier humans. Once he had them, the gods sprinkled them with their own blood, and humanity was born from that offering.

The birth of the sun is placed in Teotihuacan, where the gods gathered in the dark to choose who would throw himself into the fire to light the world. Tecuciztecatl, a proud god, volunteered first and prepared a lavish offering of quetzal feathers and gold balls. Nanahuatl, the humblest of the gods, covered in sores, used simple reeds, maguey spines and his own scabs as offerings. After four days of penance, Tecuciztecatl hesitated three times at the edge of the fire, so the gods called Nanahuatl, who threw himself in without hesitation. Ashamed, Tecuciztecatl then leaped in as well, and the sun rose in the east as Tonatiuh, while the moon appeared afterwards. For this reason the eagle has white feather tips and the jaguar has spots burned into its skin. The story gave the Mexica a powerful model: the movement of time depended on sacrifice, and the sun needed blood to keep moving.

Another cosmogony describes a thirteenth heaven where the divine pair Ometecuhtli and Omecihuatl lived. Their four sons were four forms of Tezcatlipoca, the red, black, white and blue ones. According to the old texts, after six hundred years of inactivity they met and agreed on the laws of what they would create, and they gave Quetzalcoatl and Huitzilopochtli the task of making a middle sun that gave less light. The best-known birth story, however, is that of Huitzilopochtli. His mother Coatlicue became pregnant after placing a ball of feathers in her waistband. Her other children, the Centzon Huitznahua, and her daughter Coyolxauhqui, wanted to kill her. Born fully armed on Mount Coatepec, Huitzilopochtli defeated his brothers, who became stars, and killed his sister, throwing her body down the mountain. The Mexica saw in this episode a model for combat and for the sacrifice that drives back darkness at dawn.

Nahua gods were understood as complex powers whose identities multiplied according to what they did. A single deity could be generous or cruel, fertile or destructive, and it could carry as many names as its functions. That is why Aztec mythology can seem hard to follow for anyone looking for a fixed family tree. Today it is remembered above all for its creation stories, its ideas about sacrifice and time, and the place these stories still hold in Mexican memory.

Trivia

A myth on a coat of arms. The vision of an eagle on a cactus, which according to tradition marked the founding of Tenochtitlan, appears on the coat of arms of Mexico.
The humble god. Nanahuatzin, the covered-in-sores god who stood for humility, threw himself into the fire first and became the Sun, while the proud Tecuciztecatl followed and became the Moon.
Four suns and a monster. In several versions, the earth is formed from the torn body of Cipactli, whose eyes become lakes and whose mouth becomes rivers and caves, which explains why sacrifice was seen as necessary to keep the world going.
