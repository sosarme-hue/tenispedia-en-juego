Gods, rites and creatures of the ancient Cantabri

Cantabrian mythology

Cantabrian mythology comprises the beliefs, rites and stories of the Cantabri, a pre-Roman people of northern Iberia. Most of what we know comes from classical authors, inscriptions and an oral tradition that survived in rural villages. Over the centuries it blended with Celtic and Roman myths and, later, with Christianity.

Cantabrian mythology is the body of beliefs, rites and stories of the Cantabri, the pre-Roman people who lived along the northern coast of the Iberian Peninsula. What we know of it comes in fragments. Much of it is preserved in the writings of classical authors, in inscriptions and carved stelae, and in oral traditions that lingered in village life. Ancient writers tended to record the cults that resembled their own, so many local nuances were lost.

With the arrival of Roman rule, and later of Christianity, these beliefs mingled with Celtic and Roman myths. The outcome is a syncretism in which it is often hard to tell where one element ends and another begins. The stories that survived are short and to the point. They tend to explain natural phenomena, and each teller reshaped them, so a single myth could exist in several versions.

Solar worship stands out among the divinities, as attested by Cantabrian stelae. Candamo, a supreme father figure linked to storms and mountains, was also venerated. In Roman times he was identified with Jupiter, and he is often associated with solar cult. A god of war, later identified with the Roman Mars, received sacrifices of male goats, horses and large numbers of prisoners, according to Strabo, Horace and Silius Italicus. Horace describes the Cantabrians as drunk on horses' blood. Some scholars connect the name Epane, perhaps a local form of a horse goddess, with this cult.

Horses were regarded as sacred animals, and some authors link them with the reincarnation of the war god. These great sacrifices, or hecatombs, were believed to fulfil the will of the gods and bind the community together. Human sacrifice also appears in the sources. Strabo reports that priests read omens from the entrails of prisoners and from the way a victim fell. Saint Martin of Dume mentions similar practices in the north of the peninsula, which resembled those described for the Celts of Gaul. In a warrior society, sacrifice was not necessarily seen as barbarism. It was viewed as a serious act requiring great resolve. An extreme case was the devotio, in which a community tied its fate to that of its leader.

The Cantabri were largely herders and farmers, and mother goddesses linked to the Moon held great importance. The Moon was believed to govern the times for sowing and harvesting, and lunar customs still survive in rural areas. At the sanctuary of Cildá, Mater Deva, the personification of the river Deva, was honoured. Lug, the pan-Celtic god, was also worshipped, and was associated with roads, mountains and the souls of the dead. A bronze statuette of a sea god, assimilated to Neptune, was found near Flaviobriga. Since there is little evidence that the Cantabri sailed the dangerous northern sea, very few ocean myths of their own are known.

The most distinctive Cantabrian symbol appears on the discoid stelae: four crescent moons facing each other around a central point, sometimes six, as on one stela from Lombera. Specialists note that this design has no clear parallel in other Iberian cultures, although it resembles Hallstatt plaques from central Europe. These stelae marked open-air sanctuaries, and their sacred character persisted when hermitages were built on or near them.

As for death, the Cantabri believed in the immortality of the spirit. Cremation was the usual practice, except for warriors who died in battle. Their bodies were left on the battlefield until vultures consumed them, a custom recorded in the engravings of the Stela of Zurita. The soul was thought to reach the afterlife and join its ancestors in glory. The Moon was also linked to death, and the last glimpse of sunlight at dusk was called the Sun of the Dead, because it was believed to mark the moment when the departed returned to life.

Nature worship was also important. Water, rocks and trees were revered. Pliny the Elder describes three intermittent springs, the Tamaric Fountains, whose stopped flow was taken as a bad omen. Suetonius tells how Galba found twelve axes in a lake during his time in Cantabria, and he read this as a good sign. The yew was especially respected. Silius, Florus, Pliny and Isidore of Seville report that some Cantabrians took its poison rather than face slavery. Yews were planted in squares, cemeteries and churches, and the yew beside the church of Saint Mary of Lebeña, a site sacred since pre-Roman times, became a meeting place. The oak, a symbol of the union of sky and earth, was used in ceremonies to summon rain.

At crossroads, people left stones as offerings together with prayers. Over time these piles of stones grew into pillars, busts and crosses. Saint Martin of Dume criticised practices such as lighting candles near rocks, trees or springs, and placing crosses at crossroads. With Roman rule, such traditions were absorbed through the Lares Viales and Compitales.

Marvellous creatures hold a central place in popular memory. The Ojáncanu, a cyclops-like giant, embodied cruelty and evil. It is the Cantabrian counterpart of the Greek Polyphemus and has relatives in other peninsular traditions, such as the Basque Tartalo. His wife, the Ojáncana, was even more ruthless. The Anjana, by contrast, was a kind and generous fairy who protected the honest, lovers and those lost in the woods. According to popular belief, the summer solstice is a magical night, when the damselflies known as the Devil's little horses and witches lose their power after dusk.

Trivia

A Sun of the Dead. The Cantabri used this name for the last glimpse of sunlight at dusk, which was believed to mark the moment when the dead came back to life.
Axes in a lake. According to Suetonius, Galba found twelve axes in a lake while he was in Cantabria and read it as a good omen.
The yew as witness. Yews were planted in squares and churches because they were regarded as witness trees, and the one at Lebeña served as a meeting place.
