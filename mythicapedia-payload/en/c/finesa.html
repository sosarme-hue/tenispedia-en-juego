Beliefs, spirits and gods of the Finnic peoples before Christianity

Finnish mythology

Finnish mythology comprises the beliefs of the Finnish peoples before their Christianization. It is closely related to Estonian traditions and shares features with Baltic, Slavic and, to a lesser extent, Norse myths. It survived as an oral tradition of sung poems until well into the nineteenth century, and much of what we know today comes from the work of collectors.

Finnish mythology refers to the traditional beliefs of the Finnish people before they converted to Christianity. It is closely tied to the mythology of the Estonians, the Finns' nearest relatives in language and ethnic origin, and it shares features with the myths of neighbouring Baltic and Slavic peoples and, to a lesser extent, with Norse ones. Many stories also have parallels with those of other Uralic peoples, such as the Sami. For centuries these beliefs were kept alive orally, in sung poems and folklore, well into the nineteenth century.

Over time, the influence of surrounding cultures pushed a sky god to the forefront. Ukko, whose name means “old man”, was originally a nature spirit like many others. The most sacred animal was the bear, which was seen as an embodiment of the ancestors. Its real name was never spoken aloud, and people used many circumlocutions instead, such as mesikämmen, “honey paw”, or otso, “broad brow”. According to the sources, the bear was not strictly considered a god, but occupied a special place in this view of the world.

The first written mention of these beliefs comes from Mikael Agricola, a Lutheran bishop and reformer, in the preface to his 1551 Finnish translation of the Psalms. He listed supposed deities of Häme and Karelia, twelve for each region, with their functions set out in verse. The list was quoted as a historical source for centuries, and Agricola is regarded as the father of the study of Finnish religious history. However, from the late eighteenth century scholars began to re-examine it critically, and concluded that most of the figures were not gods at all. They were local guardian spirits, characters from folk tradition, explanatory legends, cultural heroes, Christian saints under other names and, in one case, a harvest festival.

Cristfried Ganander’s Mythologia Fennica, published in 1789, was the first secular scholarly attempt to tackle the subject. In the nineteenth century, researchers such as Elias Lönnrot, Castrén and others travelled across the country recording poetry sung by runo singers, many of whom were tietäjät, traditional ritual specialists. From this material Lönnrot edited the Kalevala and the Kanteletar. Much of this poetry dealt with pagan themes from before Christianity, and it allowed scholars to study the mythology in far greater detail.

According to these traditions, the world was formed from birds’ eggs, though accounts differ on the details. In the Kalevala a pochard lays seven eggs, six of gold and one of iron. Other stories mention a swallow, a loon or a giant mythical eagle called kokko. The sky was imagined as the upper shell of the egg, but it could also be pictured as a tent held up by a pillar at the North Pole, beneath the North Star. The apparent turning of the stars was explained by the rotation of this sky-dome around the North Star and around itself. This rotation produced a great whirlpool at the pole, through which souls could pass out of the world to Tuonela. The earth was thought to be flat, and at its edges lay Lintukoto, “the home of the birds”, a warm region where birds spent the winter. The Milky Way was known as linnunrata, “the path of the birds”, because they were believed to fly along it there and back.

Birds also had another role. They were thought to bring the soul at birth and to carry it away at death. In some areas people carved wooden bird figures, called sielulintut, “soul birds”, to keep the soul from escaping during sleep and getting lost on the paths of dreams. Waterfowl appear often in tales, and also in stone paintings and carvings, which shows how much they mattered to these communities.

Tuonela was the land of the dead, an underground home or city where everyone slept forever. It was a dark and motionless place, and the myths say nothing of moral judgement. Only a shaman brave enough could travel there in trance to seek guidance from the ancestors. The journey meant crossing a dark river. If the traveller had a valid reason, a boat would come to collect them. Sometimes a shaman’s soul had to persuade the guardians of Tuonela that it was really dead.

Ukko was the god of the sky, the weather and the harvest, and the most important figure in the pantheon. The Finnish words ukkonen, meaning thunder, and ukonilma, meaning stormy weather, come from his name. In the Kalevala he is also called Ylijumala, “supreme god”. In myths he usually appears only through natural events, when he is called upon. His origins may go back to the Baltic thunder god Perkūnas, who is associated with Thor, or to the older Finnish sky god Ilmarinen. When Ukko took over Ilmarinen’s role, Ilmarinen became a smith hero, or god of rock. In the Kalevala he is credited with forging the stars in the sky and the Sampo, the mill of plenty.

Ukko’s weapon could be a hammer, an axe or a sword, and with it he struck lightning. According to one version, he created thunderstorms by driving his chariot across the clouds. Another says they came from his union with his wife Akka, whose name means “old woman”. The original weapon may have been the boat-shaped stone axe of the battle-axe culture, and the Ukkon vasara, “Ukko’s hammer”, probably referred to the same thing. When stone tools fell out of use in the Nordic Bronze Age, their origin became a mystery. People believed they were weapons Ukko left behind after a lightning strike, and shamans kept them for their supposed power to heal or to harm.

The pantheon was large. Ahti, god of the deep waters, provided fish, and his wife Vellamo was goddess of the sea, the lakes and storms. Tapio was the god of the forest, and with Mielikki he had children such as Tellervo, Tuulikki and Nyyrikki, all linked to woodland and hunting. Väinämöinen, a wise old man with a powerful magical voice, is the central character of the Kalevala. Lemminkäinen was a reckless hero, and Kullervo a tragic antihero who inspired Tolkien’s Túrin Turambar in The Silmarillion. Tuoni personified death, and Tuonetar appears in the stories as both his daughter and his lover. Perkele, the Devil, began as a thunder god but was demonised after Christianity arrived. Other figures were closer to domestic life: the tonttu was a kindly guardian of the home or cultivated land.

The bear held a place of its own. After a hunt there was a great feast in its honour, the peijaiset, and much of the ceremony was devoted to persuading the bear’s spirit that it had died by accident and had not been murdered. Afterwards the skull was hung high in a pine tree so that the spirit could return to the heavens. Other sacred beings included the Swan of Tuonela and the Elk of Hiisi.

Among the magical objects, the Sampo was a mill that, in Lönnrot’s interpretation in the Kalevala, made flour, salt and gold out of thin air. Väinämöinen’s kantele was made from the jaws of a huge pike, and his great sword shone like the sun and was exceptionally sharp.

Today a small part of the Finnish population practises a modern revival of these beliefs, while the Kalevala and its poems continue to inspire the country’s literature, music and art.

Trivia

A god who was not a god. Agricola's list of deities, published in 1551, was quoted as a source for centuries. Later studies concluded that nearly all its figures were local spirits, folk characters or Christian saints under other names.
The Sampo mill. In Elias Lönnrot's interpretation in the Kalevala, the Sampo was a mill that produced flour, salt and gold. Its exact form and whereabouts remain a subject of debate among scholars.
An echo in Tolkien. The tragic hero Kullervo, from Finnish folklore collected in the Kalevala, served as a model for the character Túrin Turambar in Tolkien's The Silmarillion.
