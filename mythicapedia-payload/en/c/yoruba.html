Beliefs, myths and traditions of the Yoruba people of West Africa

Yoruba religion

The Yoruba religion, known as Ìṣẹ̀ṣẹ, brings together the spiritual beliefs and practices of a people of West Africa, centred on Olódùmarè, the Supreme Being, and on the Òrìṣà. Its myths, such as the story of creation at Ifẹ̀, are part of a broader body of songs and stories, and its influence reached the Americas through the African diaspora.

The Yoruba religion, known in its own language as Ìṣẹ̀ṣẹ, comprises the traditional beliefs and spiritual practices of the Yoruba people, a West African ethnic and linguistic group whose homeland lies mainly in south-western Nigeria, with neighbouring parts of Benin and Togo. The name combines two words: ìṣẹ̀, meaning source or root, and ìṣe, meaning practice or tradition. Together they suggest something like "the original tradition" or "the tradition of antiquity", a phrase that reflects the belief that many Yoruba customs go back to the worship of Olódùmarè and the veneration of the Òrìṣà. For believers, the religion is not a set of stories held apart from daily life but a way of living in the world. Its beliefs belong to what the Yoruba call Ìtàn, history in the broadest sense: a whole web of songs, stories and cultural ideas that shapes society.

At the centre of this system stands Olódùmarè, the Supreme Being and ultimate source of all that exists. According to tradition, Olódùmarè gave humankind the èmí, the breath of life. His authority is expressed through àṣẹ, a vital creative force that sets the universe in motion. In Yoruba thought, what is spoken with authority must come to pass, so àṣẹ is understood both as energy and as an effective word. Yoruba thinkers describe the cosmos in two realms: Ayé, the physical world where people live, and Òrun, the otherworld where deities, spirits and ancestors dwell. Between them runs a constant exchange, and every human thought and action has effects within this network of forces. Some scholars have described this outlook as a diffused monotheism, in which the supreme creator is distant yet present throughout the universe.

The Yoruba account of creation shows how the supreme and the near are linked. In the story, when the newly formed planets were being tested for habitability, the "truth" was sent to check them. Earth was judged too wet for ordinary life, but after a time a group of divinities led by Obatala was sent to help shape its surface. Obatala carried a shell that held soil, some winged creatures and a piece of cloth. He poured the contents onto the water, and the birds scattered the soil until it formed a broad stretch of dry land, with hills and valleys. Obatala climbed onto a high place and named it Ifẹ̀, then began to mould figures from handfuls of earth. Meanwhile Olódùmarè gathered gases from the far reaches of space and set off an explosion that became a fireball. He sent it to Ifẹ̀, where it dried the land and baked the still figures. Finally he breathed the breath of life over them, and the figures became the first people. For this reason Ifẹ̀ is known as Ifẹ̀ Ọ̀ọ̀dáyé, the "cradle of existence".

Between Olódùmarè and humans stand the Irúnmọlẹ̀, primordial entities sent to carry out particular tasks, often as go-betweens linking the invisible and visible worlds. The sources count the total as 400 plus one: two hundred of the right, two hundred of the left, and one more that signals the exact number is unknown to people. The Òrìṣà, a term often rendered as "unique or selected heads", are the best-known divinities of worship. Each one governs specific forces of nature and aspects of life. Shangó, god of thunder, is among the most famous; tradition remembers him as the fourth king of the Yoruba and as a figure deified after his death. Oshun, linked to rivers and fertility, Yemayá, associated with the sea, Elegua, who opens the paths, Oya, connected with the cemetery and the winds, Ògún, lord of iron, and Obatala complete the most revered group. Many Òrìṣà are thought to have been human long ago, who after death passed to heaven to live with Olofi. Much of their lore, the pattakis, comes from this idea.

Opposing this order are the Ajogun, forces that act against a person's destiny. Their name roughly means "that which feeds on trouble or war", and they are described as eight principal figures, led by Ikú, Death. Life is understood as a balance between ire, goodness and alignment with one's destiny, and ibi, evil or being out of step with it. To stay on the right path, people rely on the ori, the head understood as the seat of personal destiny, which is cared for through ceremonies and offerings. Character, or ìwà, is another central concern. Teachings in the Ifá corpus link it to individual and collective uprightness and to self-improvement in civic, social and intellectual life.

This wisdom is preserved in the divination system of Ifá, whose reference figure is Ọ̀rúnmìlà, associated with wisdom, intellect and divination. The Ifá texts, known as Odù Ifá, were passed down orally among the babalawos, the diviners. They are traditionally divided into 256 sections, called odù, each containing verses with proverbs, stories and reflections on every part of life. In 2005 UNESCO recognised this tradition as a Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity. Orula, identified with Ọ̀rúnmìlà, is credited with the secret of divination. In one account, after a family crisis, Elegua hid the infant Orula at the foot of a ceiba tree to save him from his father Obatala's order, and later, when Shangó cured Obatala, Orula was pardoned. Shangó then carved a splendid divining board from the ceiba and gave it to his brother, who became the keeper of the oracle.

Other stories explain the characters of the divinities. In the story of Ògún, after a violent act that horrified Obatala, Ògún condemned himself to exile, withdrew to the forest and was seen only by his brother Ochosi, the hunter. The two had been rivals until an incident in which one killed a deer and the other cleared a path to reach it. They then made a pact: they need one another, and apart they are nothing. In worship, Ochosi is therefore always linked to Ògún. Elegua's stories, meanwhile, tell how a shining object found by a king's son became the first shrine of this orisha, a stone placed behind a door.

In ritual, the Òrìṣà are honoured in ceremonies in which the initiate receives the saint, the rite by which a divinity settles into a person. Dances, songs and attributes help identify each one: Elegua dances with a hooked staff and plays like a child; Ògún mimes the work of the blacksmith or the struggle with a machete; Ochosi reenacts the hunt with a bow. Ancestors are equally important. The Egungun cult honours the dead through masks and costumes that embody their spirits, and the idea of Oku, death, is understood as a passage rather than an ending. Many families also believe in atunwa, a form of rebirth within the lineage, reflected in names such as Babatunde, "the father returns".

Yoruba religion spread beyond Africa with the transatlantic slave trade. Many people carried their beliefs to the Americas, where they were reshaped and blended with other traditions. From this came the so-called New World lineages: Santería in Cuba, Puerto Rico and the Dominican Republic, Candomblé in Brazil, Argentina and Uruguay, Umbanda in Brazil, Trinidad Orisha, and Venezuelan spiritism, among others. Many of these developed syncretism with Catholicism, in which the Òrìṣà were paired with Catholic saints. In the Caribbean tradition, for example, Orula is identified with Saint Francis of Assisi, Elegua with the Holy Child of Atocha, Ògún with Saint Peter and Ochosi with Saint Hubert. These associations have their own histories and vary from community to community. In 1989 it was estimated that more than 70 million people in Africa and the New World took part in Yoruba religion in one form or another.
