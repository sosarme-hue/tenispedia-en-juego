Stories, gods and ritual in ancient Rome

Roman mythology

Roman mythology combines a native tradition, tied above all to ritual and to gods of farming and war, with a large body of stories about the founding of the city and a strong Greek influence that became more visible with the poets of the late Republic. Its myths, transmitted largely by writers such as Virgil, Livy and Ovid, are mostly political and moral, and they deal with the relationship between the individual and the community.

For a long time scholars doubted that the Romans had a mythology of their own. That idea owed much to Romanticism and to 19th-century classical scholarship, which regarded Greek civilization as the most authentically creative. The underlying reason is that in Rome ritual held the central place that narrative held in Greece. The Romans had no sequence of linked stories about their gods, such as the war of the Titans or the love affairs of Zeus and Hera, until their poets began to imitate Greek models. What they did have was a highly developed ritual system, priestly schools, calendars and pantheons of related deities, along with a rich set of stories about the foundation and growth of Rome.

Those historical stories, or legends, focus on human characters, with only occasional divine intervention, yet they carry a steady sense that destiny is ordered by the gods. The Romans themselves treated them as history, even when they contained prodigious elements. In the city's earliest period, myth and history supported each other. The scholar T. P. Wiseman put the appeal of these tales into questions that still resonate: what does it take to be a free citizen, and how does well-meaning authority turn into murderous tyranny? Those questions explain why the stories were read for centuries.

The main sources are Virgil's Aeneid and the early books of Livy's history of Rome, together with the Roman Antiquities of Dionysius of Halicarnassus. Ovid's Fasti, a six-book poem arranged by the Roman religious calendar, and the fourth book of elegies by Propertius are also important. Images add to this picture. Wall paintings, coins and above all sculptural reliefs show scenes from these stories and help us see how they were told.

The founding myths are the best known part. Native Italic traditions were combined early on with Greek heroic legends. The Trojan prince Aeneas was cast as the husband of Lavinia, daughter of King Latinus, ancestor of the Latini, and, through a rather tangled revisionist genealogy, as forebear of Romulus and Remus. By extension, the Trojans became the mythical ancestors of the Roman people. This link gave Rome a past that connected it with the Greek world and with Homeric tradition without giving up its Italian roots.

Other stories are more political or moral. They deal with the development of government in accordance with divine law, as Roman religion expressed it, and with individuals who upheld, or failed to uphold, the expectations of the mos maiorum, the ways of the ancestors. The rape of the Sabine women explains the importance of that people in shaping Roman culture and the growth of the city through conflict and alliance. Numa Pompilius, the second king, of Sabine origin, was said to have consorted with the nymph Egeria and to have given Rome many of its legal and religious institutions. Servius Tullius, the sixth king, had mysterious origins that were freely mythologized, and he was said to have been a lover of the goddess Fortuna.

The story of Lucretia is perhaps the most influential. According to the account, her self-sacrifice after she was assaulted led to the overthrow of the monarchy and the establishment of the Republic. Other episodes celebrate civic virtue. Horatius Cocles held a bridge against the Etruscans. Mucius Scaevola thrust his right hand into a fire to prove his loyalty to Rome. Cloelia escaped from an Etruscan camp with other young women and, when a peace was negotiated, returned of her own accord to protect the others. The story of Coriolanus combines politics and morality. The Tarpeian Rock, meanwhile, explains why traitors were thrown from it.

Early Roman religion centred on very specific gods. According to the classification proposed by Georg Wissowa in the 19th century, the indigetes were the original gods of the state, while the novensides were divinities added later, usually in response to a crisis or a felt need. Arnaldo Momigliano and others have questioned whether this distinction can be sustained. The Romans also had many specialist gods, invoked at each stage of a farming task such as ploughing or sowing, and their names usually came from the verb for the action. Janus and Vesta guarded the door and the hearth, the Lares protected the field and the house, Pales watched over pastures, Saturn oversaw sowing and Ceres the growth of the grain. This was closer to polydemonism than to polytheism, since worshippers knew little more about each being than its name and function.

There were also triads. The oldest brought together Jupiter, Mars and Quirinus. Later, under Etruscan influence, the Capitoline Triad of Jupiter, Juno and Minerva became central to official cult, which was unusual in Indo-European religion because it contained two goddesses and only one god. The Aventine Triad of Ceres, Liber and Libera was linked to the rise of plebeians to wealth and influence. According to ancient texts, Varro spoke of twelve principal gods, the consentes, and extended the list with others of his own choosing.

Divine genealogies were also written, and they resemble Greek ones more closely. The author Hyginus drew up a cosmogony in the Hellenic manner: from Chaos came Night and Day, and from Aether and Day came Earth, Sky and Sea. Earth alone brought forth the serpent Python, and Earth and Tartarus gave birth to the Giants. This version, however, comes from a late author who imitated Greek poets, not from an early Roman account. Ovid, in the Fasti, often turned to Greek beliefs to fill the gaps in Roman tradition.

Contact with other cultures was constant. The Romans often identified their gods with Greek ones and transferred Hellenic myths to them. When they conquered a territory, they commonly gave its local gods the same honours as the gods of the Roman state. In 203 BC Rome brought the cult object of Cybele from Pessinus in Phrygia and welcomed it with a great ceremony. The practice of evocatio, by which the gods of an enemy city were formally invited to take up residence in Rome, was another way of absorbing foreign powers. Later, the cult of Mithras spread among soldiers of the imperial army as far as Roman Britain.

The legacy of these myths is wide. Many planets bear the names of Roman gods, including Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn and Neptune. The Milky Way has its own story. In one version, Jupiter placed his son, born of a mortal woman, at the breast of Juno while she slept, so that he would drink her divine milk and become immortal. When Juno woke and pushed the child away, the milk that spurted from her breast formed the pale band of light across the sky. In another version, Minerva is the one who feeds the child, and his strength makes her tear him from her breast in pain. These stories show how the Romans, and later much of Europe, pictured the heavens through myth.
