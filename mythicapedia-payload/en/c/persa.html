Gods, heroes and the struggle between good and evil on the Iranian plateau

Persian mythology

Persian mythology is the umbrella term for the stories told by the Iranian peoples who lived on the Iranian plateau and its neighbouring lands. Many of these narratives survive in Zoroastrian texts and in the Shahnameh, and they centre on the struggle between Ahura Mazda and Angra Mainyu. For today's Zoroastrians, much of this material is a living faith rather than myth.

The label Persian mythology is convenient, but it is not entirely accurate. It refers to the beliefs of the Iranian peoples, who were linked by language and culture and lived on the Iranian plateau and in neighbouring areas, stretching from the Black Sea to Khotan in western China. Many of these stories belong to religious traditions that are still practised. For most Zoroastrians, their scriptures are not mythology but faith, and they are best read on those terms, much as the Bible is not usually called Christian mythology.

The oldest texts come from the Avesta, the sacred collection of Zoroastrianism. Within it, the Yashts are mainly hymns of praise, so they say little in the way of detailed narrative. Fuller accounts appear in works written in Middle Persian, many of them compiled in the early Islamic period from late Sasanian material. Among them are the Bundahishn, the Denkard and the Vendidad. The great narrative work is the Shahnameh, the Book of Kings, which the poet Ferdowsi completed around the year 1000. He drew on these traditions and arranged their figures and episodes into a chronicle of kings and heroes. As with most ancient sources, the versions do not always agree with one another.

At the heart of these stories is the opposition between good and evil. Ahura Mazda, the wise creator and lord, confronts Angra Mainyu, the destructive spirit, known in later Persian literature as Ahriman. According to tradition, both are born of Zarvan, and their conflict lasts for the whole span of the world. Ahriman has the divs, or daevas, at his side, along with the druj, forces of deceit, barrenness and death. On Ahura Mazda's side stand the Amesha Spentas, radiant beings who embody virtues and elements such as fire and earth. Most characters in these tales are either good or evil, a division that scholars link to the moral outlook of the Zoroastrian period.

The cosmology spans twelve thousand years, split into four periods of three thousand each. In the first, the two principles exist without place or time, each with its own realm, one of light and one of darkness. Ahura Mazda first creates the Amesha Spentas and then the gods, while Ahriman brings forth beings of his own to oppose him. At the close of this period a truce is agreed, and Ahriman falls into hell, where he lies unconscious for another three thousand years.

In the second period, with Ahriman absent, Ahura Mazda creates the sky, water, earth, plants, animals and humans. These acts of creation are commemorated in six festivals called Gahambars. The principal prototypes of creation are the cow, the four-legged animals and the human beings. Ahriman, however, returns, and in the third period he kills the primordial cow. From its body grow grain, plants and animals. According to the text, after forty years a rhubarb plant grows from the seed of Keyumars, with fifteen leaves that match the years of life of Mishiyeh and Mishyaneh, the first human couple. Ahriman attacks their thoughts, and they utter the first lie by crediting creation to their enemy. They then repent, have children and die after a hundred years. Their descendants give rise to the different races, and the Iranian people trace their line to Siamak and Neshak.

The fourth period is that of revelation. Ahura Mazda discloses the secrets of the faith to Zoroaster, who presents himself as the prophet of that religion. After the prophet's death, Oshidar, Oshidar Mah and Saoshyant lead the believers. At the end of time Saoshyant will raise the dead, and evil will be destroyed. Meanwhile, the tradition describes the journey after death: the soul must cross the Chinvat Bridge. For the righteous, it is a broad road to heaven. Those who followed Ahriman in life fall from it into hell.

The epic heroes move in a space where myth and history overlap. The most famous is Rostam, whose role in the Shahnameh is far greater than in the Zoroastrian texts. Facing him is Zahhak, a symbol of despotism, from whose shoulders grew two enormous serpents. Whenever they were beheaded, new heads sprouted to guard him. In the end Kaveh the Blacksmith led a popular uprising that brought down his rule. Here the serpent stands for evil, as it does in many other Eastern mythologies. Another well-known tale concerns the Div-e Sepid, the White Daeva of Mazandaran, whose legend has survived in that region.

Birds, by contrast, usually bring good omens. The Simorgh is a vast, beautiful and powerful bird. The Homa is a royal bird of victory whose feathers adorned crowns, and the Samandar is the phoenix. The Pari, once a beautiful but wicked woman in the older tradition, grew less malevolent and more lovely over time, and in the Islamic period came to resemble the houris of paradise. Another female figure, Patiareh, who was originally wicked, now symbolises prostitutes.

Persian mythology also interests scholars for its links with Vedic India. Before the rise of the Medes, the peoples of the plateau had varied contacts with their neighbours, and some of them contributed their own traditions. Among the Indo-Iranian peoples, the ahuras and the daevas appear to have swapped roles: in India the ahuras became evil and the daevas good, while in Iran the reverse happened. Both kinds of beings received sacrifices, though for different purposes. The body of myths known today took shape more fully with the Medes and the Persians.

National history is also woven from myth and fact. The Shahnameh arranges its reigns into mythical dynasties, such as the Pishdadians and the Kayanians, and the story runs on to the conquest by Alexander. Al-Biruni compared the Achaemenids with the Kayanians in his writings, and the last king defeated by Alexander in the mythical accounts matches Darius III. The traditional narrative thus blends with historical data along a single line.

Today Persian mythology remains present in Iranian literature, art and cultural identity. For Zoroastrians, its texts keep a living moral and spiritual teaching, while for readers from other traditions they offer a window onto one of the oldest and richest cosmologies in the world.

Trivia

Serpents that regrow. In the story of Zahhak, each time the vipers sprouting from his shoulders were beheaded, new heads grew back, making them nearly unbeatable guardians.
A reversal of roles. Among the Indo-Iranian peoples, the ahuras and daevas seem to have swapped their character: in India the ahuras became evil and the daevas good, while in Iran it was the other way round.
The Persian phoenix. Samandar is the Persian name for the phoenix, a bird that, as in other traditions, is associated with renewal and rebirth.
