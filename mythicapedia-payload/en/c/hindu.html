Hindu mythology

Hindu mythology is a vast body of stories, cosmological accounts and teachings that arose on the Indian subcontinent and accompanies a religion that is still practised today. Its texts, from the early Vedic hymns to the Puranas and the great epics, present gods, heroes and cosmic cycles that most Hindus regard as part of their faith rather than as mere tradition. This page surveys its origins, its main figures and its lasting legacy.

Hindu mythology refers to a large body of literature from the Indian subcontinent that describes Hindu cosmology, the lives of legendary figures, and the deeds of the gods and their earthly incarnations. These stories are often woven together with doctrinal and ethical teaching. It is worth noting that the word myth is an outsider's term. For scholars, a myth is a foundational narrative that gives a society its sense of order, and its value does not depend on whether it is historically true. For many Hindus, however, these texts are a living part of their religion, and they are presented that way here.

Their origins are hard to trace. Scholars have debated whether the Indus Valley Civilisation, which existed between about 3300 and 1300 BCE, with its mature form between 2600 and 1900 BCE, left any traces in later beliefs. Some researchers have pointed to motifs such as male deities, mother goddesses, snake worship and reverence for animal-shaped beings. More recent scholars consider these links unproven and note how little we know about that civilisation's religion. The firmer starting point is the Vedic tradition. The word Veda means knowledge that was heard, or shruti, passed on orally to sages called rishis. Each Vedic collection had a distinct purpose. The Rigveda gathers hymns of praise to the gods, the Yajurveda supplies the formulas for sacrifice, the Samaveda arranges verses to be chanted to melodies, and the Atharvaveda collects spells, curses and healing incantations.

Three gods stood above the rest in the Vedic pantheon. Indra was the most popular deity of the Vedic age, the lord of the sky who wields the thunderbolt and brings the rains that make the earth fruitful. According to the myth, he killed Vritra, a demon who held back the waters and caused drought; once released, the waters revived the dry soil. Agni is the messenger between gods and humans, and the texts say he dwells in every home, where he is treated like an honoured guest. Surya, often described as the giver of life, crosses the sky in a fiery chariot. His light drives out darkness and also stirs human understanding. The Gayatri Mantra, one of the most revered verses in all of Hinduism, is addressed to him. Other figures also matter. Varuna sees every truth and every hidden falsehood and is called the all-knowing asura. Usha, the goddess of dawn and daughter of the Sky, is described in the hymns as a golden goddess; she wakes the living and prepares the way for Surya. Aditi is the mother of the Adityas, and her name means boundless. Soma is both a god and an intoxicating juice essential to rituals, later identified with the Moon.

In the Brahmanical period, ritual grew into a complex system that needed specialised priests. Ceremonies such as the horse sacrifice, or ashvamedha, and the Soma sacrifice marked major public occasions. Prajapati appears as a creator god in these texts, while Agni served as the medium through which offerings reached the gods. Later came the Upanishads. These are not primarily mythological narratives but philosophical and religious texts that explore the connections between ritual, cosmic realities and the human person. Here major concepts such as atman, brahman, karma, rebirth and liberation were first formulated. The sage Yajnavalkya, a central figure of the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad, expresses these ideas through conversations. Sramanic movements, including Buddhism and Jainism, also shaped later thought by questioning animal sacrifice and promoting asceticism and vegetarianism. Brahmin thinkers absorbed many of these ideas into the broader Hindu synthesis that developed between about 500 BCE and 500 CE.

The classical period produced the great epics and the Puranas. The Ramayana, traditionally attributed to Valmiki and containing about 24,000 verses, tells how Prince Rama of Ayodhya is sent into exile in the forest. There the demon king Ravana abducts his wife Sita and takes her to Lanka, and Rama sets out to rescue her. Hindus regard Rama as an avatar of Vishnu and Sita as the goddess Lakshmi born as a woman. Rama stands for the perfect man, and Sita for the devoted and loyal wife. The Mahabharata, credited to the sage Vyasa, is among the longest literary works ever created, seven times the length of the Iliad and the Odyssey combined. It recounts the struggle between five Pandava cousins and one hundred Kaurava cousins, a conflict between right and wrong that ends in a massive war. Within it sits the Bhagavad Gita, a conversation between the warrior Arjuna and his charioteer Krishna, Vishnu's eighth avatar, just before the battle. It explores duty, the soul and the cosmic struggle between good and evil. The Harivamsa, an appendix, records royal genealogies and the main mythological episodes of Krishna's life.

The word Purana means an old story or ancient tale. Between roughly 250 and 1500 CE, dozens of these texts were written down. Unlike the Vedas, which are dense collections of hymns and sacrificial formulas, the Puranas are narrative in form. They preserve folk tales, royal and heroic genealogies, and new versions of episodes from the epics. Three main traditions grew from them: the Vaishnavas, who follow Vishnu; the Shaivas, who follow Shiva; and the Shaktas, who follow the Goddess. Each tradition's texts declared its own deity supreme. The Puranas also developed the idea of the Trimurti, in which Brahma creates, Vishnu preserves and Shiva destroys, alongside the Tridevi of Saraswati, Lakshmi and Parvati. The older Vedic gods were pushed down the hierarchy. Indra, a prime deity in the Vedas, appears in these stories as a flawed king, proud or fearful, who must beg Vishnu or Shiva for help when demons seize his throne.

The Tantric period, roughly 900 to 1600 CE, brought another major shift. Tantras, which some followers regard as a fifth Veda, are often presented as dialogues between Shiva and his consort Parvati, and they served as the main authority for the Shaktas. In this outlook Shakti, the feminine cosmic energy, is the supreme expression of ultimate reality, and male gods depend on her to be active. Tantric narratives moved away from the epic emphasis on social duty and asceticism and toward ritual complexity and sensory experience. Blood sacrifice was revived as a way to appease fierce deities such as Durga and Kali. The tradition divided into a Right-Handed Path, whose rites are performed openly and largely match mainstream Hindu custom, and a Left-Handed Path, whose secret rituals may involve substances usually prohibited by orthodoxy, such as meat and intoxicating drinks, as means toward liberation, or moksha.

Puranic cosmology offers no single account of creation but several alternatives. In one, Vishnu rests on the serpent Shesha in the cosmic ocean, and Brahma emerges from a lotus growing from Vishnu's navel to begin creating the universe and all living beings. In another, the universe originates from a golden egg, the Hiranyagarbha. Shri Vidya theology sees the cosmos as an extension of the Goddess, who expands and contracts the universe through recurring cycles of creation and reabsorption. The destruction of the universe is called pralaya, or dissolution. Texts describe several kinds: some happen during the night of Brahma, some at the end of Brahma's life, and a final one in which all time and existence cease.

The legacy of these stories is still visible. In 1987 and 1988 a television series based on the Ramayana drew over 100 million viewers and famously brought public life in India to a standstill. Indian cinema has drawn on these myths since the silent film Raja Harishchandra (1913), often considered the first feature-length film of the Indian industry. The Amar Chitra Katha comics have also made myths such as the Devi Mahatmya widely known. Beyond India, the Ramayana and Mahabharata have been adapted across Southeast Asia, where they have shaped literary and performing arts in Thailand, Indonesia, Cambodia and the Philippines for more than a millennium. Several Southeast Asian dynasties claimed descent from Shiva, Vishnu or Brahma to justify their rule.

Trivia

Ganesha, scribe of the Mahabharata. According to tradition, the sage Vyasa dictated the Mahabharata and the elephant-headed god Ganesha wrote it down, using one of his own tusks as a pen.
An epic of enormous length. The Mahabharata is seven times longer than the Iliad and the Odyssey combined, which makes it one of the longest literary works ever created.
Empty streets in front of the television. The television series based on the Ramayana, broadcast in 1987 and 1988, attracted over 100 million viewers, and public life in India came to a halt during its airtime.
A myth on the big screen. The silent film Raja Harishchandra, from 1913, is one of the earliest feature-length films of the Indian film industry and adapts a story from mythology.
