Stories that tie a community's history to the supernatural

Legend

A legend is a narrative that mixes natural and supernatural events, set in a time and place close to those of its listeners, and passed down from generation to generation. Unlike myth, which concerns gods, legend focuses on human beings and on concrete features of the world. Its story runs from the Latin lives of saints of the Middle Ages to the urban folklore of today.

A legend is a narrative that mixes natural events, supernatural ones, or both, and that a community passes down from generation to generation, by word of mouth or in writing. What sets it apart is its setting: it takes place in a time and place close to those of the people who hear it, and that familiarity gives the tale a sense of plausibility. Miracles, fairy creatures or beings from beyond the grave may appear, yet the legend does not present them as invention. It is told as something that really happened, and it forms part of the way the community understands the world.

Oral tradition keeps reshaping legends. Episodes are dropped, added or adjusted to suit new beliefs, and the result is a whole family of variants. A common change is the Christianisation of pagan stories, or their simplification for children. This wearing down turns many legends into folklore, but it also lets them survive, often in disguised form, in a world where older beliefs and customs have been pushed to the margins.

The Brothers Grimm described legend as a folktale with historical roots. In 1990 the folklorist Timothy R. Tangherlini offered a more detailed definition and set it against myth. Myths concern gods; legends concern human beings who embody archetypes, such as the hero or the wise old man, as in Greek heroic legends or the Arthurian cycle. A folktale unfolds in an imaginary time and place, usually opening with 'once upon a time', whereas a legend is anchored in a real place, even when it is populated by fabulous beings like sirens or dragons. Like myth, legend is aetiological: it sets out to explain a feature of reality, such as a custom or the name of a place. Many legends gather into cycles around a single figure, such as King Arthur, Robin Hood, the Cid or Bernardo del Carpio, and some are woven into long epics or novels. Most keep a historical core, expanded with imaginary episodes, whether through error, misunderstanding such as folk etymology, or the deliberate choices of those who reshape them.

The word comes from the Latin verb legere, which meant both 'to choose' and 'to read'. Medieval Latin formed legenda, 'things to be read', applied mainly to saints' lives read aloud in church. The best-known example is Jacobus de Voragine's Legenda aurea, a collection of the lives and miracles of about 180 martyrs and saints. Its historical and philological care was slight, and its wildly imaginative etymologies cost it credibility over time, though painters and illustrators drew on it for centuries. The Reformation changed the word's meaning. English Protestants set the 'authentic' martyrs in John Foxe's Actes and Monuments against the fanciful tales of Catholic hagiography. Legend thus acquired its modern connotations of 'undocumented' and 'spurious'. Spanish probably took on this modern notion from English models, especially after 1850. The term later widened to cover learned Romantic works that draw on popular traditions without being oral tales themselves, such as those by the Duke of Rivas, José Zorrilla, Gustavo Adolfo Bécquer and José Joaquín de Mora.

Where do legends come from? Jean-Pierre Bayard, in his History of Legends, lists about ten theories. The anthropological theory, associated with Edward Tylor among others, holds that they grew out of primitive ways of thinking, remnants of early religions and cultures. The astral or naturalistic view reads them as explanations of great natural phenomena. The mythological theory, linked to Grimm, attributes them to the childhood of peoples, while the linguistic one stresses how borrowed words, distorted as they passed between peoples, gave rise to new stories. Each illuminates part of the picture, and none explains the whole.

By theme, legends are usually grouped as historical, recounting wars, conquests or battles; aetiological, explaining the origin of rivers, lakes or mountains; eschatological, concerning life after death or journeys to the underworld, as in the Greek nekyia; and religious, with tales of the righteous and the sinful, pacts with the devil or episodes from the lives of saints. By origin, there are urban legends, which circulate by word of mouth in contemporary folklore; rural legends, valid mainly in the countryside; and local legends, popular narratives of a town, county or province. One legend can belong to several groups. A story about a way of contacting a deceased loved one can be both urban and eschatological.

Spain has a rich variety of legends because Celtic, Iberian, Roman, Visigothic, Jewish and Arab traditions overlapped on the peninsula and were told in many languages. Many appear in the Romancero and, through it, in classical Spanish theatre. The courtly novel of the Baroque was another great reservoir of tales, and many churchmen gathered pious legends into collections such as the Flos sanctorum. From the Romantic period onwards, writers began to collect, study and even imitate them. Agustín Durán published the Leyenda de las tres toronjas del vergel de Amor in 1853, and Francisco Javier Simonet, following Washington Irving, produced La Alhambra: leyendas históricas árabes in 1858. Fernán Caballero translated German legends and compiled Spanish ones. In 1953 Vicente García de Diego published an anthology with a long introductory study and a selection grouped by region. Julio Caro Baroja later continued the field with De arquetipos y leyendas (1989).

In Latin America, many legends have roots before the Spanish conquest, in the oral traditions of peoples such as the Aztecs, Mayas and Incas. After the arrival of European colonisers, these narratives blended with African and European influences, producing a mestizo body of stories that reflects the continent's ethnic and cultural diversity. La Llorona is perhaps the most widespread: the spirit of a woman who drowned her children and wanders rivers and lakes, weeping for her loss. Each region adapts her to local beliefs. Her figure is linked to guilt, motherhood and tragedy, and she is often told as a warning to children. In Colombia, the Patasola is one of the most feared tales. She first appears as a beautiful woman who lures travellers away from their companions, until she reveals a monstrous form with a single leg. Some versions give her the role of protecting the forest and wild animals from those who harm them. These legends also teach moral lessons and warn about respect for nature.

In modern times, urban legends have emerged as a genre of contemporary folklore rooted in local popular culture. They usually consist of fictional stories presented as true, often with macabre or humorous touches, and they serve both as entertainment and as semi-serious explanations for puzzling events such as disappearances or strange objects. Folklorists have used the term since at least 1968, but it reached the general public through Jan Harold Brunvand, a professor of English at the University of Utah, whose popular books began in 1981 with The Vanishing Hitchhiker. Brunvand argued that folklore is not confined to so-called primitive societies, and that studying these tales reveals much about modern life.

Trivia

A medieval saints' book. Jacobus de Voragine's Legenda aurea gathers the lives and miracles of about 180 martyrs and saints. Despite its loose historical accuracy, painters and illustrators drew on it for centuries as a source of images.
Legend or fable. By traditional reasoning, if a parable is presented as something that happened to a specific son of a historical father, it counts as a legend. If it includes a donkey that gives sage advice to the prodigal son, it becomes a fable.
A book that spread a term. The Vanishing Hitchhiker, published by Jan Harold Brunvand in 1981, helped spread the term 'urban legend' to the general public, although folklorists had been using it since at least 1968.
