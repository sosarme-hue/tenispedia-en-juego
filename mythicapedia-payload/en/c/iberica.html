Mari, the Lady of the mountains and the spirits of the Basque Country

Basque mythology

Basque mythology brings together the traditional legends of Navarre, the Basque Country and the French Basque Country, told in Basque and shaped by centuries of contact with Christianity. At its centre stands Mari, a female deity linked to mountains, caves and storms, and she is surrounded by a wide range of beings such as the jentilak, the basajaun and the sorginak.

Basque mythology is the body of traditional stories, beliefs and legends of the Basque people, who live in Navarre, the Basque Country and the French Basque Country, a region known collectively as Euskal Herria. For centuries these tales were passed on orally, in the Basque language, euskera, which has no known relatives among the languages around it. It is a mythology of blends: pre-Christian figures sit beside characters of Christian origin, and some creatures came from the Greco-Roman tradition, such as the lamia. It also shares themes with the legends of Cantabria, Asturias and the Aragonese and Catalan Pyrenees, a sign of long contact with neighbouring peoples.

The origins of the Basques remain a matter of debate. One hypothesis holds that they have lived in parts of this land since the Neolithic, although they also spread into territories once occupied by Celts. Whatever the case, the religion practised before Christianity left few texts of its own. What we know comes mainly from legends, from the study of place names and from scattered references in ancient and later writers. Strabo mentions the sacrifice of male goats and of humans. Arab writers from the time of the Umayyad conquest in the 8th century offer some information about the region. The pilgrim Aymeric Picaud, in the 12th century, recorded the name of a god. Inquisition records document practices that were persecuted. The largest body of material, however, consists of the myths and folk tales collected in the 19th and 20th centuries, especially by José Miguel de Barandiarán.

Christianization, which the sources place between the 4th and 12th centuries, brought new forms and gradually hid the older cults behind other figures. Beliefs did not vanish overnight, though. In the early modern period, the persecution of witchcraft was severe. At the auto de fe in Logroño in 1610, six of the women accused of witchcraft from Zugarramurdi were burned, and in the French Basque Country the judge Pierre de Lancre ordered the execution of around eighty alleged witches in 1609. Even so, oral tradition preserved a rich mythological world, which scholars have compared with those of other Pyrenean and Atlantic peoples without confusing the two.

At the heart of this world is Mari, a female deity whom the legends place in caves and sacred peaks. According to tradition, her consort was Maju or Sugaar, although the evidence about this pair is thin, and it is unclear whether he held an equal place in the older pantheon. It was said that when Mari and her companion met in the caves of the sacred mountains, they brought forth storms. These gatherings were linked to Friday nights, the nights of the witches' sabbaths. The zezengorri or behigorri, wild bulls of the region, guarded the entrances to the caves where the goddess lived, caverns that, according to the stories, held golden furniture and rivers of milk and honey.

Mari's dwellings vary from one story to another. The best known is Mount Anboto, from which she was said to fly across the sky as a bright light to her other home on Txindoki. One tradition tells that every seven days she travelled from one cave to another: when she was at Anboto the weather was damp, when she was at Aloña it was dry, and the harvests were plentiful when she stayed in the cave of Supelegor, in Orozco. The existence of several Maris caused no problem for believers, just as the many devotions to the Virgin Mary did not. In the Basque Country the Virgin was even said to have seven sisters. Over time, many local female figures merged into Mari.

Her name has inspired several explanations. Mari means "mother", and some read it as a contraction of Amari, "to the mother", while others see a form of María, mother of God. A link with the Roman Matri dea or with Cybele has also been proposed, supported by parallels and votive altars, though the idea is not universally accepted. Alongside the mythical Mari there is a historical figure, Mari Urraca, a Navarrese princess of the 11th and 12th centuries who is said to have had a priest brother. Some authors identify her with the wife of Diego López de Haro, and her story moved from history into legend until she became the Lady of Anboto.

Another figure in the debate is Urtzi, compared with the Latin Jupiter or the Norse Thor, whose name is often read as "sky". It is striking that he hardly appears in the stories, yet his name survives in the days of the week, in the names of the months and in weather phenomena linked to storms, which suggests he mattered at the time the week was adopted in the territory. Picaud wrote that the Basques called their god "Urcia". The philosopher Andrés Ortiz-Osés argues that Urtzi never replaced Mari, and that he was a later addition: a male sky god who governs storms, probably shaped by Indo-European influence, while Mari was far older. Other researchers note that urtzi may simply mean "sky" and that the evidence is not enough to prove a deity. Neither position has been fully accepted. In one version, Urtzi was later replaced by Hodei after a conflict that ended in his death.

The legends speak of many other beings. The lamiak resemble the mermaids, nymphs and fairies of other cultures, and place names such as Lamiako keep their memory. The mairuak were the builders of the stone circles, and their name means "Moors"; across much of Spain magical beings are called by that generic name, as with the enchanted fairies of the Aragonese Pyrenees, known as moras or moricas. The iratxoak, similar to goblins, were mischievous yet helped Mari; they wore red trousers, which is why they were also called galtzagorri. The sorginak, a term rendered as "witches" by the Catholic Church, followed Mari's teachings, and it was believed that by day they lived hidden as ordinary women. The etymology is disputed. The most popular explanation links the word to sortu and egin, "to create" and "to make", while belagile, from belar and egile, suggests a herbalist or herb-maker, a reading some have used to present the sorginak as simple healers.

The basajaun, "lord of the forest", is one of the best-known beings. Under this name, different figures have been grouped over time, and some people have even tied it to contact between prehistoric humans, an idea with no scientific support, and humorously called it the "Basque yeti". As lord of nature he has been compared with the Roman Silvanus. In some legends a culture hero manages to extract secrets from them, such as growing wheat, building mills or welding iron. Martin Txiki, "Little Martin", is another protagonist: a hero who prevails through cleverness. The mamarro is a household sprite, helpful or mischievous, known by other names in other parts of the Pyrenees. The gizotso is the werewolf, about which little is recorded, and the akerbeltz, a black billy goat, was said, according to statements extracted from accused women by inquisitors, to preside over the witches' gatherings. Those same testimonies suggest he was not meant to be Satan, an identification that came from outside.

The jentilak, "the gentiles", were beings said to live in the high lands and to possess prodigious strength. They were credited with striking landforms and megalithic monuments, and place names such as Jentilzubi in Arratia or Jentilarri in Aralar keep their memory. The very word is Christian, since "gentile" was one of the terms the Church used for pagans. A Christianized legend tells that the jentilak were busy dancing or hurling stones when they saw a luminous cloud arriving from the east. An old man among them, after being brought out to see the sign, announced the end of their kind and asked to be thrown off a nearby cliff. In this version the sign announced the birth of Christ, whom they called Kixmi. The rest hid beneath the megalithic monuments and vanished. Olentzero, the last of them, is said to have survived in some way and became a messenger of good news. A charcoal burner, he brings presents at Christmas and, as custom has it, coal to naughty children.

The Basque tradition also absorbed myths from the historical period. Jaun Zuria is the mythical first lord of Biscay, said to have been born to a Scottish princess who met Sugaar in the village of Mundaka. The battle of Roncesvalles was turned into material for the epic cycle of Charlemagne. In the Aralar range, Saint Michael was said to come to the aid of a local noble who had become a hermit. A legend explains the coat of arms of Navarre through a feat at the battle of Las Navas de Tolosa. And the battle of Amaiur is the historical episode in which Navarre lost its independence to the Crown of Castile. These stories show that Basque mythology kept growing after Christianization.

Barandiarán attributed much of the survival of these beliefs to the late Christianization of valleys far from the Roman roads, where dwellings of the gentiles were still remembered in recent times. Rural life, dominated by farming until almost the middle of the 20th century, helped pass on oral traditions. In this way the figure of Mari survived, although at times she was demonized and at others transformed into Christian figures. Echoes of the same belief system appear across the Mediterranean and the Atlantic coast of Europe, in the mouros and mouras of Galicia, or in the mysterious Raiña Lupa, the Wolf Queen, who in legend is the lady of the lands where the tomb of Saint James was set. Such parallels have led some to suggest a Neolithic origin, older than the arrival of the Indo-European peoples.

Trivia

A black billy goat. The akerbeltz appears in the statements that inquisitors extracted from women accused of witchcraft, who described him presiding over the witches' gatherings. Many scholars think this image reflects how the inquisitors interpreted the testimonies rather than older tradition.
Olentzero, the Christmas charcoal burner. In the Christianized version, Olentzero is the last of the jentilak and becomes a Christmas messenger. His trade as a charcoal burner explains the coal he gives to naughty children in the Basque Country, Navarre and the French Basque Country.
Names in the landscape. Mythical beings survive in place names such as Lamiako, which recalls the lamiak, or Jentilbaratza, the orchard of the gentiles, in Ataun, Gipuzkoa. Landscape names thus preserve part of the memory of these creatures.
