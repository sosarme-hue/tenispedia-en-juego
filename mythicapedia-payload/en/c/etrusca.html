Gods, seers and the afterlife of an ancient Italian people

Etruscan mythology

Etruscan mythology is known mostly through images, inscriptions and sanctuaries, since very few complete texts have survived. Its main gods formed a triad led by Tinia, Uni and Menrva, and a long list of native and Greek figures populated a world in which divination and the relationship with the gods were central. Many of these names and figures later passed into Roman religion.

The Etruscans lived in central Italy, in a region that today corresponds largely to Tuscany and extended north and south beyond it. Their origin remains unknown, and their language, written with an alphabet derived from Greek, is still not fully understood. Over time they were absorbed by Rome, and their religion and myths became mixed with Roman ones. To reconstruct their stories, scholars have to work with scattered material: tomb paintings, pottery, architectural terracottas, engraved mirrors, bronze cistae and a handful of inscriptions. Sacred texts have almost vanished. The best-known exception is the Liber Linteus, a linen book wrapped around a mummy in Zagreb, of which only about 800 words survive. It seems to describe a calendar of rituals.

The archaeological record shows a religious life of great antiquity. In the Villanovan period, already in the ninth century BCE, there are burials with cremation and urns accompanied by grave goods. At sites such as the shrine of Pian d'Civita in Tarquinia, sacred spaces began to take shape and grew more monumental over the centuries. By the sixth century BCE, temples were large, offerings more costly, and the quantity of animal bones found in sanctuaries points to the importance of sacrifice. Altars, some with bow-shaped moldings that seem distinctly Etruscan, appear in both urban sanctuaries and rural boundary shrines, often beside rivers or on hills, places regarded as especially sacred.

At the centre of the pantheon stood a triad. Tinia, the sky god, was the most powerful. Like Zeus, he wielded the lightning bolt, though he is often shown as a beardless youth and holds less absolute power than his Greek counterpart. His wife Uni was identified by the Romans with Juno, and she was especially venerated at Pyrgi, where the Pyrgi Tablets equate her with the Phoenician goddess Astarte. The third figure was Menrva, the equivalent of Minerva and Athena. Alongside them were Nethuns, lord of the waters, and Cel, the earth goddess. This triad was worshipped in tripartite temples, similar to the later Roman Temple of Jupiter Capitolinus.

The sources and images suggest three layers of deities. The first was native: Voltumna or Vertumnus, a primordial, chthonic god; Usil, the sun; Tivr, the moon; Turan, goddess of love; Laran, god of war; Maris, linked to birth; Leinth, to death; Selvans, to the woods; Turms, god of trade and messenger of the gods; and Fufluns, god of wine. Underworld deities such as Catha, Lur, Suri, Thanr and Calus appear on the Lead Plaque of Magliano. The second layer reflects an Indo-European pattern of higher gods, namely the triad and its relatives. The third arrived during the Orientalizing period, roughly between 750 and 600 BCE, when Greek gods and heroes were adopted: Aritimi (Artemis), Menrva, Hercle (Hercules) and Pacha (Bacchus, the equivalent of Dionysus).

The presence of Greek heroes in Italy has its own story. According to Greek tradition, Heracles wandered the western lands, fought monsters and brigands and brought civilization to their inhabitants. His love affairs with local women produced many children. He was a traveller who moved on once the lands were secured, not a founder who stayed. Odysseus was also imagined as a leader who might have guided the Etruscans to their territory, though this cannot be proven. Lycophron and Theopompus linked him to Cortona, where he was called Nanos, and from the mid-sixth century BCE there were claims that the sons of Odysseus had ruled the Etruscans. The versions varied from author to author, and such tales gave antiquity and legitimacy to the Greek presence in the region.

Another frequently depicted myth concerns Aplu, the counterpart of Apollo, and Fufluns, the counterpart of Dionysus. In Etruscan mythology they were brothers and sons of Semele, whom Dionysus led out of the underworld. That episode was celebrated at the festival Herios at Delphi and appears on a mirror from Vulci, which also shows Aplu. It shows how the Etruscans adapted Greek stories to their own gods and to their concerns about death and renewal.

Divination was one of the most distinctive features of Etruscan religion. According to tradition, the doctrine had been revealed through seers. The most important was Tages, a childlike figure born from tilled earth and gifted with foreknowledge from birth. Beside him was Vegoia, a female figure. The writings attributed to them formed the so-called Etrusca Disciplina, a body of rules rather than laws. The Tagetic Books explained divination from animal entrails, the Haruspical Books and the Acherontic Books dealt with the afterlife and its rituals, and the Vegoic Books covered the interpretation of thunder and lightning. Massimo Pallottino described it as a kind of religious and political constitution: it did not say which laws should be made, but how to ask the gods and how to understand their answers.

The people who carried out these consultations were priests whom the Romans called haruspices. Tarquinia had a college of sixty of them. Inscriptions record several words for such figures, including capen and maru, and hatrencu for a priestess. The art of haruspicy was called ziχ neθsrac. The Liber Linteus and the Piacenza liver, a bronze model of a sheep's liver divided into 40 cells bearing the names of gods, are the clearest evidence of this learning, which was passed on for centuries.

Their view of the afterlife also had distinctive features. Tombs imitated houses, with spacious chambers, wall paintings and furniture. On sarcophagi the dead were often shown in the prime of life, frequently beside a spouse. Because inhumation and cremation were both practised, ashes were kept in urns shaped like houses or like the deceased. According to the images and texts, the dead were led by Charun, the equivalent of Death, a blue figure with a hammer, to a subterranean world ruled by Aita. That world was populated by Greek figures and by others, such as the composite creature Tuchulcha. The spirit of the dead was called hinthial, roughly meaning the one beneath.

Women enjoyed more social freedom in Etruria than their Roman counterparts, at least until Rome absorbed the region. They appear beside their husbands in representations, and on sarcophagi they took part in the same ceremonial banquets as men. They also took part in religious activities documented through votive offerings, ritual textile production and the iconography of tombs.

The legacy of this tradition was considerable. The Roman Senate adopted key elements of the Etruscan discipline, haruspices continued to practise for centuries, and some noble Roman families claimed Etruscan descent. As the Republic drew to a close, the religion lost prestige, and Cicero mocked it. Claudius, whose first wife Plautia Urgulanilla claimed Etruscan ancestry, kept some knowledge of the language and religion for a while, but the practice soon faded. Today Etruscan mythology remains an open field, in which each new find forces us to reconsider what we thought we knew.

Trivia

A bronze liver. The Piacenza liver is a bronze model of a sheep's liver divided into 40 cells bearing the names of gods. It is dated to the 1st or 2nd century CE and was used to study divination.
A child born from the earth. According to tradition, Tages was a child born from tilled soil who was gifted with foresight from birth. He was said to have revealed the art of divination to the Etruscans.
A blue god with a hammer. Charun, who guided the dead to the afterlife, is shown with blue skin and a hammer. He is one of the most recognizable figures in Etruscan art.
