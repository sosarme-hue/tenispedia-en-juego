Gods, giants and the world tree in the traditions of the North Germanic peoples

Norse mythology

Norse mythology is the body of myths of the North Germanic peoples, the best-preserved form of the wider Germanic mythology. Its gods live in Asgard, within a cosmos of Nine Worlds held up by the tree Yggdrasil, and its view of the universe rests on the tension between the order of the gods and the chaos of the giants. Most of what we know comes from the Eddas and other medieval texts written during or after the Christianization of Scandinavia.

The terms Norse, Nordic, or Scandinavian mythology refer to the myths of the North Germanic peoples, the ones who lived in Scandinavia and, in earlier times, in places such as Iceland, Britain, Gaul, and Hispania. They stem from Old Norse religion, the northernmost branch of the Germanic beliefs that were shared, in one form or another, by all Germanic peoples. It is worth noting that the Finns, Estonians, and Sami, who speak Uralic languages, and the Lithuanians and Latvians, who belong to the Baltic group, had their own traditions. Those were sometimes similar, especially in the case of the Baltic peoples, who are also part of the Indo-European family.

Norse mythology was never a revealed religion. There was no sacred book and no truth handed down by the gods to humans. Instead, these stories were passed on orally, in long and regular poetry. Some tales tell of ordinary people who learned about the gods after being visited by them or after visiting them. Our knowledge depends mainly on the Poetic Edda, the Prose Edda, and other medieval texts written during the Viking Age or after Scandinavia became Christian. In folklore, many of these beliefs lasted for centuries, and some rural traditions survived into modern times. Others were revived or reinvented, most notably in modern Germanic Neopaganism.

The Norse cosmos is pictured as a flat disc suspended among the branches of Yggdrasil, the world tree, which holds up nine worlds. At its roots lives the dragon Nidhogg, who gnaws at the wood in an attempt to bring down an unnamed eagle that sits in the highest branch and watches over the nine worlds. Between them runs the squirrel Ratatösk, who carries insults back and forth and stirs up trouble. A hawk called Veðrfölnir sits between the eagle's eyes and keeps watch on its movements. At the centre of the disc lies Asgard, the upper realm where the gods live. The only way to reach it is by crossing the rainbow bridge Bifröst, guarded by Heimdall, who warns the gods with his horn whenever anyone crosses. Midgard, the world of humans, lies in between. Beyond it are Jötunheim, the land of the giants; Niflheim, a cold and dark home; and, in its deepest part, Helheim, ruled by Hel, the daughter of Loki. According to the Prose Edda, most of the dead end up there. Muspelheim, the burning realm of the fire giants, lies to the south. Other realms include Alfheim, home of the light elves, and Svartálfaheim, home of the dark elves.

This cosmology is built on duality. Day and night have their own mythic figures: Dagr with his horse Skinfaxi, and Nótt with Hrímfaxi. The sun, Sól, and the moon, Máni, are each pursued by a wolf, Sköll and Hati. Notably, in Norse thought the sun is feminine and the moon masculine. The most fundamental opposition is between the icy Niflheim and the fiery Muspelheim, and the world is said to have arisen from their meeting. This emphasis on opposites reflects a deep belief in the balance of contrary forces as the source of creation.

The supernatural beings fall into three groups: the Æsir, the Vanir, and the Jötnar. The Æsir are the chief gods, who lived in Asgard together with the goddesses known as Ásynjur. Their assembly was led by Odin, the noblest and most important of them all. Among them, Thor, the god of thunder and strength, stands out. He wears iron gloves and a magical belt and carries the hammer Mjölnir. Baldr, son of Odin, is the god of beauty and wisdom, and Tyr is the god of war, who gave up a hand so the gods could bind the wolf Fenrir. Bragi represents wisdom and eloquence, and Heimdall, the guardian of the gods, sleeps less than a bird. Höðr, the blind god, appears in the best-known story of the tradition, the death of Baldr. His mother had made every living and non-living thing swear not to harm him, but she overlooked one small plant, mistletoe. Loki, tired of Baldr's invulnerability, gave Höðr a mistletoe dart, and Höðr killed his brother with it. As punishment, Odin bound Loki to three stones and had a serpent drip venom onto his face. Other male gods include Vidar, the silent one who is the best at settling disputes; Váli, the unerring archer; Ullr, the master of hand-to-hand combat; and Forseti, the god of concord. Loki, by contrast, is the god of chaos and deceit, unpredictable and the cause of trouble for gods and humans alike, though he is not a simple villain. Among the goddesses are Frigg, Odin's wife and a seer; Eir, the healer; Sjöfn, who turns people's thoughts towards love; Var, guardian of oaths; Syn, who guards doors; and Iðunn, who keeps the apples that keep the gods young.

The Vanir form another group, linked to the sea, the wind, the forests, and the fertility of the land. According to the tradition, after a long war between the two groups, a peace was made with hostages and marriages, and the Æsir emerged as the winners. In this way Njörd, a Vanr, came to Asgard with his children Freyr and Freyja, while Hœnir, an Æsir and brother of Odin, settled in Vanaheim. Some interpreters suggest that Freyr and Freyja were once two aspects of one deity, and that Freyja's popularity and her similarity in name and function to Frigg led to the two being confused. The scholar Georges Dumézil argued that the Vanir deal with more earthly matters such as sowing, weather, and harvests, while the Æsir are concerned with the spiritual. Some experts have speculated that the story reflects the replacement of older nature gods by the gods of Indo-European peoples, but this remains a conjecture. Others see the Æsir-Vanir distinction as a Norse version of a broader Indo-European division of gods, comparable to the Olympians and Titans of Greece or to parts of the Indian epic Mahabharata.

The jötnar are dangerous to humans. They are usually translated as giants, though troll and demon have been suggested as more useful alternatives. They are huge and monstrous, but also wise and wealthy, and the gods sometimes turn to them for help. Some giantesses are very beautiful, such as Gerðr. According to the myth, the cosmos was formed from the body of the primordial giant Ymir. Some Æsir are descended from jötnar and marry them, and several giants are named in the Eddas as personifications of natural forces. There are frost giants and fire giants, and their unrelenting hostility toward the gods takes the form of battles, usually led by Thor. At Ragnarök, the final battle, the forces of destruction are led by giants such as Surtr and Hrymr.

Other beings complete the picture. The norns determine fate, and their decisions cannot be reversed. In the Viking Age three are named: Urðr, "what has happened"; Verðandi, "what is happening"; and Skuld, "what shall be". They live by the roots of Yggdrasil and weave the destiny of gods and humans. The valkyries are minor female deities who served as messengers of Odin and were often described as winged. They chose among the warriors who fell in battle, and took them to Valhalla or Fólkvangr, where they served mead. They were said to be maidens, lived in Vingólf, which lay between the two halls, and were commanded by Freyja. The dwarves emerged from the corpse of Ymir, who was killed by the gods at the beginning of time. They live underground and work as miners and metalsmiths, and their hidden wisdom lets them forge magical weapons for heroes and powerful objects for the gods. The álfar, or elves, were divided into light elves, who lived in the sky, and dark elves. Some scholars think they were not really elves at all but a kind of dwarf that mixed with elves and gods. Over time, the noble, beautiful image of the elves faded in folklore, and they came to be seen as small and mischievous, as in the works of Shakespeare. Their relationship with humans was ambiguous: they could cause illness, but they could also bring blessings. A sacrifice called the álfablót was offered to them in late autumn.

Among the monstrous beasts are Fenrir, the giant wolf, and Jörmungandr, the sea serpent that encircles the world. Both are said to be children of Loki and the giantess Angrboda. There are also gentler creatures, such as Huginn and Muninn, Odin's two ravens, whose names mean thought and memory. When they return from flying over the worlds they whisper what they have seen into his ear. Odin himself, according to the myth, hung from Yggdrasil for nine days, wounded by a spear and without food, until he gained knowledge of the runes.

Compared with other traditions, Norse mythology lacks the struggle between good and evil common in the traditions of the Middle East. The Norse spoke mainly of regin, meaning powers in a broad sense, and the gods and forces they represent are more complex than they first appear. Loki is not only an enemy of the gods, although in many stories he is Thor's rival. The giants are not evil by nature but rough, stormy, and uncivilized. The basic opposition is less good against evil than order against chaos. The gods stand for structure and order, while giants and monsters stand for disorder.

The Völuspá, one of the most important poems of the Poetic Edda, tells of the origin and fate of the world. In the poem, Odin summons the spirit of a dead völva, a seeress, and orders her to reveal the past and the future. She resists, mocks him, and agrees only reluctantly. When she has finished, she sinks back into silence. In the beginning, according to this tradition, there were two regions: Niflheim, of ice, and Muspelheim, of fire, separated by Ginnungagap, an empty abyss. In Niflheim the cauldron Hvergelmir churned, and its waters froze as they fell into the void until the gap was filled with ice. Sparks from Muspelheim melted the ice into vapour, and from that vapour came the primordial giant Ymir, nourished by the cow Auðumbla. The world was made from his body.

The legacy of these myths is wide. They survived in Scandinavian folklore, inspired literature, theatre, and film, and helped shape the popular image of the Viking. In the nineteenth century the Romantic movement revived interest in them, and today Germanic Neopagans regard them as part of their faith. That revival also had political uses. In the twentieth century, Nazism used Norse symbols and stories for propaganda, exclusion, and violence, a misuse that historians analyse as a distortion of these myths.
