The myths of the land between the Tigris and the Euphrates

Mesopotamian mythology

The civilisations of Sumer, Akkad, Assyria and Babylon left some of the oldest known stories, written in cuneiform on clay tablets. In them the gods create the world, rule over human beings and sometimes punish them with floods. These tales shaped later traditions across the ancient Near East and, more indirectly, other cultures.

The term Mesopotamian mythology covers the myths, religious texts and other literature from the region between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers, a land that today lies largely within Iraq. The earliest material comes from the Sumerian, Akkadian and Assyrian societies, which flourished shortly after 3000 BCE and had largely disappeared by around 400 CE. Most texts were written in cuneiform by scribes on stone and clay tablets. Many have survived the centuries, and some are considered the oldest stories in the world. They give historians a valuable window into the ideas and worldview of these peoples.

There is no single account of creation. Different cities and cultures in the region told the origin of the world in their own way, and because stories passed from mouth to mouth, they shifted over time. Related myths share common themes, but the order of events changes depending on when and where a story was written down. It is therefore better to think of a family of related traditions than of one official version.

According to Sumerian cosmology, the universe first appeared when Tiamat and Apsu, two watery elements, gave birth to the first gods, Anshar and Kishar, from whom the rest of the deities descend. Another line of descent begins with the union of An (Anu) and Ki, which produced Enlil, lord of the wind, who eventually became the leader of the gods. Enlil was expelled from Dilmun, the home of the gods, after assaulting Ninlil, and their son Sin, the moon god, also known as Nannar, was born. Sin and Ningal then had Inanna, goddess of love and war, and Utu or Shamash, the sun god. During his exile, Enlil fathered three underworld deities with Ninlil, the most prominent being Nergal. Nammu gave birth to Enki, lord of the Abzu, the watery abyss. Enki also held the me, sacred principles that governed everything from physical laws to social order and law.

The sources do not allow a clear division between Sumerian and Semitic deities, since the two groups shared a pantheon over the centuries. Some texts preserve four cosmogonies associated with the cities of Eridu, Shuruppak, Nippur and Uruk. In Sumerian, nin can mean lady or lord, and en means lord, master or patriarch. Likewise, ki means earth and lil means air, so divine names often hint at the nature of their bearers.

Sumerian myths were meant as simple explanations for ordinary listeners, and they survive mainly in Akkadian texts such as the Enuma Elish and the Atrahasis. They address theological, political and philosophical questions, often placing an everyday figure, usually a man, in his familiar surroundings while subject to the forces of nature, politics or the economy. His fate is set by the gods, and each story ends with a reflection or piece of advice, which is why they can be read as parables. Their themes include the origins of the earth and of things, the ordering of the world and the founding of cities, encounters between gods and humans, the hero, and the lives of the gods themselves.

The Atrahasis opens with the goddess Mami creating humans from clay mixed with the flesh and blood of a slain god, to lighten the gods' labour. Enlil then tries to control the growing population through famine, drought and finally a great flood. Atrahasis survives thanks to a warning from Enki, builds a boat and, after the disaster, placates the gods with sacrifices. A related text, the Eridu Genesis, follows a similar plot with the same flood at its centre, but its tablet is badly damaged, so the story is harder to reconstruct. There the survivor is called Zi-ud-sura. The fragmentary tablet was found at Nippur, while the Akkadian version of Atrahasis was found in the library of Ashurbanipal in northern Iraq.

The Enuma Elish is a Babylonian creation myth whose composition is unclear, though it may date back to the Bronze Age. It was probably recited during the ritual celebration of the Babylonian New Year. It tells of the birth of the gods, the world and humanity, whom the gods created to serve them and ease their work. Its focus is praise of Marduk, the patron god of Babylon, who creates the world, the calendar and mankind. The text serves the interests of that city and its god, which explains why Marduk takes centre stage.

The Epic of Gilgamesh is the best-known work from the region and is often regarded as the oldest surviving literature. It began as separate stories and was not combined into one cohesive epic until the 18th century BCE. It follows the Sumerian king Gilgamesh, typically regarded as a historical figure, and his friend Enkidu through their adventures until Enkidu dies. Grief-stricken and terrified of his own mortality, Gilgamesh then searches for immortality. He fails, accepts his fate and returns to Uruk as a wiser king. Along the way he meets Utnapishtim, the survivor of the flood, who was granted immortality by the gods.

The myth of Adapa also deals with death. According to the tradition, the wise Adapa, gifted with intelligence by Enki, broke the wings of the south wind after it knocked him into the sea. Summoned before An, he was warned by Enki not to eat or drink what was offered to him. An, impressed by his wisdom, offered him the food of immortality, but Adapa refused it out of loyalty to Enki. According to the story, this is why humans die. The myth is often compared with the fall of man in the Bible, although the stories differ considerably.

Several themes recur across these tales. The first is the quest for immortality, which in the Epic of Gilgamesh and the myth of Adapa remains out of human reach. The second is the idea that humans exist to serve the gods, working the fields and offering sacrifices. When people multiply or become troublesome, the gods try to reduce their numbers through plagues, droughts or floods. This reflects a strict hierarchy in which people are subject to divine will. A recent study of Mesopotamian rituals and their links with myth points to a third recurring theme: certain rituals, called me in Sumerian and parṣu in Akkadian, belong to particular gods, and the stories explain how they came into their hands or how the gods handed them over to humankind.

Trivia

Very ancient stories. The Epic of Gilgamesh is often considered the oldest surviving literary work, and its separate stories were joined into one coherent poem around the 18th century BCE.
A flood in several versions. The flood survivor appears under different names depending on the text: Atrahasis, Zi-ud-sura or Utnapishtim.
The me. The me were sacred principles that regulated everything from natural laws to social order, and the god Enki was their keeper.
