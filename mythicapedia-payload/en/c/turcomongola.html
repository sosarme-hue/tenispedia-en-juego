Sky, wolves, horses and heroes of the Turkic peoples

Turkic mythology

Turkic mythology gathers the myths and beliefs of the Turkic peoples, from the Tengrist and shamanic traditions of their earliest period to the legends that developed after many of them converted to Islam. Its divine powers are understood more as personified forces with defined qualities than as members of a fixed pantheon, and its central symbols are the sky, the world tree, the horse and the grey wolf. Its stories still live on in epics, folk tales and ceremonies.

Turkic mythology is the body of myths, legends and beliefs told by the Turkic peoples, whose roots lie in the steppes of Central Asia. It rests mainly on two strata: Tengrism, the oldest belief system attested in writing, and shamanism, which structured people's relationship with spirits and with the natural world. Over time these were joined by themes drawn from nomadic and warrior life, so that many stories speak of riding, herding, hunting and surviving in harsh lands.

The earliest sources are inscriptions in a script of their own, Old Turkic, such as the Orkhon inscriptions and the Kül-Tegin stele. They mention three realms: the sky (tengri), the earth (yer) and the human being (kişi). Tied to these beliefs is the idea of kut, a sacred power or fortune that could be held by a ruler and, in a more general sense, by ordinary people, and which was believed to end at death. Another key source is the Irk Bitig, a 10th-century manuscript found at Dunhuang, written in the same old alphabet and concerned with divination and traditional knowledge.

Unlike the pantheons of Greece or Rome, Turkic mythology has no fixed set of gods. Sacred beings are personified powers, and even when they take human form, their qualities are what stands out. Many of them work somewhat like what Western tradition would call angels or intercessors, passing on the will of higher powers and watching over people. The İye are guardian spirits tied to particular natural elements, such as a river, a mountain or a forest, and they are so numerous that they rarely have personal traits. Other beings include the Çor, a kind of genie, and the Abasi, demons. A widespread belief held that the colour of an unnamed spirit revealed its nature: good spirits were white and evil ones black.

At the centre stands Tengri, the Sky. In the earliest sources he appears as Kök Tengri, the Blue Sky, the first of the primordial deities and the creator. He was thought to govern the fate of all peoples. He acted freely, but he was just, rewarding and punishing, and the wellbeing of people depended on his will. No one knows how he was pictured, since tengri also simply meant the sky. As the Turks migrated and came into contact with monotheistic religions, the belief system changed. According to the account summarised in the sources, two figures came to dominate: Tengri, linked to goodness and to a heavenly abode called Uçmag, and Erlik, linked to evil and the underworld, in a structure that resembles Zoroastrian dualism.

Among the other figures is Umay, goddess of fertility and childbirth, whose name comes from a root that originally meant 'placenta' or 'afterbirth'. She is considered a daughter of Tengri and shares features with mother goddesses found elsewhere. Öd Tengri is the god of time, though little is known about him; the inscriptions call him the ruler of time and a son of Kök Tengri. Boz Tengri is associated with the lands and steppes and is also described as a son of Kök Tengri. Kayra is the spirit of God and is linked to the highest sky, the upper air, space, light and life. From the union of Kayra and Umay comes Ülgen, god of goodness, who commands the aruğ, good spirits that do good on earth. Erlik is the lord of death and the underworld. Ay Dede is the moon, Gün Ana the sun and Alaz the god of fire.

The symbols reflect the life of steppe peoples. The horse was central because it was seen as an extension of the individual, especially of the man, to the point that a person felt complete with his horse. Some scholars think this is the origin of the title at-beyi, 'horse-lord'. Horses appear in funeral rites and burial customs, which, according to a Turkologist cited in the sources, are among the cultural markers that link Turkic peoples from ancient times to the late Middle Ages.

The dragon, called Evren or Ebren, and in Turkish Ejderha, is also shown as a serpent or lizard. In eastern Turkic myths it stands for blessing and goodness, and it is linked to greatness and power. People believed that dragons still lived in the mountains of Central Asia, such as the Tian Shan and the Altai. Although dragons were associated with Tengri, they were not themselves worshipped as gods.

The World Tree, or Tree of Life, may be the most important symbol. It joins the upper, middle and lower worlds. According to the Altai Turks, human beings descended from trees. Among the Yakuts, Ak Ana, the White Mother, sits at the base of the tree, whose branches reach the heavens and shelter creatures born there. The image combines a cosmic tree with a mother goddess who nourishes and sustains life. The deer was seen as the great mediator between gods and humans. At funerals it accompanied the soul of the dead on its journey to the underworld or to the abode of the ancestors. A famous case is that of Geyiklü Baba, 'father deer', a 13th-century holy man who lived with his deer in the mountain forests of Bursa.

The great epics also carry weight. The Grey Wolf legend tells of a young boy who survived a raid on his village. A she-wolf found him, injured, and nursed him back to health. He then fathered children with her, and she gave birth to ten half-wolf, half-human sons. One of them, Ashina, became their leader and founded the clan that ruled the Göktürks and other Turkic nomadic empires. According to the story, the pregnant wolf escaped her enemies by crossing the Western Sea to a cave near the Qocho mountains, a city of the Tocharians. The first Turks later moved to the Altai regions, where they became known as skilled ironworkers. The Ergenekon legend recounts a great crisis: after a military defeat, the Turks took refuge in a valley where they were trapped for four centuries. A blacksmith opened a passage by melting the rock, and the grey wolf led them out. A New Year ceremony still commemorates this legendary escape.

The legend of Oghuz Khagan is another central narrative, with a political character, for the Turks of Central Asia and for the Oghuz who later ruled Anatolia and Iran. Versions survive in the works of medieval historians and in an anonymous Uyghur manuscript. The Book of Dede Korkut contains twelve stories of the Oghuz, preceded by a prologue. It reflects a period when Tengrist elements were still strong in Turkic culture. In 2018 the Korkut Ata heritage, presented by Azerbaijan, Kazakhstan and Turkey, was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. There are also Manichaean tales, such as that of the prince and the three demons, in which the prince shoots three arrows to decide who will receive the magical objects.

The Islamic period brought new stories. The Karakhanids and the Seljuks converted in the 10th century, and during the Middle Ages most Turkic peoples adopted Islam, which blended with older beliefs. Later writers reinterpreted sacred history in a Turkic key, and in the modern Turkish Republic some authors, such as Ismail Hakki Izmirli, argued that prophets and the Quran had a Turkic origin. These claims are not accepted by historians and belong to nationalist politics rather than to ancient mythology, so they should be kept apart from the traditional tales.

Today the legacy remains visible in art and national symbols. A motif of the Tree of Life appears on Turkish 5-kuruş coins, issued since 2009. The flag of the Chuvash Republic, a federal subject of Russia, bears a Tree of Life with three suns, a symbol of renewal. Its deep red stands for the earth, and its gold for prosperity.

Trivia

A word that recalls Buddhism. The Turkish term for a solar eclipse, güneş tutulması, literally means 'the sun has been seized'. It goes back to a story in the Samyutta Nikaya about an asura named Rahu who seizes the sun. After the conversion to Islam the story was forgotten, but the phrase remained in the language.
A treasure in the Irk Bitig. The Irk Bitig, found at Dunhuang, is written in the same old alphabet as the Orkhon inscriptions and is one of the most valuable sources for studying ancient Turkic religion.
The wolf that gave a clan its name. The Ashina clan, which according to the legend descends from a boy raised by a she-wolf, ruled the Göktürks, whose name appears in Chinese sources as T'u-chueh.
