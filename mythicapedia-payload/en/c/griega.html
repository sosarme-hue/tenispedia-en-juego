The gods, heroes and stories that shaped Western culture

Greek mythology

Greek mythology is the body of stories that the ancient Greeks told about their gods and heroes, the origin of the world and the meaning of their cult and ritual practices. It was first passed on orally and later written down in epic poems, plays, hymns and texts from the Roman period. Its influence still runs through Western art, literature and language.

Greek mythology is the body of myths and legends told by the ancient Greeks about their gods and heroes, the origin and nature of the world, and the meaning of their cults and ritual practices. These stories were part of the religion of ancient Greece, whose worship centred mainly on the Olympian gods. Today they are studied as part of classical mythology, a broader label that also covers Roman myth. Modern scholars examine them to understand the religious and political institutions of that civilisation, and also to grasp how myths are created and changed over time.

Long before they were written down, the myths circulated as a poetic oral tradition. Most likely, singers of the Minoan and Mycenaean periods were already spreading these tales from the eighteenth century BC. In time, the stories of the heroes of the Trojan War and its aftermath became part of the material that Homer shaped into the Iliad and the Odyssey. So although we now read these poems as literature, many of their episodes began as songs meant to be heard.

The next major name is Hesiod, a poet who lived at roughly the same time as Homer. Two of his works, the Theogony and the Works and Days, describe the genesis of the world, the succession of divine rulers and the succession of human ages. They also account for the origin of human suffering and for the practice of sacrifice. These poems show how the Greeks tried to explain the order of the universe and the rituals they performed within it.

Myth survives well beyond these great poems. The Homeric Hymns, a collection of pieces addressed to various gods, preserve mythic episodes of their own. From the Epic Cycle, which covered the Trojan War and events around it, only fragments remain. To these we can add lyric poetry, the tragedies and comedies of the fifth century BC, the writings of scholars and poets of the Hellenistic age, and texts from the Roman Empire by authors such as Plutarch and Pausanias. Each writer retold the myths in his own way, so the same story may appear with different emphases depending on the source.

Written texts are not the only evidence. Archaeology adds details that the literature does not always provide. Gods and heroes decorated pottery, votive offerings and many other objects. Geometric pottery of the eighth century BC already shows scenes from the Epic Cycle and the adventures of Heracles. In the Archaic, Classical and Hellenistic periods, Homeric scenes and others from varied traditions multiply and complement what we know from literature. The image therefore becomes a document in its own right, showing which stories were familiar to someone looking at a vase.

The case of Heracles shows this richness well. His labours and adventures appear in texts, in painted images and on everyday objects, which suggests that he was a figure known to very different audiences. The Trojan War worked in a similar way. The Greeks remembered it as an event where glory, loss and the fate of heroes were tightly intertwined, and that emotional weight drew poets back to it again and again.

The legacy of Greek mythology is vast. It has shaped the art, literature and language of Western civilisation and remains part of its cultural heritage. From antiquity to the present, poets, painters, sculptors, playwrights and novelists have found in these stories themes that speak to their own times. That is why, even though these tales come from another era and another religion, they still offer a familiar starting point for reflecting on love, power, guilt and the human condition.

Trivia

A sung tradition. Before they were written down, many of these stories were passed on as sung poetry, which explains their rhythm and repeated formulas.
Myths on pottery. Geometric vases of the eighth century BC already showed scenes from the Epic Cycle and the adventures of Heracles, long before many of the surviving texts were written.
Fragments of the Epic Cycle. Very little of the Epic Cycle survives beyond fragments, so much of what we know comes through summaries and quotations by other authors.
