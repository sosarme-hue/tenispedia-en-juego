Korean mythology

Korean mythology has two main strands: literary myths that narrate the founding of kingdoms within historical chronicles, and oral narratives sung by shamans in rituals still regarded as sacred. Dangun, founder of Gojoseon, and Jumong, founder of Goguryeo, are the best known founding heroes, while shamanic songs describe the creation of the world, the afterlife, and the gods of smallpox and childbirth.

Korean mythology is best understood as two distinct bodies of material. The first is literary mythology, preserved in traditional histories written in Classical Chinese, where myths are woven so tightly into the record of kingdoms that scholars have long struggled to separate legend from fact. The second, far larger and more varied, is oral mythology: songs and narratives performed by shamans during the gut, the ritual ceremonies through which the gods are invoked. Written myths survive on the page, cut off from their ritual setting. Shamanic narratives remain part of living practice, and for those who take part in the ceremonies they are sacred truth rather than entertainment.

The state-foundation myths, known as geonguksinhwa, form the core of the literary tradition. The best known concerns Gojoseon, the earliest Korean kingdom, and its founder Dangun. It is recorded in two works: the Samguk yusa, compiled by the Buddhist monk Iryeon around the late 1270s, and the Jewang ungi, a Chinese-language epic poem from 1287. In Iryeon's telling, Hwanung, a son of the sky god Hwanin, descends with three treasures and a host of followers beneath a sacred tree on Mount Taebaek. A bear and a tiger ask to become human. Hwanung gives them garlic and mugwort and tells them to stay in a cave, away from sunlight, for a hundred days. The tiger gives up. The bear endures the trial, becomes a woman, and prays under the sacred tree for a child. Hwanung takes human form to marry her, and their son Dangun Wanggeom founds Gojoseon at the site of present-day Pyongyang, a date traditionally given as 2333 BCE. He is said to have ruled for fifteen centuries before becoming a mountain god.

Scholars often read the tale as a mythic memory of several groups bound together under different totems: a sky clan, a bear clan, and a tiger clan that seems to vanish from the story. The Japanese myth of Ninigi, who also descends with three treasures, has been compared with it, as have Siberian stories in which a bear is the mother of a tribal ancestor. For centuries Dangun was honored mainly around Pyongyang. In the thirteenth century, intellectuals anxious about Mongol domination promoted him as the ancestor of all Korean peoples. By the twentieth century he had become the mythical founder of the nation, and he plays a role in the official ideologies of both North and South Korea.

Other founding myths follow a different pattern. The story of Goguryeo appears in the Samguk sagi, completed in 1145, and in far greater detail in the Dongmyeongwang-pyeon, a poem by Yi Gyu-bo written in 1193 that probably adds literary embellishment. Here the hero Jumong is born to Yuhwa, a young woman whom the son of the sky god, Haemosu, had seized and then abandoned. After a sunbeam falls on her, she gives birth to an egg, and from it hatches Jumong, a boy of remarkable skill with the bow. He leaves the court, where he has been made a stable-keeper, and flees south with a few companions. Reaching a river they cannot cross, he declares his divine descent, and the fish and turtles form a bridge across the water. He goes on to found Goguryeo. Specialists group such tales with those of the northern type, in which the founder is the child of a celestial father and an earthly mother. In the southern kingdom of Silla, the first ruler descends from heaven in the form of an object and later marries an earthly woman, and his kingship is confirmed by the consensus of local chieftains.

Shamanic mythology has a different character. Korean shamanism, known as musok, is the country's indigenous religion, and its narratives are called musoksinhwa. Each of the twelve parts of the keungut, the great ritual, includes a bonpuri, a story explaining the origin of a god or of a ritual practice. Early Koreans thought in animist terms. They believed that objects had souls and that mountains, rivers, and other places were inhabited by spirits who had to be honored. Shamans, who are usually women and are called mansin in some texts, were believed to be able to communicate with the spirit world. Some early Korean leaders were said to have shamanic qualities or to descend from shamans. Dangun, for example, is sometimes portrayed as a mountain god known as sanshin. Most of this material has been recorded on the island of Jeju and in South Hamgyong Province, now in North Korea.

These narratives cover the creation of the world, the rise of some humans to divinity, and divine punishment of the disrespectful. The Changsega, a creation song from Hamhung, tells how the giant Mireuk separated heaven from earth and propped up the sky with copper pillars. He made the first men from five golden insects and the first women from five silver insects. When another giant, Seokga, won rule over the human world by unfair means, the song presents his victory as the source of evil and sin among people. In the Magohalmi, Grandmother Mago, a giant goddess, shapes the land from clay, stone, and even her own waste. This story survived only through oral performance and never entered formal ritual records. Other tales deal with death. In the Barigongju, a princess abandoned by her father for being his seventh daughter travels to the underworld, returns with the elixir of life, and revives her parents. She then becomes the goddess who guides souls from earth to heaven. The Sonnimgut explains smallpox through a company of gods who spread the disease and punish those who offend them. The Samshin Halmoni, the triple goddess of childbirth, is linked to a story in which a woman gives birth in a cave after her brothers threaten her for shaming the family.

Others are stories of wisdom and reconciliation. The Segyeong bonpuri tells of Jacheongbi, goddess of the earth and of love, who disguises herself as a young man to study and falls in love with a fellow student who turns out to be the son of the Emperor of Heaven. After passing a test of walking on knives through fire, she wins back her husband, and by mediating a quarrel among the gods she brings peace. As a reward she receives five grains and, at her own request, buckwheat, which grows in harsh conditions. This is why she is honored as the goddess of agriculture and love. In the Jeseok bonpuri, a girl becomes pregnant by supernatural means and gives birth to triplets who become gods. Its versions vary considerably from region to region.

Finally, the picture we have depends largely on who wrote it down. As Confucianism gained influence, with its emphasis on pragmatism and rationalism, many shamanic myths were lost. Only a fraction of what once existed was recorded, and the Buddhist monks and Confucian scholars who did the recording often reshaped the stories to fit their own beliefs. Serious study of the oral material began in 1930 but grew in the 1960s, when scholars brought structuralist, comparative, and ritual-based methods to the songs. More recent work compares Korean narratives with those of neighboring cultures, examines the myths of village shrines centered on local guardian gods, and reads the stories through feminist perspectives, given the prominence of women in many narratives and their frequent tension with patriarchal norms.

Trivia

A bear who became a woman. In the Samguk yusa version of the Dangun myth, a bear spends twenty-one days in a cave eating garlic and mugwort and becomes a woman. The tiger that attempted the same trial gave up and remained an animal.
A bridge of fish and turtles. In the Jumong story, the fish and turtles of a river form a bridge so that the fleeing hero and his companions can cross.
A goddess who shapes the land. Mago, the giant grandmother of one creation tale, forms the landscape from clay, stone and her own waste. The story survived only through oral performance and never entered formal ritual records.
