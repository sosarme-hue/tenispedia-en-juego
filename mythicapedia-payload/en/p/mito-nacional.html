Mythology and politics

Stories from the past that shape a nation’s identity

National myth

A national myth is a story about a nation’s past that has been elevated to a symbolic and cherished status, so that the community regards it as true. It may take the form of an epic, a founding myth or part of a civil religion. Its power comes less from historical accuracy than from the meaning a society gives it.

A national myth is a story about a nation’s past that has been raised to a symbolic, cherished level, so that many people regard it as true. It works as an emblem of collective identity and affirms a set of shared values. The word comes from the Greek μῦθος, which originally meant a tale or a word and only later came to be associated with stories of gods and heroes.

It helps to separate a story taken literally from one that nobody believes word for word but which still carries symbolic weight. National myths may exaggerate real events, leave out important details or add elements for which there is no evidence. Their meaning is therefore often contested among different groups within the population. Some take the shape of an epic, some are woven into a civil religion, and in certain cases the nation’s origins are credited to a god, several gods or leaders favoured by the divine.

Many great national epics grew out of the work of poets. Homer’s Iliad was adopted by ancient Greek culture as a justification of its theoretical unity. Virgil, who lived from 70 to 19 BCE, wrote the Aeneid to support the political renewal and reunification of the Roman world after long civil wars. Centuries later, Ferdowsi’s Shahnameh told the mythical and partly historical story of Persia, from the creation of the world to the Muslim conquest of the seventh century. In the nineteenth century, Elias Lönnrot gathered Karelian and Finnish oral poetry into the Kalevala, which helped shape Finnish identity. Camões composed Os Lusíadas in Macao as Portugal’s national epic.

Other myths rest on two basic ideas: the permanence of a community and common ancestry. In Armenia, according to a history attributed to Movses Khorenatsi, the founding father Hayk defeated Belus, a tyrannical giant who ruled Babylon, and led his people to Ararat. In Greece, tradition traces the Hellenes to Hellen, who according to different accounts was the son of Deucalion or of Zeus.

Japan provides an example of a founding myth with an official date. According to the Nihon Shoki and the Kojiki, Emperor Jimmu descended from the sun goddess Amaterasu and established a power base in Yamato. His accession is traditionally dated to 660 BCE, and his legendary anniversary is now marked as National Foundation Day on 11 February. There is no evidence that Jimmu existed, although a powerful dynasty probably held sway in what is now Miyazaki Prefecture during the Kofun period. In Korea, the first kingdom is credited to Dangun, a god-king who founded Gojoseon in 2333 BCE. His legend first appears in the thirteenth-century Samguk Yusa, and it has been established that no relevant record exists in the Chinese Book of Wei. Around seventeen religious groups still worship Dangun.

National myths serve social and political ends, and they often function as propaganda sponsored by the state. In totalitarian dictatorships, a leader may be given a supernatural life story that makes him seem almost divine. The Pahlavi dynasty of Iran (1925–1979) deliberately drew on heroes from the Shahnameh to build an identity centred on the pre-Islamic past, presenting the seventh-century Arab conquest as a tragedy that had halted Persian greatness. This account appeared in textbooks, speeches and monuments, and culminated in the 1971 celebration of the Persian monarchy. In Brazil, the sociologist Gilberto Freyre argued in his 1933 book Casa-Grande & Senzala for the idea of racial democracy, which became a source of national pride, although its reach remains debated.

In Israel, the Promised Land, given by God to Abraham and his descendants, serves as a founding myth with roots in ancient Jewish texts. During the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, Zionism gave it political meaning, using it to justify Jewish migration to Mandate-era Palestine. Leon Uris’s novel Exodus (1958) carried the story to a worldwide audience. The myth remains present in education and commemoration, and it is also contested in the Israeli–Palestinian conflict, because different readings of it bear on territorial claims.

National myths are not limited to authoritarian regimes. In liberal societies they can inspire civic virtue and sacrifice, or consolidate the power of dominant groups. Their link with violence is also part of their history: scholars note that they can fuel conflict, exaggerate threats from other groups and understate the costs of war. When studying them, it helps to distinguish the traditional story, historical research and the political interpretation of each era.

Trivia

A national day with a legendary date. Japan marks 11 February as National Foundation Day, commemorating the legendary accession of Emperor Jimmu, traditionally dated to 660 BCE. There is no evidence that he existed.
A poem rooted in oral tradition. The Kalevala, published in the nineteenth century, brings together oral poetry from Karelian and Finnish tradition collected by Elias Lönnrot. It helped shape Finnish identity.
An epic of great length. Ferdowsi’s Shahnameh contains about 50,000 couplets, making it one of the longest epic poems in the world and the longest composed by a single author.
