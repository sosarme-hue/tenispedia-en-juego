Mythology and politics

Myth, the Strait of Gibraltar and a symbol that ended up on coins

Pillars of Hercules

In antiquity, the Pillars of Hercules were the two promontories flanking the entrance to the Strait of Gibraltar. Myth links them to the tenth labour of Heracles and to the edge of the known world. Over time they became an emblem of the Spanish crown and of its presence in the Americas.

The Pillars of Hercules are one of the most enduring images that Greco-Roman mythology left to later cultures. In antiquity the name referred to the two promontories that flank the entrance to the Strait of Gibraltar, and for a long time they marked the boundary between the Mediterranean and the outer ocean. Today the name still refers to a real landscape, but also to a story, a motto and a symbol that moved from heraldry onto coins.

Geographically, the northern pillar, known as Kalpe or Calpe, is identified with the Rock of Gibraltar. The southern pillar, Ábila or Abila, has been disputed throughout history, and the two most likely candidates are Monte Hacho, in Ceuta, and Jebel Musa, in Morocco. The Greeks called them the Stelai of Heracles, and the Romans knew them as Columnae Herculis. Pliny the Elder mentions the name in his geographical descriptions.

The link with myth is clearest in the tenth labour of Heracles. According to tradition, Eurystheus, king of Tiryns and Mycenae, set the hero twelve formidable tasks, and the sources do not agree on why he was bound to serve. In some versions, Heracles had killed his own children in a fit of madness, and the Oracle of Delphi ordered him to serve Eurystheus for twelve years in order to be purified. In others, the labours were simply the price of his freedom. The family story also varies. Heracles was the son of Alcmene and Zeus, but Hera, jealous, hastened the birth of the son of King Sthenelus, so that the power went to Eurystheus instead of the hero.

The tenth labour was to bring back the cattle of Geryon, a monster with two legs, three torsos, six arms and three heads, grandson of Medusa. According to the myth, the journey took Heracles to the western edge of the world, to the island of Erytheia, in what is now the Bay of Cádiz. There a two-headed dog related to Cerberus and the Hydra guarded the herd. On the way the hero crossed northern Libya, where he defeated the giant Antaeus, son of Gaia and Poseidon, and borrowed the Cup of Helios to sail across the ocean.

Many accounts say that, on reaching that far point, Heracles raised the two columns as a sign of his passage. The sources offer different explanations. The poet Pindar, in a lost fragment quoted by Strabo, called the mountains the gates of Gades and treated them as the farthest limit Heracles reached. Diodorus Siculus argued instead that the hero did not open a passage but narrowed a channel that already existed, to stop sea monsters from the Atlantic entering the Mediterranean. Some Roman versions place the garden of the Hesperides beyond the mountain that had once been Atlas, which Heracles split apart to join the ocean and the sea.

The mythical frontier also shaped how people thought about the world. Plato placed the legendary island of Atlantis beyond the Pillars in his dialogues Timaeus and Critias. In the Renaissance a tradition spread that the columns bore the warning Ne plus ultra, meaning that no one should go any further. Later the motto was turned around into a statement of ambition: Plus ultra, meaning further beyond.

The story of the columns is also tied to the Phoenicians. Strabo describes a temple of the Tyrian Heracles at Gades, associated with the Phoenician god Melqart, which held two bronze columns that many visitors believed to be the true Pillars of Hercules. Strabo himself judged this claim fraudulent, since the inscriptions spoke only of the cost of making them. The Phoenicians also founded colonies on the Atlantic coast of Morocco, including Lixus, Chellah and Mogador.

The symbol entered heraldry under Charles I of Spain, who adopted it in 1516 at a chapter of the Order of the Golden Fleece held in Brussels, following a suggestion from his adviser Luigi Marliano. Linked to the motto Plus ultra, it came to stand for the expansion of the Spanish dominions. The columns appeared on the arms granted to American towns, such as Villa Rica de la Veracruz in 1523 and San Juan Bautista in Tabasco in 1598. They no longer appear in the king's own arms, but they remain in the coat of arms of Spain.

The symbol also circulated on money. The Spanish piece of eight, a silver coin minted by the Spanish empire, was widely used for centuries, and its reverse showed the two columns with a ribbon bearing Plus ultra. It is commonly explained that the simplification of this design gave rise to the dollar sign. The word dollar also has roots in the coin: English speakers called it the Spanish daller, and the piece of eight was the first legal tender in the United States until a law of 1857 ended its use.

Today the Pillars appear in very different places. On the Spanish coast at Los Barrios, twin towers draw their inspiration from them. In the Central Library of the National Autonomous University of Mexico, a mural by Juan O'Gorman shows them as an allusion to Mexico's colonial past, and in Ceuta a modern statue depicts Heracles pushing apart two large bronze pillars. Dante places Ulysses beyond them in Canto XXVI of the Inferno, and Francis Bacon drew them on the title page of his Instauratio Magna. In each case, the myth becomes a way of talking about the limits of knowledge and of power.

Trivia

A coin with a famous name. The word dollar comes from the Spanish daller, the name English speakers gave the Spanish piece of eight, a silver coin that was legal tender in the United States until 1857.
A motto that reversed its meaning. According to Renaissance tradition, the columns carried the warning Non plus ultra, go no further. Charles I turned it into Plus ultra, further beyond, as a symbol of expansion.
Three pillars in a Syriac text. The Syriac compendium Ktaba d'ellat koll 'ellan is unusual in claiming that there were three columns rather than two.
