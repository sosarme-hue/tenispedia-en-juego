The personification of the French Republic

Marianne

Marianne is the allegorical figure who personifies the French Republic and is one of its most recognisable national symbols. Shown as a woman wearing a Phrygian cap, she embodies republican values and the idea of a protective motherland. Her image did not appear all at once; it changed with the needs of each regime.

Marianne is the national personification of the French Republic and one of its most recognisable symbols. Shown as a woman wearing a Phrygian cap, she stands for the motto 'liberty, equality, fraternity' and for the idea of a motherland that is at once passionate, warlike, peaceful and protective. As with many political myths, her image was never fixed; it was reshaped again and again to suit the needs of each period.

Classical antiquity had a long habit of representing abstract ideas through gods, goddesses and personifications, and the French Revolution of 1789 followed that habit. Allegorical figures of Liberty and Reason multiplied, until they merged into a single woman, seated or standing, surrounded by attributes such as the cockade and the Phrygian cap. The historian Maurice Agulhon, who studied the origins of Marianne in detail, suggested that the choice of a female figure reflected French traditions and ways of thinking, and also marked a break with a monarchy embodied by kings. It is worth noting that in French 'France', 'the Republic', 'liberty' and 'reason' are all feminine nouns, while before 1789 the kingdom was often shown through male figures.

Marianne's first major public appearance was on a medal of July 1789 celebrating the storming of the Bastille and other early events of the Revolution. Between 1789 and September 1792 her image was overshadowed by figures such as Mercury and Minerva. The change came when the First French Republic looked for a new official image. The National Convention decreed that the state seal should show a standing woman holding a spear topped by the Phrygian cap. This version was relatively conservative and, in the words of later commentators, 'not exactly aggressive', reflecting the moderate Girondins, who wanted to move away from the frantic violence of the early revolutionary days.

The image soon changed again. By 1793, with the start of the Terror, Marianne had become fiercer: a bare-breasted woman with a defiant face, sometimes shown leading men into battle. After the Girondin deputies were arrested in October 1793, the Convention wanted an even more radical emblem and for a time used the figure of Hercules to represent the Republic. Historians note that the increasingly radical imagery ran parallel to the violence known as the Reign of Terror.

When the Terror ended, the Directory needed a message that was more civil and peaceful. In the official vignette of 1798, Marianne returns wearing the Phrygian cap, but without a pike, leaning languidly on the tablet of the Constitution of Year III and looking to one side rather than straight out at the viewer. The Directory fell in 1799 after a coup led by Sieyès and Napoleon Bonaparte, yet the figure kept adapting. According to scholars, what allowed her to endure was her abstraction and impersonality: what she symbolised was so flexible that successive powers could use her for their own purposes.

In 1848, with the Second Republic, the Ministry of the Interior launched a competition to represent the new regime, because no official image existed. Two rival Mariannes were authorised. One is combative, bare-breasted and with an arm raised, recalling the Greek goddess Athena. The other is calmer, with rays of sun around her head, a royal symbol transferred to the Republic, and surrounded by wheat, a plough and the fasces of Roman lictors. They embodied two visions of the Republic, one bourgeois and one democratic and social. Town halls chose to display her willingly, often with their backs to the church, and she appeared on a French postage stamp in 1849. Under the Second Empire (1852–1870) the image became clandestine and was used as a form of protest. The name 'Marianne' spread across France around 1875.

The origin of the name is uncertain, but one proposal links it to Juan de Mariana, a Spanish Jesuit born in Talavera de la Reina (1536–1624), a natural law philosopher who taught in Paris between 1569 and 1574. During the Revolution, counter-revolutionary aristocrats called the people's representatives 'marianos', a jab at his ideas. The revolutionaries took the name up, mainly because it stressed the image of a nourishing mother protecting her children. Republicans in the Midi also associated it with their political ideal: an Occitan song, La garisou de Mariano, was very popular in the autumn of 1792. Because the name was accepted by both supporters and opponents of the Republic, it spread throughout the country.

Under the Third Republic (1870–1940) her use became more official. Her neutrality made her acceptable to most people, whereas the memory of the Revolution divided the French, each group having its own heroes and villains. For that reason the state preferred abstract symbols to historical personalities. In 1880 the Hôtel de Ville in Paris displayed a statue of Marianne wearing a Phrygian cap, and other cities soon followed. Aimé-Jules Dalou lost the contest for the Place de la République, but the city built his monument on the Place de la Nation, inaugurated in 1889 as a plaster version and finished in bronze in 1899, with a blacksmith representing labour beside her. The final inauguration took place amid the turmoil of the Dreyfus Affair and a large workers' demonstration carrying red flags, showing how the labour movement claimed her as the symbol of a social republic.

The myth also featured in foreign relations and political quarrels. After the Entente Cordiale of 1904, Marianne and John Bull appeared together in paintings and cartoons. In the right-wing press of the early twentieth century she was often denigrated as a prostitute. In Imperial Germany she was portrayed in crude terms as a symbol of weakness: propaganda used her to present France as a 'weak' and 'feminine' nation, in contrast to a 'strong' and 'masculine' Germany. The American historian Michael Nolan described a world of hyper-masculine militarism in which manliness and military power were equated. Such images aimed to foster contempt for France and to warn Germans about what they should not become. German cartoons usually set Marianne against a male figure, while French cartoons pitted her against Kaiser Wilhelm II.

The story of Marianne shows that a single symbol can serve national unity, protest, political debate or exclusionary propaganda. Her history belongs both to modern mythology and to the history of France, and she remains present today on public buildings, stamps and coins.

Trivia

A symbol on coins and stamps. Marianne's profile appears on French euro coins and postage stamps, and she is officially used on most government documents.
A cap with a history. The Phrygian cap worn by Marianne became a sign of liberty during the Revolution and has been one of her attributes since the 1790s.
Famous faces. Several official busts have used celebrities as models, among them Brigitte Bardot in 1970, Catherine Deneuve in 1985 and Laetitia Casta in 2000.
