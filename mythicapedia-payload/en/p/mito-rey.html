When royal power was presented as a mandate from God

Divine right of kings

The doctrine of divine right holds that a king's authority comes from God rather than from any earthly power, whether the people, the Pope or a parliament. Although it grew strong in post-Reformation Europe, its roots lie in the Bible, in medieval political thought and in traditions as different as the Chinese, Japanese and Zoroastrian. This page looks at its origins, its defenders, its critics and its cultural variants.

The divine right of kings holds that the monarch answers to no earthly authority, because the right to rule comes from God. Neither the people, nor the aristocracy, nor a parliament, nor even the Pope could judge a king. It followed that only divine authority could judge him, and that deposing a king or restricting his prerogatives went against God's will. Rather than a single political theory, it was a cluster of ideas, and in practice monarchs faced considerable limits on their power.

The notion that God had granted earthly power to the monarch was familiar long before the phrase was coined. In the Middle Ages, political order was described through the Res publica Christiana, a decentralised system in which a regime's legitimacy depended on Lex Aeterna, the divine plan for the world, which rational beings could know through natural law. Human laws and local customs were meant to conform to that natural law, and the Church had the final word on whether a king did so. This is why excommunication was feared by monarchs: it effectively undermined the legitimacy of their rule. Thomas Aquinas accepted tyrannicide in cases of usurpation, but he rejected the deposition of a legitimate king by his subjects. In his reasoning, only the Pope could do so.

Christian texts offered several precedents. The Old Testament tells how God chose kings for Israel, beginning with Saul and then David, whose dynasty continued. In the New Testament, the Letter to the Romans says that existing authorities are appointed by God, and the Gospel of Matthew records the instruction to give to Caesar what belongs to Caesar. In the Gospel of John, Jesus tells Pilate that his authority comes from above. Later, the alliance between the Church and emperors such as Constantine, Theodosius or Charlemagne reinforced the idea of royal power with religious backing. In The City of God, Augustine argued that the earthly city had been established by God to serve his purposes, so that questioning rulers was, in a sense, questioning God.

After the Protestant Reformation, and during the religious conflicts of the following centuries, the doctrine became a justification for absolutism. In England, Henry VIII declared himself Supreme Head of the Church, and the idea was further developed by James VI of Scotland and I of England, who set out his views in 1597-98. Robert Filmer, in Patriarcha (1680), linked the king's authority to that of Adam, to whom God was said to have given rule over the family and creation. The monarch's power would thus resemble that of a father over his children. In France, Jean Bodin, who was influenced by Calvinism, argued that sovereign princes had been appointed by God, and Jacques Bossuet defended the absolutism of Louis XIV by likening rebellion against the king to rebellion against God. Bossuet wrote, as is commonly reported, to protect the monarchy from the upheavals of the Fronde. The traditional account holds that the notion was ultimately swept away by the French Revolution.

The doctrine also faced critics within Catholic thought. The Spanish Jesuit Juan de Mariana, in De rege et regis institutione (1599), argued that society was formed through a pact and that, in certain circumstances, tyrannicide could be justified. Cardinal Robert Bellarmine did not believe that monarchy had any specific divine sanction, and he answered James I, who replied with his Preface to Monitorial of 1609. Francisco Suárez, in his Defensio fidei catholicae (1613), held that the political power of kings does not come directly from God but from the people organised as a political community, while the spiritual power of the Pope does have a divine origin.

Other traditions developed similar ideas along their own paths. In China, the concept of Tian, or the Mandate of Heaven, granted rule to a just sovereign, the 'Son of Heaven'. If a ruler lost the mandate through injustice or incapacity, his overthrow was read as a sign that he no longer deserved it. In Japan, the emperor was descended from the sun goddess Amaterasu, and his right to reign was considered less conditional, although in practice real power often rested with regents, ministers or the shōgun. In Zoroastrianism, the concept of Khvarenah expresses a divine glory or favour that sustains kings. In Judaism, the debate over monarchy, based on 1 Samuel 8, has been long-running: some rabbinical sources understood the choice of a king as an act of the people, while Maimonides concluded that Judaism supports the institution of monarchy.

The divine right of kings thus expresses an idea found across very different cultures: that political power has a sacred origin. Its practical application varied widely across time and place, and its defenders and critics used it to justify or to limit the power of monarchs.

Trivia

A motto still in use. According to the account of his trial at the Diet of Speyer in 1193, Richard I of England was the first to use the motto Dieu et mon droit. It remains the motto of the British monarch.
A ram as a sign. In the Kar-namag of Ardashir, a ram that follows Ardashir I is interpreted as the manifestation of the Khvarenah leaving Artabanus V. His advisers explain to him that divine favour is passing to his rival.
A blessing before a monarch. Jewish law prescribes a special blessing upon seeing a monarch, acknowledging that God has given some of his glory to a human being.
