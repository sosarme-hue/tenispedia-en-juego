The medieval stories of King Arthur, Brutus of Troy and the legends of the British Isles

Matter of Britain

The Matter of Britain is a collective name for medieval texts about Celtic traditions, the legendary history of Britain and Brittany, and above all King Arthur and his knights. The name was coined in the twelfth century to set these stories apart from two other great narrative traditions, and its core was built on works such as Geoffrey of Monmouth's History of the Kings of Britain.

The Matter of Britain is the body of medieval literature that centres on the British Isles, on Brittany in France and, above all, on King Arthur and his knights. The name was coined in the twelfth century by the French poet Jean Bodel, who used it to separate these stories from two other great traditions: the Matter of Rome, which drew on classical antiquity, and the Matter of France, which dealt with the paladins of Charlemagne and their battles against Moors and Saracens.

Within this material sits a legendary history of Britain that was presented as a people's own past. The Historia Brittonum, dating from the ninth century, is the oldest surviving source for Brutus of Troy, a hero who is said to have reached the island after the fall of Troy. It is traditionally attributed to Nennius, although its real compiler is unknown and the text survives in several versions. The story caught on because it linked the island to the scattering of heroes that followed the Trojan War, much as Virgil had tied the founding of Rome to that same war in the Aeneid.

Geoffrey of Monmouth took up this material in his History of the Kings of Britain, written in the twelfth century, and expanded it enormously. Among his best-known inventions is the claim that the Trinovantes, a Celtic tribe, took their name from "Troy-novant", or "New Troy". He also named a king called Coel, whose daughter Helena married Constantius Chlorus and became the mother of Constantine the Great, so that the Roman imperial line was traced back to British ancestors. His book also carried the Arthur material, in which the post-Roman Britons under Arthur conquer much of Europe, Rome included, in the manner of the great conquerors of antiquity.

These stories also had a political life. For the new Norman England, Geoffrey's material served as a national story: it cast the Norman Conquest as a kind of restoration of the Celtic Britons, freed from their old enemies, the Anglo-Saxons. This shows how a myth could help shape a shared identity while remaining, at heart, a literary creation.

King legends also overlapped with figures from Celtic myth. Some scholars have suggested that Leir of Britain, who later became Shakespeare's King Lear, was originally the sea god Lir of Irish tradition, or Llŷr in its Welsh form. In the same way, Morgan le Fay has been linked with the Irish war goddess the Morrígan and with Modron, a Welsh water deity. These identifications grew popular with the comparative study of religions in the late nineteenth century, and they are still debated. Many researchers accept that Arthurian material has Celtic roots, but by the twelfth century those roots had been reshaped by Christianity and transformed, or simply forgotten.

Shakespeare knew these stories well. His plays draw on King Lear and Cymbeline, and the chronicler Raphael Holinshed, in his Chronicles of England, Scotland, and Ireland, also retold these tales; the same chronicle is among the sources for Macbeth. It is thought that a Welsh schoolmaster, Thomas Jenkins, first introduced him to this material.

The Scots built a mythical history of their own, with royal lines reaching back to the Picts and the kingdom of Dál Riata. Although these lines eventually became historical, their origins are hazy and blend British and Irish traditions. The story of Gabrán, in particular, combines both.

The Arthurian legend is the best-known part of the Matter of Britain. In the early thirteenth century the so-called Vulgate or Lancelot-Grail Cycle gathered interlinked episodes covering the whole span of Arthur's reign. It has two main threads. One presents Camelot as a utopia of chivalric virtue that falls apart through the moral failings of Arthur, Gawain and Lancelot. The other follows the Holy Grail and the quests of the knights: some succeed, such as Galahad, Bors and Perceval, while others fail. Chrétien de Troyes and Robert de Boron introduced or popularised many of the motifs that followed, including the Grail, Camelot, Excalibur, Merlin and the romance between Lancelot and Guinevere. The Post-Vulgate Cycle came after, and in the fifteenth century Thomas Malory compiled Le Morte d'Arthur in English, a work that remains hugely influential.

The story of Tristan and Iseult is also part of this world, above all through the great Prose Tristan. Arthurian tales are full of Christian themes and of courtly love, which sets them apart from older narratives.

In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, Romanticism and the Celtic revival led many writers to link the cycle again with Celtic mythology, often in heavily reconstructed versions. Joseph Campbell, among others, read the Grail tradition as an allegory of human development and spiritual growth. Other scholars, such as Jessie L. Weston, looked to ritual and ancient nature cults for the origins of its imagery, an interpretation that is now little accepted. The debate over whether a historical Arthur ever existed also continues, with many candidates proposed for the role.

Trivia

A name with three parts. In the twelfth century the poet Jean Bodel distinguished three great narrative matters: Rome, France and Britain. The classification is still used today to organise medieval literature.
A Lear with sea roots. It has been proposed that Geoffrey of Monmouth's Leir, later Shakespeare's King Lear, descends from the Celtic sea god Lir or Llŷr. This remains a hypothesis rather than a proven fact.
A book that lasted. Thomas Malory's Le Morte d'Arthur, compiled in the fifteenth century, became the reference English version of the Arthurian myth and was reprinted and adapted for centuries.
