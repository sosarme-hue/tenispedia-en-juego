The Roman idea of what it meant to be Roman

Romanitas

Romanitas is the set of political and cultural concepts through which the Romans defined their identity. The Latin term barely appears in ancient sources, but modern historians use it to describe how Rome saw itself. Its ideals, such as the citizen-soldier-farmer, are illustrated by stories like that of Cincinnatus.

Romanitas is the set of political and cultural ideas and practices through which the Romans defined themselves. It is a Latin word that can be roughly translated as Roman-ness, or the Roman way. Modern historians use it as a shorthand for Roman identity and for the image the Romans had of themselves. It was not a common word in ancient sources.

According to the surviving texts, the term first appears in De pallio, a work by Tertullian, an early Christian writer from North Africa in the third century. He used it in a disparaging way to criticise those in his native Carthage who imitated Roman culture. The word was therefore born with a critical edge, and only later became a tool for historical analysis.

To be Roman meant, in essence, belonging to a political and religious community that shared values, customs, morality and a way of life. Historians have approached this identity in different ways. One is to examine the ideals that the Romans attributed to themselves. Another, which enjoys broad agreement among specialists, looks at how Roman identity was built during the process of colonisation. Some scholars, however, doubt that the notion of identity, borrowed from the social sciences, is adequate for understanding what it meant to be Roman.

The Roman ideal was summed up in the figure of the citizen, soldier and farmer. The farmer was a hardworking, prudent and practical man who worked the land with his own hands. The soldier was brave and strong, obeyed orders and risked his life in the name of Rome. Before Gaius Marius shaped the standing army, Rome relied on a militia-style defence force that was called up in wartime and disbanded in peacetime.

According to tradition, the model of this homo militaris was Lucius Quinctius Cincinnatus. The legend tells how he was tending his farm when a messenger arrived to tell him that Rome was under attack and that he had been elected dictator. At first he was reluctant to accept, but the Senate pleaded with him. He defeated the enemy within a matter of weeks and, although most of his six-month term with absolute power remained, he returned to his farm. His name came to stand for selfless service to the public good.

Another highly valued quality was gravitas, a mix of seriousness, dignity and commitment that Romans of the early Republic and their intellectuals saw as the most defining trait of their society. Honesty was seen above all in the handling of public money. The Greek historian Polybius drew a sharp contrast: a Greek statesman entrusted with a single talent, even with ten clerks checking him and many seals and witnesses, might still fail to keep his word. Romans who handled large sums in their magistracies and embassies, by contrast, kept their faith out of pure respect for their oath.

According to the ancient accounts, this civic culture led the Romans to develop self-government by adopting a republic, and the citizens who embodied these values formed the backbone of that Republic. Over time, the ideal of the homo militaris also took root in the colonies and in the early United States, thanks to the wide influence of classical Roman literature.

In the end, romanitas offers a window into how the Romans saw themselves: not a single trait, but a set of civic, military and moral virtues passed on through stories and examples.

Trivia

A term with a critical edge. Tertullian used the word romanitas to criticise people in Carthage who imitated Roman customs. Its original use was therefore disparaging.
A dictator who went back to the farm. According to tradition, Cincinnatus returned to his farm while most of his six months as dictator still remained, giving up absolute power.
Honest magistrates. Polybius contrasts the way Romans handled large sums of public money with Greek statesmen, whom he portrays as less faithful to their word.
