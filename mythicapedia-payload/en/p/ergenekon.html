Mythology and politics

The valley of a Turkic and Mongolic founding epic and its political echoes

Ergenekon

Ergenekon is a Turkish name with several uses. Its oldest meaning is a myth, the Epic of Ergenekon, which tradition regards as the origin story of the Turkic and Mongolic peoples. Later, the name also entered political debate in modern Turkey.

Ergenekon is a Turkish term that can refer to several things. The oldest is a myth, the Epic of Ergenekon, which Turkic and Mongolic peoples regard as the story of their origins. The name also appears in other contexts: a Turkish officer, Necabettin Ergenekon, who died in 2020, a village in Cyprus known in Greek as Agios Chariton, and an alleged clandestine organization that became the subject of a much-debated legal process. This article focuses on the myth and on how its name has since been used in politics.

According to the traditional story, a people became trapped in a valley surrounded by almost impassable mountains. With no visible way out, its members could not leave, and life in the valley grew harder. The versions of the myth differ in their details, but they agree that the community needed a way to escape and that deliverance came with the help of a guiding animal, a grey wolf that appears in many narratives from the region.

According to the story, the wolf showed the way through a mountain of iron. The answer lay in fire. The people gathered wood and charcoal, lit fires and worked bellows to melt the metal and open a passage. When the mountain gave way, the people left the valley and spread across the surrounding lands. In the different versions, this ending marks the beginning of a new stage, in which the community regains its freedom of movement and divides into groups that would eventually form different peoples.

Scholars of myth have proposed several readings, and none is definitive. Some see in the story a symbolic memory of migration from an enclosed place toward more open territories. Others highlight the wolf as a guide and a figure of kinship with the group, while others stress fire and iron-working as symbols of skill and collective strength. These are modern interpretations. The myth was passed on as a tradition's story, not as a historical record of specific events.

It is precisely because the story expresses a shared origin and a departure toward a future of their own that the Epic of Ergenekon has been important to the identity of the peoples who claim it. Its image of a community that frees itself through its own effort has been taken up at different moments as a symbol of unity and pride.

That symbolic use later crossed paths with politics in contemporary Turkey. The name Ergenekon was used to designate an alleged clandestine organization, secular and ultra-nationalist, whose existence and scope were the subject of accusations and intense debate. These accusations reached the courts in the so-called Ergenekon trials, which dealt with cases connected to the alleged network. Weapons were found during the investigation, and proceedings were also opened against people who had reported on the case. The course and conclusions of these trials have been widely disputed, and the public and analysts continue to judge them in different ways.

Today, then, the word Ergenekon refers both to an ancient story of origin and to a recent legal controversy. The myth keeps its value as a narrative of a community that leaves an enclosed valley through fire, collective labour and an animal guide. The history of the name also shows how a traditional story can acquire new meanings once it enters the territory of national identity and political dispute.

Trivia

A wolf as guide. In the epic, a grey wolf shows the people the way out. The figure of a guiding wolf appears in other traditions of the region.
One name, several uses. The word Ergenekon names a myth, a Turkish officer, a village in Cyprus and an alleged organization, so it is always necessary to clarify which one is meant.
Fire and iron. In the story, the way out is opened by melting a mountain of iron with fire and bellows, a detail many scholars read as a symbol of collective labour.
