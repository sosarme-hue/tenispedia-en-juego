The mythical homeland of the Mexica

Aztlán

In Mexica tradition, Aztlán is the mythical homeland from which the peoples who would later found Tenochtitlan set out. Colonial sources describe it in conflicting ways, and its location remains unresolved. Over time the name has also become a symbol for political and cultural movements.

According to Mexica tradition, Aztlán was the place of origin of the peoples who would later found Tenochtitlan. The word aztecah, from Classical Nahuatl, meant "people from Aztlán," and it is the root of the word "Aztec" that we use today. Several colonial-era sources mention the place. Their lists of migrating groups differ, but all of them include the Mexica among the travellers.

The sources do not agree on what Aztlán was like. Some legends picture it as a paradise without sickness or death. The Codex Aubin, by contrast, describes a land ruled by a tyrannical elite known as the Azteca-Chicomoztoca. Tradition also tells of the Nahua peoples emerging from Chicomoztoc, "the Place of the Seven Caves," where each cave gave rise to a group: the Xochimilca, Tlahuica, Acolhua, Tlaxcalteca, Tepaneca, Chalca and Mexica. After leaving the caves, these groups settled somewhere near Aztlán, though the texts do not agree on how near.

In the Codex Aubin, the Aztecs flee Aztlán under the guidance of a priest. On the road, their god Huitzilopochtli forbids them to call themselves Azteca and tells them to be known as Mexica. Another version of the journey begins with a man who hears a bird urging him to go. The chief, who had long been waiting for a sign, gathers the people. They carry an idol of Huitzilopochtli, which the priests bear and through which, the story says, the god speaks. They must walk until they reach a large lake and there look for an eagle perched on a cactus.

The journey, in the telling, lasted about two hundred years and included a stay in Tollan, the old Toltec capital. Some people remained there while others moved on. Now and then Huitzilopochtli took the form of a white eagle to encourage them, until they reached Lake Texcoco and saw an eagle on a cactus holding a serpent. There they founded Tenochtitlan. Even the dates are debated. A 2004 translation of the Anales de Tlatelolco gives a date that corresponds to 1064–1065, and some researchers have linked the start of the migration to the observation of a supernova in 1054.

The search for a real location has occupied many scholars. In the sixteenth century, the friar Diego Durán recounts that the emperor Moctezuma I sent an expedition to find Aztlán, and that the travellers found a place with similar features, although they could not pin down its exact position. Toward the end of the nineteenth century, the historian Alfredo Chavero proposed linking Aztlán with the island of Mexcaltitán in Nayarit, and Wigberto Jiménez Moreno later considered this idea valid. Cristóbal del Castillo, for his part, mentions that the lake around the island was called Metztliapan, or "Lake of the Moon." Other researchers think that Aztlán cannot be located with the evidence available and see it as a symbolic place.

The meaning of the name is uncertain too. The Crónica Mexicáyotl explains it as "place of herons," but under Nahuatl grammar that form would be Aztatlan, not Aztlán. Other suggestions include "place of whiteness" and "place near the tools," which share the element āz-.

In the twentieth century, Aztlán took on a new meaning within the Chicano movement in the United States. In 1969 the poet Alurista read a poem in Denver that became known as the preamble to El Plan Espiritual de Aztlán. For some activists, Aztlán refers to the lands of the American Southwest that passed to the United States after the Mexican-American War of 1846–1848 and the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, and they assert a historical right to that land. Some have proposed independence or a new northern republic, while other groups, such as MEChA and the Brown Berets, have used the name mainly as a symbol of identity and heritage. Many Chicanos understand Aztlán more as a spiritual force than as a concrete territory.

Aztlán also appears in fiction and popular culture, from Rudolfo Anaya's novel Heart of Aztlán and Max Brooks's World War Z to the role-playing game Shadowrun and an album by the Mexican band Zoé. Its story remains a meeting point between ancient myth, historical research and present-day identities.

Trivia

A land with many readings. Colonial sources describe Aztlán as a paradise in some versions and as a land ruled by tyrants in others.
The name of the Aztecs. The word Aztec comes from aztecah, which means "people from Aztlán" in Classical Nahuatl.
An island in Nayarit. The island of Mexcaltitán, in Nayarit, is one of the proposed locations of Aztlán, although the hypothesis is still debated.
