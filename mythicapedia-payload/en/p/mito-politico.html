Mythology and politics

Stories that give a community meaning and identity

Political myth

A political myth is a narrative about a person, situation or event in public life that generates collective meaning and identity. Its power does not depend on its facts surviving historical checking, but on the group sharing it accepting it as valid in its essentials. The concept sits at the crossroads of political science, history and the study of culture.

A political myth is an emotional and intellectual story about a historical figure, a political situation or an event of this kind, one with a collective effect that generates meaning and identity. Its strength lies in the fact that it presents itself as self-evident. It can build bridges across social and cultural divisions and reduce relationships that would otherwise be hard to grasp to a simple scheme.

Its most characteristic feature is that the facts are not read according to what historians can verify, but in a narratively selective and stereotyped way. A political myth can even be a complete fabrication and still support an ideology. Nor does it rest on a transcendent world, as religious myths do. Its centre is usually historical memory, although it may also look towards the future.

The British political scientist Henry Tudor, in his book Political Myth (1972), argued that what makes a myth political is its subject matter. A narrative becomes a myth when it is cast in dramatic form, around a decisive event placed in the past that orders present experience, and when it serves a practical argument. Its protagonist is usually a community, presented as destined to create a morally coherent world. For Tudor, the myth offers an account of past and future in whose light the present can be understood, and its effectiveness depends on that practical argument being accepted as true.

Building on Tudor, Christopher Flood proposed a working definition: an ideologically marked narrative that claims to give a true account of political events from the past, present or future, and that a social group accepts as valid in its essentials. The definition highlights two ideas: the ideological charge of the story and the social acceptance that gives it force.

The relationship with ideology is one of the most debated questions. Andreas Dörner warned that myth and ideology are often used as synonyms, which sometimes blurs the boundary with theory. He stressed the narrative side of myth, a symbolically condensed sequence of events. Yves Bizeul, following Cornelius Castoriadis, understood ideology as a set of action-oriented convictions, and saw in it the soil in which myths and utopias flourish. For Bizeul, broadly speaking, the political myth is part of ideology.

Bizeul also pointed to a difference in how they work. The ancient, preindustrial myth bound people together and fostered cohesion, whereas ideologies of isolation and closure tended to divide. The ancient myth thrived on an either-and structure, while ideology follows an either-or logic. Bizeul noted that this openness has partly been lost in modern political myths, which were used to set a national, social, racial or ideological group deliberately apart from others. Yet that separation, he argued, does not come from the myth itself but from the role it plays in the service of ideology.

Myth and utopia have both kinship and difference. Bizeul, like the historian Heidi Hein-Kircher, saw in the political myth a desire to eliminate complexity. Utopia, by contrast, is driven by hopes of a total reconciliation and, ultimately, by the wish to abolish politics itself. Utopias are projects aimed at an imagined future, while the ancient myth recounts an act of creation that gives direction to the present. Drawing on Jan Assmann, Bizeul rejected the idea that political myths are always conservative, since a utopian memory persists within them and can have emancipatory force. In his words, myth and utopia both have a double face, regressive and progressive, and both serve to liberate as well as to legitimise domination.

The relationship with legend is also debated. Gilbert Durand stretched the concept of myth so that it covers the whole imaginary. Dörner, by contrast, distinguished political myths and ideologies as collectively anchored sources of meaning, unlike sagas, fairy tales or legends, whose link to reality is more playful. The philosopher Reinhard Brandt observed that when speaking of the state, the word myth can loosely replace the word legend, but not the other way round. Bizeul held that the political myth, unlike the fairy tale, always contains a part of truth.

Mexican muralism, which became national art in the 1940s, offers an example. The researcher Oliver Schulz showed how new myths were built from old ones, through the suppression of time and place, blurring legend and reality and linking historical events with present-day figures.

Classifications vary from author to author. Dieter Nohlen distinguished historical myths, such as national epics and decisive battles like Kosovo, Trafalgar, Waterloo, Verdun or Stalingrad, from future-oriented myths linked to revolution or progress. Hein-Kircher proposed myths of persons, events, spaces and times. Myths of persons present the leader as a father or teacher of the nation; myths of space sanctify territories; myths of time recall periods of splendour regarded as foundational.

Herfried Münkler identifies three forms in which political myths appear: the story, the image and staged ritual. The story must be told again and again, each time differently, since that variation keeps the myth from hardening into dogma. The image, such as history painting or a monument, fixes one of those variants. Staged ritual appears on commemorative days, with speeches and parades.

Finally, Ernst Cassirer spoke of a truth of memory that lies not in a reference to the past but in a living present that makes up the collective history. The truth of a political myth, then, should not be understood as scientific verifiability, but as efficacy: its capacity to lend credibility and to legitimise a social order.
