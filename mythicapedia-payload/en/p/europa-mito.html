The Phoenician princess carried off by Zeus and the origin of a continent's name

Europa (mythology)

Europa was a Phoenician princess who, according to Greek myth, was carried to Crete by Zeus disguised as a white bull. Ancient texts preserve several versions of her family and of the details of her story. Her tale has inspired artists for centuries and, by tradition, has given its name to the continent of Europe.

In Greek mythology, Europa was a princess of Phoenician origin. According to the myth, Zeus saw her gathering flowers in a meadow with her companions and fell in love with her. To approach her without frightening her, he took the form of a white bull and mingled with her father's herds. The princess came near the animal, stroked it and eventually climbed onto its back. The bull then ran to the sea, swam with her to the island of Crete, and there Zeus revealed his true identity.

The sources do not agree about Europa's family. The oldest texts make her the daughter of Phoenix, a name linked to Phoenicia, while later tradition makes her the daughter of Agenor, king of Tyre. Her mother's name changes as well, appearing as Telephassa, Argiope or Perimede depending on the author. In the most widely known versions, Europa had brothers such as Cadmus, to whom tradition credits the arrival of the alphabet in Greece, and Cilix, who gave his name to Cilicia in what is now Turkey. After reaching Crete, she had three sons with Zeus: Minos, Rhadamanthus and Sarpedon. She later married Asterion, king of Crete, who adopted her children. Minos and Rhadamanthus appear in later tradition as judges of the dead.

The story also includes divine gifts. Zeus gave her a necklace made by Hephaestus and three remarkable objects: Talos, a bronze automaton who guarded the island; Laelaps, a hound that never failed to catch its prey; and a javelin that never missed. Eratosthenes says that Zeus later placed the figure of the bull among the stars, in the constellation Taurus. Acusilaus, by contrast, identified this bull with the Cretan Bull that Heracles captured and that fathered the Minotaur. Other authors assign the abduction to Tauros, a Cretan king or general, so the name of the abductor also changes from one source to another.

Ovid described the seduction in his Metamorphoses with picturesque detail, the kind found in anecdote and fable. In ancient art Europa is shown riding the bull astride on archaic vases and on a fragment of a metope from Sikyon, or sitting gracefully sideways in a North African mosaic. In all of these she shows no fear. Sometimes she steadies herself by touching one of the bull's horns, as if calming it. This trusting image contrasts with the violence suggested by other versions.

Herodotus offered a rationalized account. In his telling, Europa was kidnapped by Cretans who wanted to avenge the abduction of Io, a princess of Argos. Palaephatus spoke of a Cretan named Tauros who waged war near Tyre and carried off many girls, Europa among them. In the second century AD, the writer Lucian of Samosata reported that a temple of Astarte in Sidon was said by some priests to be sacred to Europa, and that the city's coins showed her seated on the bull.

Scholars have proposed various readings. Some see in the myth a memory of Dorian raids on the coasts of Phoenicia, perhaps accompanied by the abduction of women in pre-Hellenic Greece. The researcher Francoise Gange reads the abduction as a move from the age of the mother goddess to an all-powerful patriarchy. Others connect the bull with Near Eastern cults, where sacred animals and goddesses were closely linked. At Lebadea, Pausanias noted that Europa was an epithet of Demeter, and on Crete the festival of the Hellotia was celebrated in her honour.

The name Europa has given rise to several theories. One links it to Greek words meaning 'wide' and 'eye' or 'face', so that it would mean broad-faced, an epithet similar to that of Athena. Another proposal, put forward by Klein and Semerano in 1966, connects it with a Semitic root meaning 'sunset' or 'west'. M. L. West judged the phonetic resemblance to those Semitic forms to be very weak. Over time the name came to designate a region of Thrace and then the continent. Today Europa appears on euro banknotes since 2013, in the watermark and hologram, inspired by a Greek krater in the Louvre. The metal europium, named in 1901, and one of Jupiter's moons also bear her name.

Trivia

A coin from Sidon. According to Lucian of Samosata, coins from Sidon showed Europa seated on the bull, linking the princess with that Phoenician city.
A name on banknotes. Since May 2013, euro banknotes have carried an image of Europa in the watermark and hologram, inspired by a Greek krater in the Louvre.
A metal and a moon. The rare-earth metal europium was named in 1901 after the continent, and one of Jupiter's moons bears the princess's name.
