Mythology and politics

Virgil's epic about the Trojan hero Aeneas and the mythical origins of Rome

The Aeneid

The Aeneid is a Latin epic written by Virgil between 29 and 19 BC. It follows the Trojan Aeneas from the ruin of Troy to Italy, where, according to the myth, his descendants became the ancestors of the Romans. The poem was left unfinished and is regarded as the high point of Latin literature.

The Aeneid (in Latin, Aeneis) is an epic in dactylic hexameter that tells the legendary story of Aeneas, a Trojan who escaped the burning of his city and reached Italy. Virgil wrote it between 29 and 19 BC. It runs to about 9,896 lines, and around sixty of them were left incomplete when the poet died. Even so, it is widely considered his masterpiece.

Aeneas was not a new figure. He appears in the Iliad as one of the Trojan heroes, but there he has few distinctive traits. The stories about his adventures after the fall of Troy circulated as scattered episodes. From the sixth century BC, Greek colonies in the West often linked their new lands to the myths of their homeland, and in some traditions Aeneas was already said to have founded settlements in Italy. The Romans absorbed these legends as they came into contact with Greek colonies, a process that scholars usually place, tentatively, after the war against Pyrrhus in 280 BC.

Virgil gathered these scattered tales into a single founding myth. His poem ties Rome to the war of Troy, explains the origins of the rivalry with Carthage, and celebrates traditional Roman virtues. According to the reference text, it also presented the Julio-Claudian dynasty as heirs to the founders, heroes and gods of Rome and Troy. Although it is a work of myth, its political purpose is one of the aspects scholars most often discuss.

The poem has twelve books, compared with the twenty-four songs of each Homeric epic, and it falls into two halves. Books I to VI follow the model of the Odyssey and recount the voyage to Italy. Books VII to XII, modelled on the Iliad, tell of the war in Latium. The story opens in medias res, with the Trojan fleet in the western Mediterranean. Juno, hostile to the Trojans, asks Aeolus to unleash a storm, and Neptune, annoyed at the intrusion into his realm, calms the waters. The Trojans reach the coast of Africa, where Venus, disguised as a huntress, guides Aeneas to the city of Carthage.

The episode of Dido is among the best known. The queen had sworn never to remarry after the death of her husband, Sychaeus, but she falls in love with Aeneas through a scheme of Venus and Cupid. When Jupiter orders the Trojan to continue his journey, Dido takes her own life, cursing his descendants. In the story, this act foreshadows the lasting enmity between Carthage and Rome that led to the Punic Wars. In books II and III, Aeneas recounts to Dido the fall of Troy, including the episode of the horse, the death of Laocoön and the loss of his wife Creusa, as well as his wanderings through Thrace, Delos, Crete and Sicily. Book VI describes his descent into the underworld, guided by the Sibyl of Cumae, where his father Anchises shows him the future glory of Rome.

The making of the poem has its own story. Augustus had asked Virgil for a great epic, and the poet tested passages by reading them aloud. According to tradition, he travelled to Greece around 19 BC to revise the work, but he fell ill on the way back and died at Brundisium on 21 September. He is said to have asked for the manuscript to be burned because he considered it unfinished, but Augustus refused and ordered its publication by his literary executors, Lucius Varius Rufus and Plotius Tucca, with as few changes as possible. This is why some lines do not complete a full hexameter.

Virgil drew on Latin predecessors as well. Naevius had written a Punic War, and above all Ennius composed the Annals, the great model of Roman epic in his time. In several passages Virgil quotes lines of Ennius, sometimes almost word for word. The Aeneid, in short, reworks the Greek tradition while engaging with the history and identity of Rome.

Trivia

A poem its author did not want published. According to tradition, Virgil asked for the manuscript to be burned before he died, but Augustus refused and ordered its publication.
Lines left unfinished. About sixty lines were left incomplete, and some do not form a full hexameter because they consist of a single hemistich.
A hero from the Iliad. Aeneas already appeared in the Iliad as a Trojan hero, though with few distinctive traits, and Virgil gave him the personality we know.
