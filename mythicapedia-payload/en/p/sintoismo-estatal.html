Mythology and politics

Japan's state religion and the political use of myth

State Shinto

State Shinto was the ideology promoted by the Japanese government from the Meiji era until defeat in the Second World War, centred on the divinity of the emperor. It drew on Shinto practice and its myths, but reshaped them within a nationalist and administrative framework. Historians still debate whether it was a religion in its own right.

State Shinto was the ideology promoted by the government of Japan from the start of the Meiji era in 1868 until defeat in the Second World War. It drew on Shinto practice, merged the rites of the Imperial Court with those performed at shrines, and became the state religion of the Empire of Japan. Its tone was nationalistic: it aimed to recover the country's original and pure customs, dating from before the arrival of foreign belief systems such as Buddhism, Confucianism, Taoism and Christianity.

Scholars disagree about whether it was truly an independent religion. According to the historian Kuroda Toshio, the idea of Shinto as a native religion only took shape with the Kokugaku movement of Motoori Norinaga. Other interpreters see it more as a moral and ethical code, with mystical overtones and a strong emphasis on respect for tradition. The term “State Shinto” did not originate in Japan. It was first used abroad by foreign Japanologists, and its Japanese translation was not made official until after the war.

To understand its roots, it helps to look further back. The oldest surviving chronicles, the Kojiki and the Nihonshoki, mix historical and mythological material. According to the tradition they record, the imperial family descended from the sun goddess Amaterasu, and the great shrine at Ise, where the sacred mirror Yata no Kagami is kept as one of the three Imperial Regalia, was the most important centre of worship. Before these texts, local communities revered their own kami, spirits or tutelary deities tied to a place and a clan. The ritual sites of those clans were the forerunners of the shrines.

When Buddhism arrived in the sixth century, Shinto began to live alongside other traditions. For centuries it blended with Buddhism, so that honouring the kami and revering the Buddha were regarded as compatible. During the Nara period the court promoted a network of shrines linked to imperial power, and in the Heian period the Engishiki, completed in 927, set out in great detail the rituals, offerings and duties of the priestesses at Ise and Kamo.

The great break came in the Meiji era. After the imperial restoration, the government tried to separate Buddhism from Shinto and in 1871 made the shrines state property. Supporters of the new policy held that the emperor was of divine descent and that this was a matter of history, not only of faith. Because the Meiji Constitution guaranteed freedom of religion, the state presented Shinto as a moral and patriotic tradition rather than a religion. This allowed it to require participation in civic ceremonies without imposing a belief. The administrative separation of 1900, which created a dedicated bureau for shrines, reinforced this distinction.

In practice, the state funded fewer and fewer shrines, applied financial limits and encouraged local shrines to integrate into community life. According to the sources, nearly 80,000 shrines closed or merged with neighbours. Public schools, civic events and ceremonies honouring the emperor became part of everyday life. Some priests adopted these directives on their own initiative, while others objected to the claim that Shinto was not religious. Intellectuals such as Yanagita Kunio questioned that separation, and alternative movements such as Omotokyo suffered persecution.

The system never had a single, coherent legal framework. It developed through individual laws and administrative measures, which made it unstable, and repeated attempts to unify it failed. In 1940 the state created the Institute of Divinities, which extended its control over shrines. After the war, the Shinto Directive of 15 December 1945, issued by the Supreme Commander of the Allied Powers, dismantled the system, separated Shinto from state administration and classified it as one religion among others in Japan. Shrine Shinto survives today, with its kami, myths and festivals, but without the political role it once played.

Trivia

One emperor, many shrines. Of the 124 Japanese emperors, only 20 have dedicated shrines. Emperor worship was not a central tradition of ancient Shinto.
Shinto as “education”. The state described Shinto as a tradition beginning in the age of the gods, rather than as a religion. This allowed it to require participation in civic rites.
From Kokugaku to the state. In the Meiji era Hirata Atsutane called for a return to National Learning to remove Buddhist influence from Shinto. His nativist approach shaped later ideas.
