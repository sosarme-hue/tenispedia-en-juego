Mythology and politics

A copper giant in the Teutoburg Forest that tells the story of Arminius, Rome and German identity

Hermann Monument

The Hermann Monument stands on the Grotenburg hill near Detmold and commemorates the victory of the Cheruscan chieftain Arminius over Roman legions in 9 AD. It was built between 1838 and 1875, at a time when the story of Arminius was becoming a symbol of German national unity. Its history shows how an ancient tale can be reshaped to fit the political needs of each era.

The monument commemorates an episode that ancient writers describe in some detail. In 9 AD, Arminius, a chieftain of the Cherusci and a commander of auxiliary cavalry serving Rome, turned against his allies. According to the account that has come down to us, he ambushed three legions led by the governor Publius Quinctilius Varus, and most of them were destroyed. For Rome and Emperor Augustus it was a catastrophe, known as the Varian disaster. Many historians later came to see it as a turning point that may have helped halt Roman expansion into Germania, although that reading is a modern interpretation rather than settled fact.

The name Hermann came later. From the 1520s Arminius was linked with it, perhaps first by Martin Luther. In the nineteenth century German nationalism grew as a challenge to aristocratic rule, and the idea that a nation consisted of all its people rather than only its rulers was then revolutionary. Arminius became a symbol in this climate: he was said to have "united" the Germanic tribes, while Roman historians also recorded their internal quarrels, which supporters of unity preferred to overlook. Literature helped fix the image. Friedrich Gottlieb Klopstock wrote three dramas on the subject between 1769 and 1789, and Heinrich von Kleist did most to popularise Arminius with his Hermannsschlacht of 1808, written after the old empire had dissolved and Prussia had been defeated by Napoleon. Arminius was cast as the "Father of the German Nation" for those who wanted to resist France, seen as the heir of the Roman Empire.

Work began when Ernst von Bandel arrived in the Teutoburg Forest in 1836, dreaming of a monument to the chieftain. He considered the Externsteine but settled on the Grotenburg. In 1837 an association, the Verein für das Hermannsdenkmal, was founded in Detmold to raise funds, and Prince Leopold II approved the project only for that site. Earthworks began in July 1838 and the foundation stone was laid in October. On a journey to Italy, Bandel met King Ludwig I of Bavaria, who offered financial support in return for a change of design: the rock meant to crown the temple was replaced by a domed gallery. Criticism and doubts about the project's finances soon followed. In 1839 Karl Friedrich Schinkel and Christian Daniel Rauch proposed an alternative, but the Prussian king preferred Bandel's more martial version.

Money remained a constant problem. The pedestal was finished in 1844, overshooting its budget by 4,000 thaler, which caused a rift between the association and Bandel, who returned to Hanover in 1846. The German revolution of 1848 failed to create a unified state, and donations dwindled to almost nothing. Bandel carried on with his own money, and from 1862 he worked on the copper plates. After Prussia defeated Austria in 1866 and annexed the Kingdom of Hanover, its former king took renewed interest in the project. In 1869 King William I of Prussia visited the workshop. After the Franco-Prussian War and the creation of the new German Empire, the monument seemed to capture the spirit of the moment, and the Reichstag allocated 10,000 thaler to complete it. It was inaugurated on 16 August 1875 in the presence of the emperor and the crown prince, with between 20,000 and 30,000 people taking part. Although it was symbolically handed to the German people, the association remained its legal owner until it was dissolved in 1881.

From the outset the monument was presented as a memorial to the war against France, and it coincided with the Kulturkampf, the struggle between the Prussian state and the Catholic Church. Arminius thus became a convenient emblem of "another victory over Rome". Catholics at first, and later social democrats, trade unionists and communists, did not identify with the monument. The railway reached Detmold in 1881 and tourists began to arrive, a few hundred a year at first, but 20,500 by 1895. In 1909 the 1900th anniversary of the battle was marked with a multi-day event of parades, costumes and re-enactments. During the First World War the monument was used as a tool of military propaganda, suggesting that the war would end in a German victory like those of Arminius or of 1870-71.

In the Weimar Republic the monument became a meeting place for nationalist, monarchist and conservative right-wing groups, while the government kept its distance. The 1925 anniversary was dominated by the political right and included a long-distance relay, the Hermannslauf, which began at sixteen points across Germany. Adolf Hitler visited the monument in 1926, and after 1930 the Lippe branch of the NSDAP used it for many rallies. After the Nazis took power in 1933, the Detmold government tried to have the monument declared a pilgrimage site of the German nation, but the government in Berlin refused. The monument featured in Nazi propaganda, and the events of 1935 and 1941 focused on presenting Hitler as the successor of Arminius. In 1936 it received 191,000 visitors.

After 1945 efforts were made to depoliticise the site. The 75th anniversary in 1950 was low-key and mainly promoted the region as a holiday destination. In the 1960s and 1970s annual visitors rose to 400,000, and the monument was seen mainly as a tourist sight, although its political meaning still lingered. In 1975 and 2000 critical debate about the nineteenth-century image of Arminius and the monument's role in shaping ideas of the Germanic past was included for the first time.

The statue faces west, the direction from which, in the designer's view, Varus' troops came, and also towards France, regarded at the time as Germany's hereditary enemy. The warrior wears a winged helmet, though Bandel knew Germanic peoples did not use such helmets; the wings were meant as a heraldic nod to the German eagle. Under his left foot lies a Roman eagle, beside a fasces, the symbol of Roman judicial authority. Gold letters on the sword read "German unity is my strength, Germany's might is my strength", and the shield bears the word "Treufest", roughly "ever faithful".

The pedestal imitates a classical monopteros, a round temple of ten columns supporting Gothic arches. Gothic, which actually originated in France, was then regarded as a "truly German" style. Inside, a spiral staircase of 75 steps climbs to the platform beneath the statue. Three niches carry inscriptions: a Latin quotation from Tacitus' Annals that describes Arminius as the liberator of Germania; a text on the wars of liberation against Napoleon that presents Prussia as the leader of the nation; and a relief of Emperor William I, honoured as the victor over France and the unifier of Germany.

Trivia

An eagle under the foot. The statue's left foot rests on a Roman eagle, the standard of the legions, and beside it lies a fasces, the symbol of Roman judicial authority.
A helmet that was not Germanic. Bandel knew Germanic peoples did not wear winged helmets, but he added wings to suggest the German heraldic eagle.
Built from copper. The statue is made of around 200 riveted copper plates on an iron frame, with an estimated 11.8 metric tons of copper.
