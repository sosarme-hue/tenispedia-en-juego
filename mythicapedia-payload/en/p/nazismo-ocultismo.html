Between documented history and modern mythology

Occultism in Nazism

The idea that Nazism had hidden occult roots has circulated since the early 1940s, but much of it comes from later accounts rather than hard evidence. Historians separate the real links between Nazi thinkers and esoteric currents in late nineteenth and early twentieth century Germany and Austria from the popular mythology that portrayed Hitler and his followers as puppets of supernatural forces.

The association between Nazism and occultism appears in a wide range of theories, speculation and research about the movement's origins and its possible links to esoteric traditions. These ideas have circulated in popular culture since the early 1940s, during the Second World War, and regained popularity from the 1960s onwards.

The most serious starting point for the debate is the work of the British historian Nicholas Goodrick-Clarke, The Occult Roots of Nazism (1985). He argued that there were real connections between some ideas of Ariosophy and Nazi ideology. Ariosophy was a racist, esoteric movement that carried weight in German and Austrian nationalism during the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. According to the author, it grew out of the Völkisch movement, a traditionalist, pan-German reaction to industrialisation and urbanisation, but it linked the troubles of modern life to the supposed misdeeds of Freemasonry, Kabbalah and Rosicrucianism, in order to show that the modern world rested on false and evil principles. Its ideas and symbols reached several antisemitic and nationalist groups in late Wilhelmine Germany, and from them the early Nazi Party emerged in Munich after the First World War. Goodrick-Clarke also documents links between two Ariosophists and Heinrich Himmler.

The same author was highly critical of much of the popular literature on the subject. In his view, many books exaggerated the link between Nazism and the occult to the point of presenting the phenomenon as the product of arcane and demonic influences. He judged most of the books on Nazi occultism published between 1960 and 1975 as sensational and under-researched. He proposed separating empirical and sociological work from this modern mythology, which he called the Nazi Mysteries and, more broadly, a form of crypto-history.

The story of that speculation has a traceable path. The Austrian writer Hans Thomas Hakl places its first formulations in works from the early 1940s. As early as 1933, a writer using the pseudonym Kurt van Emsen had described Hitler as a "demonic personality", but the text was soon forgotten. The first hints that Hitler was guided by occult forces come from the French Christian esotericist René Kopp, who in two articles in the journal Le Chariot, in 1934 and 1939, sought the source of Hitler's power in the supernatural. In 1939 Edouard Saby published Hitler et les Forces Occultes, a book that already mentioned Hanussen and Ignaz Trebitsch-Lincoln. Hakl regards Saby as a key precursor of the myth.

That same year brought Hermann Rauschning's Hitler Speaks, which is often cited as an early source. In it Hitler is described as surrendering himself to forces that carried him away, a spell that could be called demonic magic. Most historians now regard those conversations as unreliable; Goodrick-Clarke concludes that they were largely invented, though he sees in them an imagined but recognisable voice of Hitler. In 1940 Lewis Spence, in Occult Causes of the Present War, credited Alfred Rosenberg and his book The Myth of the Twentieth Century with spreading pagan, occult and anti-Christian ideas that had motivated the party.

The image took firm shape in the 1960s. Louis Pauwels and Jacques Bergier's The Morning of the Magicians (1960) is often seen as the origin of the genre, since its second half is devoted to Nazi connections with the occult. According to Goodrick-Clarke, such accounts portrayed Hitler and the Nazis as controlled by a hidden power, whether a disembodied entity or a magical elite in a remote age or place. Many of these works, such as Trevor Ravenscroft's The Spear of Destiny (1972) or J. H. Brennan's The Occult Reich (1974), relied on doubtful documents or unverified events.

The debate also has concrete episodes. Letters and diaries have been cited, but several turned out to be forgeries or inventions. One example is The Medusa's Head (1991), attributed to John Symonds, which presented supposed conversations between Aleister Crowley and Hitler and is now regarded as fiction. Another is Erik Jan Hanussen, a Viennese clairvoyant who made political predictions in his own newspaper in the late 1920s. No firm evidence shows that Hitler knew him before 1927, nor that his influence was decisive.

Science fiction was not untouched either. The researcher Nagl examined German novels such as Edmund Kiss's The Last Queen of Atlantis, which promoted racial superiority under the banner of Ariosophy, and argued that such works could justify an elitist political order rather than simply reflect a cultural heritage.

Today academic work distinguishes occultism from propaganda. The Nazi movement did draw on symbols, Germanic myths and a mystique of race and destiny to legitimise itself and mobilise the population, and leaders such as Himmler were interested in esoteric history. But the idea that the regime was directed by supernatural forces is not supported by the available sources. There is also no evidence for a supposed secret plan by Hitler and the Thule Society to create a New World Order. What history documents is the political use of myths, with its consequences of exclusion, antisemitism and violence, and the lasting fascination these myths continue to attract.

Trivia

A highly influential book. The Morning of the Magicians (1960), by Louis Pauwels and Jacques Bergier, is considered by many authors the origin of the genre on Nazi occultism, although much of its content is conspiratorial rather than historical.
Doubtful dialogues. The conversations Hermann Rauschning attributed to Hitler in 1939 are now considered largely invented by most historians.
A letter with no evidence. A 1949 letter from René Guénon to Julius Evola mentions a supposed contact between Aleister Crowley and Hitler, but offers no sources or evidence.
