Mythology and politics

When past, language and myth became political identity

Romantic nationalism

Romantic nationalism holds that a state draws its legitimacy from the organic unity of those it governs, a unity built on language, culture, customs and, depending on the account, race or religion. It arose in the nineteenth century as a reaction against dynastic and imperial rule, and its influence reached art, folklore and national epics.

Romantic nationalism, also called organic or identity nationalism, is a form of nationalism in which a state claims its legitimacy from the natural unity of the people it governs. That unity rests on language, race, ethnicity, culture, religion and customs, understood in the older sense of a nation as those born within a shared culture. The idea can apply to ethnic nationalism as well as to civic nationalism. It emerged as a reaction against dynastic and imperial power, which justified authority from the top down, from a monarch whose rule might itself be traced to a god or the gods, as in the divine right of kings or the Mandate of Heaven.

From a mythological point of view, the movement changed how people saw their traditions. If every people had a spirit of its own, then vernacular languages, folk tales and local customs stopped being curiosities and became expressions of identity. This linked artistic creation with political thought. The nation, defined by its language, history and cultural character, became both an ideal for artists and a tool for raising political awareness.

The year most often cited as a turning point is 1848, when a wave of revolutions swept across Europe. Many uprisings failed and the old order was soon restored, but they opened the way to liberalisation and to the modern nation states. Earlier, romantic ideas had been present in the Greek War of Independence (1821-1830), in which the poet Lord Byron died of a high fever, and in the Belgian Revolution of 1830-1831, which began with a riot in Brussels during a performance of La muette de Portici, an opera about a doomed romance set against foreign oppression. Music played its own part as well. Rossini's William Tell (1829) launched romantic opera with the national myth that united Switzerland, and Verdi's choruses, such as «Va pensiero» from Nabucco (1842), inspired two generations of Italian patriots.

The romantic impulse mixed with economic and political forces. It helped shape the unification of Germany and Italy, and movements that sought to build nations around ethnic groups in the Balkans, along the Baltic and in the heart of Central Europe, where the Habsburgs eventually gave way. In Norway, romanticism found expression less in literature than in the search for a national style in architecture and daily life. Even earlier, in North America, the American colonists' declaration of independence and the Constitution of 1787 combined Enlightenment reason with a romantic nationalist rhetoric, and the revolts in the Spanish colonies, beginning with Argentina's May Revolution of 1810, drew on increasingly local identities.

After Napoleon's fall, conservative powers led by Klemens von Metternich shaped European politics. The balance of power and the Concert of Europe kept control in the hands of a few large empires: Russia, the restored French monarchy, the German Confederation under Prussian leadership, Austria and the Ottoman Empire. Cultural movements forming around peoples who were becoming conscious of their own identity pressed against this order until 1848. Those revolutions failed, yet they marked the beginning of a process that ended the Concert and took more than a century and a half to complete. The two World Wars, and then the collapse of the Soviet Union, Yugoslavia and Czechoslovakia at the end of the twentieth century, produced many new states.

Romantic nationalism also encouraged the collection of folklore. The Brothers Grimm believed that fairy tales, if they were not contaminated by outside literary sources, had passed down almost unchanged for thousands of years and revealed the primordial character of a people. For that reason they discarded tales that resembled those of Perrault, and they kept Sleeping Beauty because the story of Brynhildr convinced them of its German origin. In later editions they changed the language, turning fairies into enchantresses or wise women, and princes and princesses into the king's sons and daughters. They regarded Basile's Pentamerone as the first national collection of fairy tales, because of its Neapolitan voice. Their example influenced Afanasyev in Russia, Asbjørnsen and Moe in Norway, and Joseph Jacobs, who was Australian.

The idea of a «national epic» is another product of this current: a long, heavily mythologised poem of central importance to a nation. The manuscript of Beowulf, first transcribed in 1818, had sat for two centuries among scholars' collections before it was read as the Anglo-Saxon epic people felt they lacked. Few paused over the fact that its hero was a Geat. In Greece, the Iliad and the Odyssey gained new urgency during the War of Independence. In the Russian Empire, national minorities resisting Russification produced or revived poems such as the Estonian Kalevipoeg, the Finnish Kalevala, the Polish Pan Tadeusz and the Latvian Lāčplēsis.

In Germany, the movement accompanied the revival of Prussia after its defeat by Napoleon at Jena in 1806. Fichte, Kleist and Arndt helped shape a nationalism that was more ethnic than civic, and it would mark the country for much of the following century and a half. Romanticism also popularised myths such as that of Emperor Frederick Barbarossa asleep in the Kyffhäuser mountain, expected to rise and save Germany, and the legend of the Lorelei. Later, the Nazi movement appropriated elements of this tradition. Alfred Rosenberg presented romanticism as a racial precursor that needed to be freed from its excesses, and in May 1933 Joseph Goebbels told theatre directors that German art should be heroic and national. After the war, critics such as Thomas Mann revised their earlier support for romanticism. Heinrich Heine had already parodied these modern revivals in 1844 in Germany. A Winter's Tale.

In Poland, romanticism was decisive after the failure of an uprising crushed under Nicholas I. Adam Mickiewicz argued that Poland was the «Messiah of Nations», destined to suffer as Christ had suffered in order to save other peoples. The image appears strongly in Dziady, a patriotic drama directed against the Russians. During the foreign occupations, the Catholic Church had been a bulwark of the Polish language and identity. Scholars read the work in different ways: some stress its moral or Christian dimension, while Zdzisław Kępiński focused on Slavic pagan and occult elements.

By the 1870s, romantic nationalism had become a recognisable artistic movement. Bedřich Smetana's symphonic poem Vltava is a musical example. In Scandinavia and the Slavic lands, artists looked for a style that was culturally meaningful without simply copying the past. The Church of the Savior on Blood in St Petersburg was built in a style that evoked traditional Russian features. In Finland, the reassembly of the Kalevala inspired paintings and murals, with Akseli Gallen-Kallela as the foremost figure. By the turn of the century, ethnic self-determination was widely seen as progressive and liberal, and separatist movements emerged in Finland, Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania.

Trivia

A forgotten manuscript. The only surviving manuscript of Beowulf sat for almost two centuries among scholars' collections with little attention. Its rediscovery and transcription in 1818 coincided with romantic interest in national epics.
Fairies turned into wise women. In their editions, the Brothers Grimm replaced the word fairy with enchantress or wise woman, and princes with the king's sons, so that their tales would sound more German.
A sleeping emperor. The myth of Frederick Barbarossa asleep in the Kyffhäuser, who would awaken to save Germany, was popularised by romanticism and reappeared in later political debates.
