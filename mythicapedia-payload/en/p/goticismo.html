Sweden's belief in a Gothic origin and its mark on architecture

Gothicism

Gothicism was an ethno-cultural ideology and cultural movement in Sweden that gave Swedes reason for pride, since they were believed to be related to the Goths. Its supporters held that this people had come from Scandinavia and looked for evidence in ancient texts, medieval chronicles and Nordic manuscripts. It lost force in the 18th century, was revived by Romantic nationalism, and left a lasting mark on wooden architecture.

Gothicism was an ethno-cultural ideology and cultural movement that arose in Sweden and gave Swedes a sense of pride in being, supposedly, related to the Goths. Its starting point was a claim about where that people had come from: Gothicists believed the Goths had originated in Scandinavia, and not somewhere else.

The belief rested largely on Jordanes, the author of a history of the Goths that places an original homeland in Scandza, a northern land. From there, Gothicists added a further tradition: that Theodoric the Great, the Ostrogoth who came to power in the Roman Empire, had Scandinavian ancestry. For them, a northern people conquering Rome was proof of valour and shared greatness.

The idea was older than the movement. Medieval chronicles already described the Goths as ancestors of the Scandinavians. At the Council of Basel, Nicolaus Ragvaldi used it to argue that the Swedish monarchy was the foremost in Europe. Later, the brothers Johannes and Olaus Magnus took it up in works that shaped Swedish scholarship: Johannes wrote Historia de omnibus Gothorum Sueonumque regibus, and Olaus wrote Historia de gentibus septentrionalibus. Together with Ragvaldi, they are regarded as the founders of the movement.

The view was never accepted without reservations. Respected historians, such as Carlo Troya, a scholar of early medieval Italy, debated it and treated it with scepticism. Nor was it confined to Sweden. In Denmark, some scholars tried to identify the Goths with the Jutes, but the idea never took hold in society as it did in Sweden. Danes of the period did not base their political legitimacy on their land being the original homeland of the Goths.

In the 17th century, when Sweden was a great power after the Thirty Years' War, the belief reached its peak. Danes and Swedes competed to gather and publish Icelandic manuscripts, Norse sagas and the two Eddas. In Sweden these texts became part of an origin myth: they were seen as proof that the greatness and heroism of the ancient Geats had been handed down to the present population. This pride culminated in Olaus Rudbeck's treatise Atland eller Manheim (1679-1702), in which he argued that Sweden was identical to Atlantis.

In the 18th century, Swedish Gothicism grew more sober, but it revived with Romantic nationalism from 1800 onwards. Erik Gustaf Geijer and Esaias Tegnér, in the Geatish Society, gave it new life, this time with the Vikings as heroic figures. Interest in Norse subjects spread elsewhere too. In Denmark, Johannes Ewald, N. F. S. Grundtvig, whose translation of Beowulf into Danish was the first into a modern language, and Adam Gottlob Oehlenschläger turned again to Old Norse themes. Beyond Scandinavia, English writers such as Thomas Gray, John Keats and William Wordsworth, and German ones such as Johann Gottfried Herder and Friedrich Gottlieb Klopstock shared that fascination.

Gothicism also left its mark on architecture. In Scandinavia it peaked in the 1860s and 1870s, though it continued until 1900. Inspired by stave churches, it produced a distinct wooden style with dragon heads, which is why it is often called dragon style, along with false arcades, lathed colonnades, protruding lofts and ridged roofs. Its greatest impact was in Norway.

This Swedish movement should not be confused with neogoticismo, the Spanish current that regards the Christian kingdoms of the Iberian Peninsula as heirs to the Visigothic monarchy, nor with Gothic revival art, the artistic tendency that draws on Gothic architecture.

Trivia

Sweden as Atlantis. Olaus Rudbeck, in his treatise Atland eller Manheim, argued that Sweden was Atlantis, an idea that shows how far Gothicist pride could go in the 17th century.
From dragons to a style name. The wooden architecture of this movement is often called dragon style because of the dragon heads that frequently decorate its buildings.
A pioneering Danish Beowulf. N. F. S. Grundtvig's Danish translation of Beowulf was the first translation of the poem into a modern language.
