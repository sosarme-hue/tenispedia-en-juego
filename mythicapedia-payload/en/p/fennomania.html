Mythology and politics

Finland's nineteenth-century nationalist movement and the power of an epic

Fennomania

Fennomania was a Finnish nationalist movement that sought to raise the Finnish language and culture from peasant vernacular to national status within the Grand Duchy of Finland. Many of its leaders came from Swedish-speaking families, and its clash with the Svecomans shaped public life in the country. Its greatest cultural symbol was the Kalevala, a compilation of oral poetry published in 1835.

Fennomania was a nineteenth-century Finnish nationalist movement that developed within the Grand Duchy of Finland and drew on the fennophile currents of the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries. Its aim was to lift the Finnish language and culture from the status of a peasant vernacular to that of a national language and culture, and to place Finland among the European cultural nations on equal terms. Its opponents, the Svecomans, defended the position of Swedish, and the rivalry between the two camps fuelled the language strife that ran through the public life of the Grand Duchy.

What stands out is that much of the movement's leadership came from Swedish-speaking families. Johan Vilhelm Snellman, who gave political fennomania its most influential formulation in the newspaper Saima from 1844, argued that Swedish-speaking intellectuals such as himself had a duty to adopt the Finnish language and a Finnish national identity. In his view, Finnish was destined to replace Swedish as the language of education and administration. His ideas took official form in the language rescript approved by Emperor Alexander II in 1863. Many Fennomans Finnicized their surnames. The best-known case is Yrjö Sakari Yrjö-Koskinen, born Georg Zacharias Forsman.

From the standpoint of mythology, the movement has a central touchstone: the Kalevala. Elias Lönnrot compiled it from Finnish oral poetry, and it appeared in 1835. The Finnish Literature Society, founded in 1831, had publishing it among its purposes. At the society's annual meeting the following year, the professor of rhetoric J. G. Linsén declared that Finland, in possession of these epic poems, would learn with uplifting self-esteem to understand its future spiritual development, and could say to itself: "I too have a history." The remark captures what an epic narrative could mean to a country looking for a past of its own. The work's impact was enormous, even though many of its enthusiasts could not read it in the original language and had to wait for the Swedish translation of 1841.

The historian Miroslav Hroch distinguished three phases in national movements, and Max Engman applied that scheme to Finland. The first phase, a learned interest in language, folk culture, antiquities and history, coincides with early fennophilia and with the founding of the Finnish Literature Society, which grew out of the Saturday Society, a circle made up mainly of Swedish-speaking intellectuals. The second phase, the formulation of political demands and patriotic agitation, coincides with the appearance of Saima in 1844. The third, the age of mass organisations, begins in the 1860s, when the Finnish Party was founded in 1863.

The word fennomania has a history that differs from its later political sense. According to Jorma Vallinkoski, it appears in private correspondence as early as 1804, and by 1810 it was in public use in Sweden. In that first sense it meant a personal or scholarly interest in the culture, history and language of Finland, with no political charge. The political connotation grew after the mid-nineteenth century, when the word came to be linked with intensified Fennicization. Max Engman objected to the suffix -mania because of its connotations of disease or derangement, and some contemporaries used the term as a label for excessive sectarianism. Yrjö-Koskinen preferred the Finnish term suomikiihko, meaning zeal or passion for Finnishness. Johan Ludvig Runeberg, for his part, spoke of a civilised fennophilia set against the crudeness of the fennoman.

After Snellman, the so-called Young Fennomans, led by Yrjö-Koskinen, dominated the movement's main organisations from the 1860s onwards. They insisted on strict adherence to their programme, which alienated the older generation and, in the early 1880s, a socially radical student wing, the K.P.T. group founded in 1880. In the 1890s the party split into a conservative Old Finnish wing and a liberal Young Finnish wing. Under the Russian integration policy of 1899 to 1917, that rift increasingly turned on loyalty to the emperor as opposed to constitutional resistance.

After independence in 1917, the intellectual legacy of fennomania was carried on in its most far-reaching form by the interwar movement of True Finnishness and the Academic Karelia Society. This in turn met a Swedish-language counter-mobilisation in the form of East Swedishness. Scholars still debate whether the classic distinction between fennophilia and fennomania is useful at all. Juha Manninen prefers to describe both Juslenius and Porthan as early Finnish nationalists, though not separatists. The epic, the language and the schools remain the clearest places to see how a cultural project became a political one.

Trivia

A surname changed. Yrjö Sakari Yrjö-Koskinen was born Georg Zacharias Forsman into a Swedish-speaking family. He took a Finnish form of his surname and was ennobled under the name Yrjö-Koskinen in 1884.
A word with a neutral start. According to Jorma Vallinkoski, the word fennomania appears in private correspondence as early as 1804. It first meant a simple interest in Finland, and only later took on a political charge.
A line about history. In 1836, J. G. Linsén said Finland could tell itself: "I too have a history." He was referring to the impact of the Kalevala, whose Swedish translation appeared in 1841.
