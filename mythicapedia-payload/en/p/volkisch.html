Folk, blood and soil in Germany from the 19th to the 20th century

Völkisch movement

The Völkisch movement was a loose set of German ethno-nationalist currents that, from the late nineteenth century, looked to the traditions of the ancient Germanic peoples as a response to modernity. It blended Romanticism, folklore, reconstructed paganism and racism, and some of its ideas were absorbed into Nazism, although the movement was never unified and cannot be reduced to it.

The word Völkisch comes from the German Volk, meaning 'folk' or 'people'. It is hard to translate, because it carries overtones of the folksy and the populist, but also of nation, tribe and race. The Völkisch movement was a Pan-German ethno-nationalist movement active from the late nineteenth century until the collapse of Nazi Germany in 1945, with remnants in the Federal Republic of Germany afterwards. It was built on the idea of 'blood and soil' and on the metaphor of a single body, the Volkskörper or 'ethnic body', which pictured communities as organisms that grow naturally.

It was never a single doctrine. It was a varied subculture that rose in opposition to the social and cultural changes of modernity. What its theorists shared was the hope of a national rebirth inspired by the traditions of the ancient Germans, traditions that adherents reconstructed on a Romantic basis. That rebirth could take one of two forms: either 'Germanizing' Christianity, a religion of Near Eastern origin that had spread across Europe, or rejecting Christian heritage altogether in favour of a revived pre-Christian Germanic paganism. In a narrow sense, the term refers only to groups that see human beings as essentially shaped by blood or inherited traits. In a broader sense, it covers a range of sensibilities that historians often place within the so-called conservative revolution, the nationalist conservatism that gained prominence during the Weimar Republic (1918–1933).

The movement's roots lie in early Romantic nationalism. The philosopher Johann Gottlieb Fichte, in his Addresses to the German Nation, published from 1808 during the Napoleonic wars, argued that the individual finds meaning only in the spiritual character of the people from which he comes. Richard Wagner, in The Artwork of the Future (1849), imagined an art arising from the needs of the Volk, understood as a community bound by a shared collective desire. The movement combined sentimental patriotic interest in German folklore, local history and a 'back to the land' ideal. It was an anti-urban populism that dreamed of a self-sufficient life in a mystical bond with the earth, and it reacted against the cultural alienation brought by the Industrial Revolution. A similar but non-German community was Monte Verità at Ascona in Switzerland, where anarchism, bohemian art and neopaganism mixed, and where visitors included Hermann Hesse and Carl Jung.

The word itself became politicised. On the left, Volk could mean ordinary working people and the non-privileged classes, and it was even used as a synonym for the proletariat. On the centre and right, it increasingly came to mean 'race'. Inside the movement, the early interest in pre-Christian traditions merged with a growing preoccupation with racial purity. One example is the Germanenorden, a secret society founded in Berlin in 1912, which required its members to prove they had no non-Aryan ancestry and to promise to keep their bloodline pure in marriage. Its members celebrated the summer solstice and read the Eddas and German mystics. The Thule Society grew out of it.

After the First World War, several Völkisch groups were reorganised along propagandistic lines. The Tatkreis, or Circle of Action, formed around the journal Die Tat under Hans Zehrer and gathered far-right figures who later opposed Hitler, among them Kurt von Schleicher and Gregor Strasser, both murdered during the Night of the Long Knives in 1934. Another was the German Völkisch Freedom Party, founded in 1922, which formed an electoral alliance with the Nazis in 1924.

The Thule Society was founded on 17 August 1918 by Rudolf von Sebottendorff under the name Study Group for Germanic Antiquity. It spread antirepublican and antisemitic propaganda and, in January 1919, was instrumental in founding the German Workers' Party, which later became the NSDAP. Yet the popular image of its influence is exaggerated. Hitler was never a member, and Rudolf Hess and Alfred Rosenberg were only visiting guests in its early years. When Hitler became party chairman, he moved to cut the party's ties with secret societies, and the Thule Society declined and was dissolved a few years later. Some symbolic details do connect the two worlds. The swastika, used by Völkisch groups, was adopted in the party's flag. Friedrich Krohn, a Thule member, is credited with the original design, and the idea of placing it in a circle came from Hitler himself.

In Mein Kampf, Hitler proposed a Völkisch state that distinguished between citizens, subjects and foreigners, and that made the racial question central to public life. That distinction between citizens and subjects was reflected in the Nuremberg Laws of 1935. The Nazis defined the German Volk in exclusive terms. Jews, Romani people, Jehovah's Witnesses, homosexuals and other groups labelled 'foreign elements' were persecuted, and many were rounded up and murdered in large numbers in what became known as the Holocaust. Tensions with Völkisch writers arose because the Nazis wanted ideological control over racist nationalism. The historian Victor Klemperer noted that Hitler rejected the folkloric side of the movement while adopting some of its goals.

After 1945, the movement was widely viewed as a proto-fascist phenomenon within German society. Its material has reappeared in post-war occult and modern pagan far-right groups, in some green and alternative circles, and among some British and American modern pagans. Today it is studied as an example of how a myth about the origins of a people can become a tool of exclusion and violence.

Trivia

A word with many meanings. On the left, Volk could mean ordinary working people. On the centre and right, it increasingly meant race. The same word could point to social class or to biological categories, depending on who used it.
The swastika on the flag. The swastika, used by Völkisch groups, was included in the Nazi Party flag. The proposal to tilt it to the left came from Friedrich Krohn, a dentist who collected Völkisch literature.
A myth about the Thule Society. The Thule Society's influence on the Nazis is often exaggerated. Hitler was never a member, and Hess and Rosenberg were only visiting guests in the party's early years.
