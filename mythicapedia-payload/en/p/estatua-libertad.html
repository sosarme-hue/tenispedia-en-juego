Mythology and politics

Roman goddess of liberty

Libertas

Libertas is the Roman goddess of liberty and the personification of the political status that set free citizens apart from slaves. Her cult and image accompanied the Roman Republic and the Empire, and centuries later they inspired modern symbols of freedom. The name has also been used in contemporary politics, from civic mottos to a pan-European party.

Libertas is the Roman goddess of liberty and the personification of libertas, the political condition that distinguished free men from slaves. Her name comes from the Latin word līber, meaning “free,” which goes back to the Proto-Indo-European root *h₁leudʰero-, “belonging to the people.” According to tradition, the Roman Republic was founded at the same time as her cult, and she is linked to the overthrow of the Tarquin kings.

Her iconography is easy to recognise. Libertas usually carries a spear and the pileus, a cap worn by freed slaves. Unusually, she does not wear it on her head but holds it out in her right hand. She was also associated with the vindicta, a rod used in the manumissio vindicta, or “freedom by the rod,” a ceremony in which an enslaved person was formally freed before a magistrate. Her Greek counterpart was Eleutheria, the personification of liberty.

Her temples also have a political history. According to ancient sources, Tiberius Sempronius Gracchus built a temple to her on the Aventine Hill in 238 BC, before the Second Punic War, and census tables were kept in its atrium. The Junii family, to which Marcus Junius Brutus belonged, also worshipped her. A later temple, built on the Palatine Hill in 58–57 BC by Publius Clodius Pulcher, stood on the site of the house of Cicero, who was then in exile. By consecrating the land, Clodius made it legally uninhabitable. When Cicero returned, he successfully argued that the consecration was invalid, recovered the site, and the temple was destroyed.

In 46 BC the Roman Senate voted to build a shrine to Libertas in honour of Julius Caesar, but it was never built. Instead, a small statue of the goddess was placed in the Roman Forum. The episode shows how the language of freedom could serve the powerful. Galba, during his brief reign after the death of Nero, issued coins known as “Freedom of the People,” and the goddess appeared on other coins of the imperial period.

After antiquity, her image endured. She appears on the Great Seal of France, created in 1848, and it was this image that influenced the sculptor Frédéric Auguste Bartholdi when he designed the Statue of Liberty on Liberty Island in New York Harbor. The statue’s Roman-style robe is its most obvious link to the goddess. Libertas also inspired many modern personifications, including Columbia, the symbol of the United States, and Marianne, the symbol of France.

The name also appeared on money. In the United States, Liberty was shown on the obverse of most coinage until well into the twentieth century, and the image is still used on the American Gold Eagle bullion coin. Switzerland depicts her on its 5, 10 and 20 rappen coins.

In modern politics the term appears in several settings. The former Republic of Ragusa, today Dubrovnik, used Libertas as its motto and symbol, and the motto Libertas quæ sera tamen appears on the flag of the Brazilian state of Minas Gerais. Libertas.eu is a pan-European political party that uses the same Latin word and has national branches in several countries, including Spain. Its use of the name reflects the word’s long life in political language rather than any continuation of the ancient myths.

Trivia

A cap in her hand. Libertas holds the pileus in her hand instead of wearing it. It was the cap of freed slaves, so the image recalled the moment of liberation rather than a permanent state.
A house and a temple. The Palatine temple was built on the house of Cicero while he was in exile. After his return he persuaded authorities that its consecration was invalid, and the building was destroyed.
Liberty on coins. In the United States, Liberty appeared on the front of most coins until the twentieth century, and she still appears on the American Gold Eagle bullion coin.
