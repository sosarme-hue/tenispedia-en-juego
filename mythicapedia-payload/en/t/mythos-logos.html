Plot as the soul of tragedy

Mythos (Aristotle)

Mythos is a Greek word meaning 'tale' or 'story', which Aristotle uses in his Poetics to designate the plot of a tragedy, that is, the arrangement of its incidents. For him, plot is the first and most important of the elements of tragedy, ranking above the characters. His view of narrative structure remains a reference point in the study of storytelling.

The Greek word μῦθος (mythos) originally meant 'tale' or 'story'. Over time it also came to refer to the plot of a work. Aristotle gave it this technical sense in his Poetics, a treatise written around 335 BCE that analyses Athenian tragedy. For him, the mythos is the representation of an action, meaning the arrangement of the incidents that make up the story. It should be distinguished from praxis, the actions themselves that the plot represents.

Aristotle listed six elements of tragedy, and plot came first. His reasons for ranking it so high rest on a central idea: tragedy is an imitation not of people but of actions and of a life. For this reason character, or êthos, was placed in a secondary position. To explain the point he turned to painting. Plot is like the outline of a sketch, while character is the colour that fills in the figure.

Within the plot, chapters 13 and 14 of the Poetics combine two questions: how the fortune of the characters changes, and what kind of person is at the centre. According to Elizabeth Belfiore, a tragic plot is a movement between two extremes, good fortune and bad fortune, so there are two possible directions of change. As for characters, Aristotle distinguishes decent people, people outstanding in virtue and justice, evil people, and the intermediate man. Belfiore notes that although six combinations are logically possible, Aristotle lists only four.

According to Aristotle, the best tragedy from the standpoint of the craft is one in which an intermediate character falls from good to bad fortune because of a hamartia, an error. By contrast, he considers the most untragic case to be an evil man who moves from bad to good fortune, since it arouses neither pity nor fear. Chapter 14 turns to combinations of pathos, a dramatic suffering such as a death or a wound, with the knowledge or ignorance of the agent. Working from Aristotle's examples, Belfiore derives four logical possibilities, ranked from worst to best.

It is worth remembering that the Poetics deals with Greek tragedy, whereas modern theories of plot also apply to novels, short stories and films. Belfiore argues that for Aristotle plot is essential, and that psychological and ethical considerations are secondary to the events themselves. Meir Sternberg sums up the Aristotelian model as a complete action with a beginning, a middle and an end, linked by a necessary or probable sequence, so that nothing follows the point where the story stops.

This view has consequences. By demoting character and showing no interest in theme, Aristotle sets aside a focus on inner conflict within a person or on the clash of opposed principles, in Belfiore's reading. Hegel, by contrast, understood tragedy as a conflict between two equally justified positions which, unable to recognise the justice of the other, must negate it; the only resolution would be the death of the hero. Sternberg notes that modernism moved toward open endings and poststructuralism toward indeterminacy.

The idea has also reached the study of narrative. Peter Brook, as cited by Belfiore, speaks of a notion of plot that is only 'quasi-Aristotelian'. Vladimir Propp reversed the philosopher's idea by writing that stories are about characters who act. Yet Belfiore points out that many of his narrative functions carry an ethical colouring, since they are defined in terms of characters with specific moral qualities. Aristotle's mythos therefore remains a useful starting point for thinking about how a story is put together.

Trivia

A name with many uses. Besides its literary sense, Mythos is the name of a beer aimed at the Greek market, a collectible card game based on the Cthulhu Mythos, and a book by Stephen Fry retelling myths of ancient Greece.
Six parts of tragedy. Aristotle listed six elements of tragedy, and plot came first, ahead of character, thought, diction, spectacle and song.
A sketch and its colours. To explain the relation between plot and character, Aristotle used the image of a sketch brought to life by colour. The comparison sums up his view that events weigh more than personalities.
