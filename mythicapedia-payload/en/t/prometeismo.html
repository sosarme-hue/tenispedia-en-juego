Thought, psychology and science

The view that nature serves human needs and can be fixed by innovation

Prometheanism

Prometheanism is an environmentalist term describing a view of the Earth as a resource whose value depends mainly on human needs, with environmental problems solved through human innovation. The name comes from Prometheus, the Titan of Greek myth linked to fire, craft and progress. Unlike other green currents, this outlook puts human interests ahead of those of ecosystems or of individual creatures.

Prometheanism is a term made popular by the political theorist John Dryzek to name a particular environmentalist outlook. In this view, the Earth is above all a resource, and its usefulness is measured mainly by what people need and want. Environmental problems, from this standpoint, are not solved by giving up growth or limiting human activity, but by inventing new technologies and solutions. Dryzek introduced the idea in The Politics of the Earth: Environmental Discourses (1997), where he analyses different ways of talking about the environment and compares them.

The name points back to Greek mythology. According to the tradition, Prometheus was a Titan who sided with human beings and gave them fire, a gift that allowed them to work, cook and create. Hesiod, one of the earliest authors to record the story in Theogony and Works and Days, presents him as a clever defender of mortals against Zeus, who punishes him for it. Aeschylus later returned to the figure in his tragedy Prometheus Bound, where the Titan appears as a sufferer who refuses to submit. The versions differ in their details and in how far the trickery goes, but they agree on one point: the stolen fire is the origin of human crafts.

That figure has had a long life in later culture. Prometheus became a symbol of rebellion, of knowledge and, from the eighteenth century onward, of technical progress. Mary Shelley's novel Frankenstein, published in 1818, carries the subtitle The Modern Prometheus, a sign of how the figure became linked to the ambition to create and reshape the world. Dryzek drew on this imagery to stress that, in this outlook, nature is something human ingenuity can tame and improve.

To measure its reach, it helps to compare it with the other major environmental tradition, usually called Arcadian. The name comes from Arcadia, a region of ancient Greece that pastoral poetry turned into an ideal landscape of shepherds and harmony with nature. Arcadian currents, such as deep ecology, tend to place the main value in ecosystems and in the balance of the whole, and to hold that humans should adjust to natural limits. Prometheanism, by contrast, puts human needs and interests at the centre.

It also differs from eco-feminism, which gives special attention to the situation and rights of each creature and of the most vulnerable groups. Although all three positions share a concern for the environment, their priorities differ. For Prometheanism, an environmental problem is a technical and social challenge that better knowledge and better tools can solve, while for the other currents it requires, at least in part, changing the relationship with nature itself.

In 1992 Martin Lewis published Green Delusions, a book that offered one of the first systematic comparisons between Promethean and Arcadian environmentalism. His critique was aimed mainly at radical currents, which he accused of idealising nature to the point of distrusting progress. Since then the term has been used mostly as an analytical label for positions within the environmental debate, rather than as the name of a movement that calls itself by that word. It is best read as a map of ideas rather than as a closed camp.

In short, Prometheanism inherits from the myth of Prometheus a trust in human ingenuity and in fire as a tool of transformation. The question it raises remains open: whether nature is mainly a resource that technology can manage, or whether there are limits technology cannot cross. That tension, which the myth already contained between gift and punishment, continues to shape today's environmental debate.
