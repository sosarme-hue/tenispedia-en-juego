How myths from Greece and Rome inspired painters, poets and playwrights for centuries

Greek mythology in Western art and literature

Greek and Roman myths have been a constant source of inspiration for Western artists. From the Renaissance to the twentieth century, painters, poets, playwrights and composers reworked these stories to suit their own times. Their influence differed between northern and southern Europe, but it never disappeared.

With the rediscovery of classical antiquity in the Renaissance, the poetry of Ovid became one of the strongest influences on the imagination of poets and artists. His Metamorphoses spread many of the mythological tales that writers and painters would turn to for centuries. From the early Renaissance, artists portrayed subjects from Greek and Roman myth alongside the more familiar Christian themes.

Among the best-known works by Italian painters are Botticelli's The Birth of Venus and Pallas and the Centaur, the Ledas of Leonardo da Vinci and Michelangelo, and Raphael's Galatea. In Italy, Greek myth also reached poetry through Latin and Ovid's work, shaping medieval and Renaissance writers such as Petrarch, Boccaccio and Dante.

In northern Europe, Greek mythology never took the same hold on the visual arts, but its presence in literature was very clear. Classical texts, both Latin and Greek, were translated, so the stories became available to a wider readership. In England, Chaucer, the Elizabethans and John Milton were all influenced by Greek myths, and nearly every major English poet from Shakespeare to Robert Bridges looked to them for inspiration.

In France, Jean Racine revived Greek drama and reworked myths such as those of Phaedra, Andromache, Oedipus and Iphigeneia to new ends. In Germany, Goethe did something similar with classical drama. The Enlightenment, which spread a philosophical revolution across Europe, brought a certain reaction against Greek myth, as writers preferred to stress the scientific and philosophical achievements of Greece and Rome. Even so, the myths remained a rich source for dramatists, including those who wrote the librettos for Handel's Admeto and Semele, Mozart's Idomeneo and Gluck's Iphigénie en Aulide.

By the end of the eighteenth century, Romanticism set off a wave of enthusiasm for everything Greek, Greek mythology included. Britain saw a great period of new translations of Greek tragedy and of Homer, which in turn inspired poets such as Keats, Byron and Shelley. The Hellenism of Alfred Lord Tennyson, poet laureate to Queen Victoria, was so strong that even his tales of the court of King Arthur, so quintessentially English, echo the Homeric epics. The visual arts followed, helped by the purchase of the Parthenon marbles in 1816. Many of the "Greek" paintings of Lord Leighton and Lawrence Alma-Tadema were taken seriously as part of the transmission of the Hellenic ideal.

In nineteenth-century America, writers such as Thomas Bulfinch and Nathaniel Hawthorne believed that myths should give pleasure, and that studying them was essential to understanding English and American literature. Bulfinch wrote that the so-called gods of Olympus no longer had a single worshipper among living people, and that they belonged to literature and taste rather than theology.

In more recent times, classical themes have been reinterpreted by major dramatists such as Jean Anouilh, Jean Cocteau and Jean Giraudoux in France, and Eugene O'Neill in America, and by T. S. Eliot in England. Great novelists, among them the Irish James Joyce and the French André Gide, have also returned to them. Composers such as Richard Strauss and Jacques Offenbach have set Greek mythological themes to music. The gods and heroes of Greece have thus kept speaking to each generation, always in different voices.
