A mythic container that held the world's troubles

Pandora's box

In Greek mythology, Pandora, the first woman, opens a jar that releases toil, sickness and other evils into the world. Only hope remains inside. Passed down through Hesiod, the myth has prompted centuries of debate over whether hope is a blessing or a curse, and it has given English an idiom still in everyday use.

In the Greek myth, Zeus wanted to punish Prometheus, who had stolen fire and given it to humans. In revenge, he had Hephaestus shape the first woman, Pandora, out of clay. She was presented to Epimetheus, Prometheus's brother, who took her as his wife. In Hesiod's Works and Days, composed around 700 BC, Pandora brought with her a sealed container that she was never to open.

Her curiosity, which the gods had given her, proved too strong. When she lifted the lid, harsh toil, grievous sicknesses and many other unnamed evils poured out and spread across the world. She hurried to close it, but only one thing stayed behind: hope, elpis. The meaning of that word is debated. It is usually translated as hope, but it could also mean a deceptive expectation. Hesiod calls Pandora a 'beautiful evil', kalòn kakòn, and her name has been read in several ways, such as 'all-gifted'.

The original vessel was a pithos, a large earthenware jar used to store wine, oil, grain and other provisions. It was also used ritually as a container for burial. Many scholars see a close link between Pandora, who was made of clay, and the jar that dispenses evils. The idea of a 'box' is usually credited to Erasmus of Rotterdam, the sixteenth-century humanist. When he retold the story in Latin in his Adagia (1508), he replaced pithos with pyxis, meaning box. In his version Epimetheus is the one who opens it, and his name is often understood as 'Afterthought'.

Other ancient versions exist. The Iliad mentions two jars standing at the threshold of Zeus, one holding evils and the other blessings. The sixth-century poet Theognis of Megara hints at a myth in which the jar held blessings. A fable by Babrius tells of a foolish man who opens a jar sent by the gods. The blessings escape, and only hope remains, promising to bring back the good things that have gone. This version is number 312 in the Perry Index.

Scholars still disagree about hope. M. L. West reads it as a comfort, something to be grateful for. Mark Griffith argues that it is a blessing withheld, so that human life is bleaker. Friedrich Nietzsche, in Human, All Too Human, wrote that hope is 'the most evil of evils because it prolongs man's torment'. A middle reading holds that the evils are already loose in the world, while the capacity to hope stays inside, ready to face them.

The myth also attracted artists during the Renaissance. Andrea Alciato, in his Emblemata (1534), showed the goddess Hope seated on a jar. A print by Giulio Bonasone shows Epimetheus holding the lid while Roman virtues such as security, harmony and peace fly out, and Hope lingers at the rim. In eighteenth-century France, Alain René Lesage, Philippe Poisson and Pierre Brumoy all wrote plays about the jar. Brumoy's 1743 comedy was subtitled 'curiosity punished'. In 1951 René Magritte painted a street scene under the title Pandora's Box.

Today, 'to open a Pandora's box' means to start something that brings many unforeseen problems. The myth has also inspired songs, including 'Pandora' by Nach, 'Chivo Expiatorio' by El Cuarteto de Nos and 'La caja de Pandora' by Tierra Santa. Few myths leave such a clear picture with so few elements: a woman, a jar and a lid that should never have been lifted.

Trivia

A translation mistake. The Greek pithos was a large jar, but it was rendered as 'box'. The change is usually credited to Erasmus of Rotterdam in his Adagia of 1508.
Two jars at the threshold. The Iliad describes two jars at the threshold of Zeus, one holding evils and the other blessings, an image older than the Pandora story as told by Hesiod.
Shifting blame. Across retellings, the blame moves between Pandora, Epimetheus and Prometheus, depending on the author and the period.
