James George Frazer's study of magic, religion and the sacred king

The Golden Bough

The Golden Bough is a vast comparative study of mythology and religion that James George Frazer published between 1890 and 1915. Its central thesis holds that the oldest religions revolved around the periodic sacrifice of a sacred king, the embodiment of a god who dies and is reborn with the seasons. Although his evolutionary theories are now largely rejected, the book shaped modern literature and art.

The title comes from an episode in Virgil's Aeneid. To enter the underworld, Aeneas and the Sibyl of Cumae carry a golden bough, taken from a sacred grove, to Charon, the ferryman of Hades. The English painter J. M. W. Turner portrayed this scene in an 1834 canvas, and Frazer took the image as the starting point for a much wider inquiry. For Frazer, the golden bough in the poem stood for life that renews itself, and that symbol let him link myths from very different regions.

Frazer's starting point was Lake Nemi, near Rome, known in antiquity as Diana's Mirror. There, according to the tradition that classical authors pass on, a priest of the sanctuary of Diana, known as the King of the Wood, held his office until another man could break a branch from a sacred tree and kill him. Frazer saw in this custom the key to an older belief: that of a sacred king who embodied a god and had to die so that the earth would stay fertile.

From this insight he built a far-reaching thesis. The earliest religions, he argued, were fertility cults centred on a solar god who died and rose again, mystically wed to an earth goddess. The king, as the god's living embodiment, paid with his life for the cycle of the harvests. Frazer found echoes of this pattern in the myths of Adonis, Attis and Osiris, in the figure of Balder in Norse tradition, and in many European farming customs, such as the spirits of the corn and the scapegoat rites.

The book did more than describe these rites. Frazer divided magic into two broad branches. Imitative or homeopathic magic rests on the idea that like produces like: a doll representing an enemy will harm him. Contagious magic holds that things once in contact remain linked even after separation, so that whatever is done to a lock of hair or a nail clipping will affect the person it came from. Both, for Frazer, belonged to a single principle of secret sympathy between things, a way of thinking he attributed to the primitive human mind.

The best-known part of his evolutionary scheme proposed that humanity moved from magic to religion and finally to science. Religion differs from magic, in his view, because it seeks the favour of higher beings, while magic tries to obtain results directly, as if nature obeyed mechanical laws. Frazer believed magic rested on a mistake about cause and effect, a mistake science would correct. This sequence, now considered far too simple, was the frame of his argument.

Later scholars have challenged much of the work. Several anthropologists, Edmund Leach among them, objected that Frazer bent ethnographic evidence to fit his conclusions, and the philosopher Ludwig Wittgenstein wrote that his explanations of rites were cruder than the meaning the rites had for those who performed them. Frazer himself admitted that his associations were speculative and that books of this kind would be superseded. The 1922 abridgement also left out most of his references to Christianity, which had scandalised part of the British public.

Still, the book had an enormous influence on twentieth-century culture. Robert Graves recast the sacrificed king as the poet who suffers for his Muse in The White Goddess. T. S. Eliot acknowledged his debt to Frazer in the notes to The Waste Land, and James Joyce, D. H. Lawrence and Ernest Hemingway were drawn to his view of myth. Sigmund Freud took it into account in Totem and Taboo, and Joseph Campbell kept the idea that myth is a primitive attempt to explain nature. In film, Francis Ford Coppola placed the book in Kurtz's hideout in Apocalypse Now, and his death is staged as a ritual sacrifice.

Today The Golden Bough is read mainly as a document of its time and as a source of literary inspiration. Its material on farming rites and sacred sacrifice remains a reference for those who study such customs, even though its general conclusions no longer hold. For the curious reader, the book opens a door onto a way of thinking about myth that combined learning, imagination and a faith in science that the twentieth century itself came to question.

Trivia

A Turner title. Turner's 1834 painting that gave the book its name actually shows Lake Avernus, not Lake Nemi, as the sources point out.
From twelve to one. The third edition filled twelve volumes between 1906 and 1915, and in 1922 Frazer published a one-volume abridgement.
A Yeats poem. William Butler Yeats refers to Frazer's thesis in his poem "Sailing to Byzantium".
A Doors song. The title and opening lines of The Doors' song "Not to Touch the Earth" were taken from the book's table of contents.
