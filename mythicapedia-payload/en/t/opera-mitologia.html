From Florentine sung drama to the great stages of the world

Opera

Opera is a form of theatre in which music is the central element and the characters express themselves mainly through singing. Born in Italy at the end of the sixteenth century, it spread across Europe and took different shapes in each country. Although its origins lie in an attempt to revive Greek tragedy, its repertoire mixes stories of gods, heroes, legends and human drama.

The word opera comes from the Italian opera, meaning 'work', in the sense of both the labour involved and the result produced. It describes a staged performance in which music is a fundamental component and the dramatic roles are sung by singers. Each production is usually the fruit of a collaboration between a composer and a librettist, and it brings together several arts: acting, scenery, costume and, at times, dance or ballet. Performances take place in an opera house, with an orchestra or a smaller ensemble, typically led since the early nineteenth century by a conductor.

Opera is closely related to musical theatre, such as zarzuela, singspiel or the musical, but the two are considered distinct, above all in form. Originally conceived as an entirely sung work, opera has since embraced genres that include spoken dialogue. In traditional number opera, singers alternate two styles: recitative, which imitates the inflections of speech and drives the plot, and aria, a more structured piece in which a character expresses emotion. Duets, trios and choruses complete the picture. In the nineteenth century, Richard Wagner almost abolished the distinction between aria and recitative.

Its origins lie in Florence around 1597, in the circle known as the Camerata de' Bardi, a group of humanists who wanted to revive Greek tragedy. They believed the choral parts of those tragedies had been sung, perhaps even the entire text, and opera was conceived as a way of restoring that practice. Its first work, Jacopo Peri's Dafne, is lost. The earliest surviving opera is Peri's Euridice, from 1600. The first one still regularly performed is Claudio Monteverdi's L'Orfeo, composed for the court of Mantua in 1607. Its story of the musician who descends to the underworld to rescue Eurydice draws on the myth of Orpheus, a figure the humanists admired because his song could move nature and the dead.

From 1637 onwards, Venice opened seasonal opera houses funded by ticket sales, and the audience was no longer limited to the court. Monteverdi composed his last operas there, Il ritorno d'Ulisse in patria and L'incoronazione di Poppea, and his pupil Francesco Cavalli spread the genre across Italy. These works blended comedy and tragedy, a mix that unsettled some educated tastes and sparked a series of reform movements.

The Baroque period gave rise to opera seria, whose libretti by the poet Metastasio, linked to the Arcadian Academy, set a model built on heroes of antiquity and moral dilemmas. Its structure of dry recitatives alternating with long da capo arias gave great weight to virtuosity. Heroic roles were written for castrati such as Farinelli and Senesino, and singers such as Faustina Bordoni were in high demand. The comic element moved into opera buffa, which partly derived from the commedia dell'arte and from the intermezzi performed between the acts of opera seria.

Opera seria attracted criticism for its love of vocal ornament and its reliance on spectacle. Francesco Algarotti, in his Essay on the Opera (1755), argued that every element should serve the drama, and Christoph Willibald Gluck put these ideas into practice in Orfeo ed Euridice, with simple vocal lines and a richer orchestra. Mozart, in many ways Gluck's successor, combined drama, harmony and counterpoint in comic operas such as The Marriage of Figaro, Don Giovanni and Così fan tutte, with librettos by Lorenzo Da Ponte. In the German sphere, his Singspiele The Abduction from the Seraglio and The Magic Flute marked a milestone. The latter, with its gods, initiations and symbolic figures, shows how opera draws on mythic and ritual imagery.

The nineteenth century brought bel canto, with Rossini, Bellini and Donizetti, and then the dramatic force of Giuseppe Verdi, whose operas became emblems of Italian nationalism. In Germany, Richard Wagner conceived opera as a Gesamtkunstwerk, a total work of art fusing music, poetry and staging. His dramas, such as The Ring of the Nibelung and Parsifal, draw on Germanic mythology and Arthurian legend, and his use of leitmotifs, recurring themes tied to characters and ideas, influenced generations of composers. Wagner built his own theatre in Bayreuth, devoted to his works. For many later composers his legacy proved a heavy burden, while Richard Strauss took his ideas in a very different direction.

In the twentieth century opera explored atonality and serialism, as in the work of Arnold Schoenberg and Alban Berg, and neoclassicism, as in Igor Stravinsky. Recordings and radio brought singers such as Enrico Caruso and Maria Callas to audiences far beyond opera lovers, and today performances are broadcast to cinemas and streamed online. Despite these changes, many of its stories still come from ancient myth and literature. As with any mythic narrative, each era reinterprets them and gives them new meaning.

Trivia

Dafne, the lost work. The first opera in history, Jacopo Peri's Dafne, was staged in Florence and is now completely lost. The oldest surviving score is Peri's Euridice, from 1600.
A myth set to music. Monteverdi's L'Orfeo, from 1607, retells the myth of Orpheus and Eurydice and remains the earliest opera still regularly performed today.
Castrato voices. Heroic roles in opera seria were written for castrati, male singers castrated before puberty to keep a high voice. Farinelli was one of the most famous of the eighteenth century.
