An essay on the absurd, revolt and freedom

The Myth of Sisyphus

Albert Camus published this philosophical essay in 1942. It asks whether life is worth living in a world that offers no final answers, and it uses the punishment of Sisyphus, condemned to repeat a pointless task forever, as its central image. For Camus, the key is to hold on to the tension of the absurd without retreating into false hope.

In 1942 the French writer Albert Camus published The Myth of Sisyphus, an essay built around a plain and uncomfortable question: is life worth living in a world that offers no final answers? Camus believed this was the only philosophical problem that truly mattered, which is why the book begins with suicide as its central concern. As an epigraph he chose a line from Pindar that advises against striving for immortal life and urges us instead to exhaust the limits of what is possible. The title comes from a figure in Greek myth whose punishment serves Camus as an image for nearly everything he investigates.

According to the tradition Camus draws on, Sisyphus was a crafty character who angered the gods with his cleverness. His punishment was to push an enormous boulder up a mountain. Just as he reached the summit, the stone rolled back down to the valley, and he had to descend and begin again, endlessly. Ancient accounts do not agree on every detail. Some present him as a trickster who outwitted Death before being caught, and the versions differ on how and why the sentence was imposed. Camus was less concerned with these variations than with the image itself: an effort that never gets anywhere.

The essay's central idea is the absurd. Camus describes the gap between the human need to find meaning and the silence of a world that does not answer that need. We live leaning on tomorrow, even though tomorrow brings us closer to death, and when routine breaks and the question of meaning surfaces, a strange sense of estrangement follows. For the author, the world is not absurd on its own, and reason is not to blame either. The absurd is born from the collision between the two. If that tension is taken seriously, it cannot be resolved through leaps of faith or through abstract systems, which Camus regarded as forms of what he called philosophical suicide.

Camus reviews thinkers such as Kierkegaard, Shestov, Husserl, Heidegger and Jaspers, and argues that several of them abandon the absurd by taking refuge in God or in overly abstract rational constructions. Against such leaps, he proposes holding on to the contradiction without false hope. This is not a call to resignation, since the absurd demands constant revolt. Whoever recognizes it gains a very concrete freedom: no longer bound to a better future or to a meaning imposed from outside. That person can direct life toward the intensity of experience rather than toward an imagined perfection. What counts, he writes, is not living as well as possible, but living as much as possible.

If the absurd cannot be solved by a doctrine, how should one live? Camus devotes a chapter to figures of the absurd man: the seducer Don Juan, who savours passion precisely because it is brief; the actor, who embodies fleeting lives in exchange for equally fleeting fame; and the conqueror, who gives up any promise of eternity and prefers action to contemplation, aware that no victory is final. These are portraits of intensity rather than models of virtue. Without a divine order to set rules, traditional morality loses its foundation, and the idea that everything is permitted is less a cry of liberation than a bitter recognition.

In the realm of creation, Camus argues that the absurd artist limits himself to describing the experience of the world, without explaining it or offering consolation. He examines Dostoevsky, whose works explore suicide from the standpoint of the absurd, although some of his later novels find a path toward faith, which moves them away from Camus's ideal. An appendix also turns to Franz Kafka, whose description of the absurd Camus finds exquisite, yet in whose work he detects a glimmer of hope that keeps it from being fully absurd.

The final chapter places Sisyphus at the centre as the absurd hero par excellence. Camus imagines him walking back down the mountain, in the pause when the stone has already fallen and the effort is about to begin again. For the author, it is there that Sisyphus becomes conscious of his condition, and that moment is the truly tragic one. It is also the moment of freedom: by recognising the futility of his task and the certainty of his fate, he can accept his situation and let go of hope. That is why the essay closes by insisting that one must imagine Sisyphus happy. Camus also carries the myth into modern life, where the worker who repeats the same task every day lives a fate that is no less absurd.

Trivia

An epigraph from Pindar. The essay opens with a line from the Greek poet Pindar that urges us not to strive for immortal life and to exhaust the limits of what is possible.
A preface written in 1955. Justin O'Brien's English translation, first published in 1955, includes a preface in which Camus states that suicide is not legitimate even for someone who does not believe in God.
Born during the fall of France. Camus began the work in 1940, during the fall of France, when millions of refugees were fleeing the advancing German army.
