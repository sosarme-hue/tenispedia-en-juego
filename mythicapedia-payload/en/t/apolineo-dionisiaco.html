Thought, psychology and science

Two gods of Greek myth turned into a duality for thinking about art and the mind

Apollonian and Dionysian

In Greek myth, Apollo and Dionysus were both sons of Zeus, and they stood for very different poles: light, order and form on one side, wine, ecstasy and excess on the other. The duality known today as the Apollonian and the Dionysian owes much to Friedrich Nietzsche, who applied it to tragedy and the arts. Since then it has helped philosophers, psychologists, anthropologists and scientists think about the tension between reason and impulse.

According to Greek tradition, Apollo and Dionysus were both sons of Zeus, although ancient texts do not agree on their mothers: Apollo is born to Leto and Dionysus to Semele. Apollo is linked to the sun, clarity, music, poetry, order and prophecy, and his sanctuaries, such as the one at Delphi, were places where people sought knowledge of the future. Dionysus presides over wine, dance, pleasure, ecstasy and the forces that escape control. The Greeks did not see them as enemies. Both received worship, and their festivals and sacred places often overlapped.

The duality as it is known today comes largely from Friedrich Nietzsche, who developed it in The Birth of Tragedy out of the Spirit of Music. For him, Apollo stood for form, the perfect beauty of sculpture, sanity, dreams and individual contemplation. Dionysus stood for intoxication, the dissolving of the self and collective exaltation, as in the bacchanals, where participants danced, moved by wine. Nietzsche saw in Attic tragedy a union of both forces: the chorus and music as a Dionysian impulse, and the stage and dialogue as Apollonian order. It is worth stressing that this is Nietzsche's reading, not the way the Greeks themselves understood the relationship between the two gods.

The idea did not appear from nothing. The adjective 'Dionysian' was already in use in German Romantic literature, where it was set against the Apollonian as a contrasting artistic impulse. Poets such as Hölderlin, and scholars such as Schlegel, Schelling and Creuzer, dealt with these ideas. For them, dreams, inspiration, ecstasy and overwhelming emotion were forms of knowledge that reason could not reach, and they had a language of their own, a symbolic one. The art historian Winckelmann had also reflected on the calm of Greek sculpture. Nietzsche therefore took up a current that already existed and gave it a more influential form.

From there the duality moved into psychology and culture. Freud, Jung, Baudelaire, Rimbaud, Thomas Mann and Hermann Hesse are among those who drew on it, explicitly or implicitly. The classicist E. R. Dodds, in The Greeks and the Irrational (1951), read Greek religion through psychology. In his account, the crisis of the Archaic period led to a shift from a shame culture, rooted in Homer, to a guilt culture, with its individualism and anxiety. Dodds argued that prophecy gave meaning to events, while Dionysian rites offered release from guilt through the experience of union with the divine. His approach drew on Nietzsche.

The anthropologist Ruth Benedict used the terms to describe whole cultures. For her, Apollonian societies valued restraint and modesty, while Dionysian ones pursued excess, ostentation and ecstasy. As an example she contrasted the Zuñi, leaning Apollonian, with the Kwakiutl, leaning Dionysian. Benedict understood these patterns as socially learned ways of channelling the individual's impulses and integrating them into the community.

In science, Albert Szent-Györgyi divided researchers into Apollonians, who perfect established lines of inquiry, and Dionysians, who explore the fringes of knowledge, guided by intuition. Camille Paglia later revived the duality in Sexual Personae (1990). For her, the Apollonian is light, structure and reason, while the Dionysian is dark, chthonic and wild. Her reading, very much her own, departs from Nietzsche in several respects and has sparked debate.

In later philosophy the idea reappeared in thinkers such as Martin Heidegger, Michel Foucault and Gilles Deleuze, who read it as an expression of fragmented consciousness or existential instability. Peter Sloterdijk describes it as a dialectic: opposing forces that are not ranked, with neither valued above the other. Perhaps that is why the pair remains useful. As myth, Apollo and Dionysus suggest that form without passion dries up, and passion without form dissolves, and that art, especially theatre, lives from the tension between beauty that orders and suffering that is shown.

Trivia

Two different mothers. Ancient texts do not fully agree on the family ties of these gods. Apollo is the son of Leto, while Dionysus is born to Semele, a mortal woman.
A literary term before a philosophical one. The adjective 'Dionysian' was already used in German Romanticism to discuss art and religion before Nietzsche made it famous.
A classification of scientists. The biochemist Albert Szent-Györgyi proposed dividing researchers into Apollonians and Dionysians, and complained that the grant system favoured the former.
