Plato's image of shadows, truth and education

Allegory of the Cave

In Book VII of the Republic, Plato presents a scene of prisoners chained in a cave who see only shadows on a wall. Beneath the image lie his theory of two worlds, the degrees of knowledge and the training of the philosopher-ruler. Although it is not a myth in the strict sense, it has become one of the most quoted stories in Western philosophy.

The allegory of the cave appears near the start of Book VII of the Republic, when Socrates asks Glaucon to picture a scene. People have been held in a cave since childhood, chained by the neck and legs so that they can only face the wall in front of them. Behind them runs a low wall, and beyond it people carry objects of every kind, while a fire burns higher up. The objects cast shadows on the wall, and the prisoners, who have never seen anything else, take those shadows for reality. Even the echoes of the voices in the cave seem to them to come from the shadows.

From this starting point, Socrates imagines that one prisoner is freed by force. When he turns toward the fire, the glare hurts his eyes, and the shapes he used to see now seem more real than the ones shown to him. If he is dragged up a steep and rough path into the open, the sunlight dazzles him and he takes time to adjust. At first he sees only shadows, then reflections in water, then the things themselves, and at night the stars and the moon, until at last he can look at the sun. Only then is he able to reason about what he sees.

The story does not end there. The freed man would want to go back down to tell his former companions what he has discovered. On his return, however, his eyes, now used to the light, stumble in the darkness, and those still chained conclude that the ascent has harmed him. Socrates adds that, if they could, they would kill anyone who tried to free them. For readers in Athens, the reference to the fate of Socrates, condemned to death, would have been obvious.

Plato then offers the key to his image. The cave stands for the sensible world, the one we perceive through the senses, full of changing appearances. The fire is the light that casts the shadows, and the sun, which is contemplated at the end, stands for the Form of the Good, the foundation of all other Forms. The intelligible world, to which the Forms belong, is the one that can be known by reason rather than by the senses alone. The prisoner's climb corresponds to the soul's journey toward that knowledge.

Plato also divides knowledge into degrees that match the stages of the story. The shadows correspond to imagination, or eikasia, and the objects carried past to belief, or pistis. Both belong to opinion, or doxa, which can be mistaken. Looking at the things outside the cave is associated with thought, or dianoia, which grasps the Forms, and looking at the sun with reason, or noesis, which grasps the Form of the Good. This classification connects with the traditions of Heraclitus, who attended to what changes, and Parmenides, who attended to what is eternal. Plato combines both, though he gives more weight to the Parmenidean path.

The political reading of the text is more debated. Plato gives great importance to the education of rulers, and the allegory shows a process of formation in which the wise person must return to the cave to guide others. Yet the allegory does not fully match his thought. In it the prisoner is dragged up by force, whereas elsewhere Plato rejects violence as a method of teaching and holds that each person should be educated according to his or her capacity. For this reason it is often said that the allegory is an image rather than a programme.

Later interpretations have been very varied. Some have read it as a reflection on human ignorance, others as a critique of the politics of its time, and in the twentieth century Heidegger read it as a meditation on freedom. Francis Bacon took it as a starting point for his "Idols of the Cave", the errors of reasoning caused by personal prejudice. The image has also inspired films and novels, from The Matrix to The Truman Show, which repeat the pattern of someone discovering that what they took for reality is only an appearance. What has lasted is the idea that knowledge demands effort and that the truth can be uncomfortable.

Trivia

A name borrowed by Bacon. Francis Bacon used the phrase "Idols of the Cave" for errors of reasoning that arise from each person's own biases.
Present in film. Films such as The Matrix (1999) and The Truman Show (1998) follow the allegory's pattern of someone discovering that what they took as real is only an appearance.
An allegory, not a myth. No ancient text presents the cave as a mythic narrative. It is a teaching image created by Plato to explain his theory of knowledge.
