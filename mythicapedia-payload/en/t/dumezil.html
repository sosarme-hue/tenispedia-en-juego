Three functions for understanding Indo-European myth and society

Trifunctional hypothesis

The trifunctional hypothesis proposes that Proto-Indo-European peoples organized their view of the world, society and the divine around three functions: sovereignty, war and productivity. Its main proponent was the French comparative mythologist Georges Dumézil, who reached the idea by comparing myths and heroic narratives from several traditions. The hypothesis remains debated, both for its insights and for its critics and for its author's controversial political sympathies.

The trifunctional hypothesis holds that Proto-Indo-European peoples organized their view of the world into three functions: sovereignty, which had two complementary sides; war; and productivity, which covered fertility, prosperity, herding, farming, wealth and crafts. According to this idea, the pattern was not only a way of thinking, but also appeared in social organization, ritual and, above all, myth. Its best-known advocate was the French comparative mythologist Georges Dumézil, although Jean Boissel had already proposed a similar idea in 1929 in his book Flamen-Brahman.

Dumézil reached his conclusion by comparing myths and heroic narratives from several traditions. In the Vedic world of India, the first function is associated with Mitra and Varuna; in Norse mythology, with Odin and Týr; and in the Roman pre-Capitoline triad, with Jupiter. The second function brings together deities linked to physical force, command or victory: Indra in the Vedas, Thor in the North, Ares and Athena in Greece, and Mars in Rome. The third gathers what concerns peace, beauty, harvests, wealth and love: the Ashuin of the Vedas, Freyr, Freyja and Njord in Norse mythology, and Quirinus in Rome. The epic Mahabharata also features heroes who fit the same scheme, such as Yudhishthira in the first function, Arjuna and Bhima in the second, and Nakula and Sahadeva in the third.

The idea has medieval antecedents. Adalberon of Laon proposed a theory of three social orders: those who pray, those who fight and those who work. He had no notion of the concept of 'Indo-European', yet his scheme resembles the trifunctional logic that Dumézil would study centuries later. Dumézil noted that this three-part structure appears above all in myths, though it also shows in narratives and in social organization, so the model works at several levels at once.

One of the most cited examples is a Swedish tapestry. The Norwegian historian Terje Leiren describes it as an illustration of a divine order that mirrors the order of human society. It shows Odin, the supreme god, one-eyed and patron of priests and magicians; Thor, who fights with his thundering hammer; and Freyr, a lesser god linked to fertility and agriculture, who holds wheat. For supporters of the hypothesis, the arrangement of the tapestry sums up the logic of the three functions.

India offers another classic case. The three higher castes are the Brahmins, who are priests; the Kshatriyas, rulers and warriors; and the Vaishyas, cattle breeders and traders. The Shudras form a fourth category. The so-called 'untouchables' fall outside the system, and they are thought to descend largely from the population that lived in the subcontinent before the Aryan invasions, so they do not fit the Indo-European scheme. In Greece, Plato proposed in The Republic a tripartite theory of the soul, with a rational part, a spirited part and an appetitive part, which correspond to the three classes of a just society. Among the ancient Germanic peoples a division into king, nobles and freemen has been suggested, though this reading is more disputed.

One of the most striking aspects of the hypothesis is what Dumézil called the 'war of the functions'. It is a mythic pattern in which a foundational conflict pits the first two functions against the third. The outcome does not eliminate productivity but incorporates it into the victorious group, so the whole becomes complete only when the three functions are joined. The Roman example is the Rape of the Sabine women: the Romans would stand for sovereignty and war, and the Sabines for wealth and fecundity. After the conflict, the intervention of the women unites both peoples. Other scholars read this legend as a reflection of the historical absorption of the Sabines into Rome. In Norse mythology, the conflict between the Æsir and the Vanir has been read in the same key: the Æsir, with Odin, Týr and Thor, would stand for sovereignty and warrior force, and the Vanir, with Njörðr, Freyr and Freyja, for fertility and prosperity. Their war ends with an exchange of hostages and the incorporation of the Vanir into the pantheon. C. Scott Littleton extended the comparison to the Iliad, where the Greek side would match the royal and warrior functions and Troy the wealth and luxury of the third.

The hypothesis has enjoyed wide reception. Its supporters include Bernard Sergent and Iaroslav Lebedynsky, who concludes that 'the basic idea seems proven in a convincing way'. Émile Benveniste, who at first criticized aspects of Dumézil's method, ended up supporting the tripartite model. Beyond Indo-European studies, it was taken up by mythographers and anthropologists such as Mircea Eliade and Claude Lévi-Strauss, and by historians such as Georges Duby, who used it to analyse the medieval estates of those who pray, fight and work. Jacques Le Goff argued that, in medieval studies, the model mattered less for proving Indo-European origins than for explaining how a social ideology worked and whose interests it served.

Criticism has been just as considerable. Arnaldo Momigliano, a former student of Dumézil, challenged the hypothesis sharply and qualified it, and Dumézil himself reworked his theory after the debates it provoked. Other scholars have pointed out that the functions blur together in some examples, that the Indo-Iranian comparisons raise methodological problems, and that similar social divisions appear in societies outside the Indo-European sphere. Some propose modifying the model rather than rejecting it, such as André and Pierre Sauzeau, who argue for a fourth function linked to disorder and transformation.

The debate has also focused on Dumézil himself. Carlo Ginzburg, Momigliano and Bruce Lincoln connected his work with his sympathies for the political right. Dumézil supported the Action Française movement and, under a pseudonym, wrote praise for Benito Mussolini. Guy Stroumsa considers the charge that his scholarship was shaped by these sympathies unfounded, while Stefan Arvidsson, acknowledging these facts, considers Dumézil a scholar professional enough to separate his research from his ideological commitments. Today the hypothesis is still discussed, and some comparativists, such as M. L. West, reconstruct Indo-European myth and poetry while relying less on Dumézil's system.

Trivia

A tapestry as evidence. A Swedish tapestry showing Odin, Thor and Freyr is often cited as an illustration of the tripartite grouping of Norse gods, according to the reading of the Norwegian historian Terje Leiren.
A war that ends in alliance. In the pattern of the 'war of the functions', the conflict does not end by eliminating the third function but by incorporating it into the victorious group, as happens between the Æsir and the Vanir.
Before Dumézil. Jean Boissel proposed a similar idea in 1929 in his book Flamen-Brahman, and Adalberon of Laon had defended a three-part division of society centuries earlier.
