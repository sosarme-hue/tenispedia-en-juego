How features on other worlds get their names

Planetary nomenclature

Planetary nomenclature is a system that gives each surface feature of a planet or natural satellite a unique name, so it can be located, described and discussed. The IAU approves names under strict rules, and mythology supplies many of the themes, especially for Venus, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune and Pluto.

Planetary nomenclature works like a street directory for other worlds. Each surface feature on a planet or natural satellite receives a unique name so that it can be located, described and discussed without confusion. Astronomers have been naming what they see since the telescope arrived, especially on the Moon and Mars. To bring order to the process, the International Astronomical Union (IAU) was founded in 1919 to designate and standardise names for features on bodies in the Solar System.

Approval happens in stages. When the first images of a world arrive, a theme is chosen and an IAU task group names a handful of major features. As higher-resolution maps appear, further names are added at the request of researchers who map or study particular terrains. Anyone can suggest a name, and a task group considers it. Names that pass review go to the IAU Working Group for Planetary System Nomenclature (WGPSN), where they become provisional and may already appear on maps and in papers. They gain official status only when the IAU General Assembly approves them.

The IAU's rules aim for names that are simple, clear and unambiguous. Features shorter than about 100 metres are generally left unnamed unless they are of exceptional scientific interest. Each body should carry as few names as possible, and duplicating a name across bodies is discouraged. Names should be given in their language of origin, with transliteration allowed but no translation. Early Solar System themes should be reused and expanded. Names with modern political, military or religious meaning are excluded, yet the gods and goddesses of ancient religions are considered acceptable. Commemorating people is reserved for figures of lasting international standing who have been dead for at least three years.

Myth is therefore one of the richest sources. Venus is the clearest case: all but three of its features bear women's names, whether goddesses or historical and mythological women. The three exceptions, Alpha Regio, Beta Regio and Maxwell Montes, were named before the convention was adopted, and the last honours James Clerk Maxwell.

The moons of Saturn show how varied the themes can be. Janus and Epimetheus take names from the myth of Castor and Pollux, the twins. Mimas draws on characters and places from the Arthurian legends in Malory's Le Morte d'Arthur, Enceladus on The Arabian Nights in Burton's translation, Tethys on Homer's Odyssey, Dione on Roman mythology and Virgil's Aeneid, Rhea on creation myths, and Hyperion on deities of the Sun and Moon. On Jupiter, Amalthea and Thebe follow the myths associated with their names. The moons of Uranus carry names from Shakespeare and from Pope's The Rape of the Lock. On Neptune, Proteus is limited to water spirits and deities who are neither Greek nor Roman, and Triton to aquatic names with the same exclusion.

The themes for Pluto and its moons, approved in February 2017, look towards the Underworld. For Pluto, the suggested names are gods, goddesses and other beings of the Underworld, its locales, and the heroes who explored it, all drawn from mythology, folklore and literature. Charon is linked to fictional and mythological vessels and travellers, Nix to deities of the night, Hydra to legendary serpents and dragons, Kerberos to dogs from literature, mythology and history, and Styx to river gods. Here myth provides a vocabulary for a region of the Solar System that is still being explored.

Mars offers a contrast. Informal names given to rocks, dunes and hollows, as the source explains, were often playful: ice creams, cartoon characters and 1970s music acts. The two named craters on Deimos, Swift and Voltaire, honour writers who anticipated the existence of Martian moons. Features on Phobos are named after scientists involved in the discovery of the Martian moons and after people and places from Swift's Gulliver's Travels.

Finally, most names include a descriptor term such as regio or montes, though craters carry an implicit one. Many large features have no clear topographic edges, so their coordinates are measured from a centre point chosen arbitrarily. Rings and gaps in the rings are named by the scientists who study them. The result is a system in which ancient stories, literary works and even ice cream flavours help map worlds millions of kilometres away.

Trivia

Venus, almost only women's names. All but three of Venus's features are named after women, whether goddesses or historical and mythological figures. The exceptions were named before the convention was adopted.
Writers who anticipated moons. The craters Swift and Voltaire on Deimos honour two writers who, according to the reference text, predicted the existence of Martian moons.
