From coins and warships to films, video games and corporate logos

Greek Mythology in Popular Culture

Greek myths have travelled far beyond the ancient texts. They appear on coins and stamps, in the names of warships and chemical elements, in films, music, video games and company logos. This page looks at some of the most recognisable ways these stories live on in modern life.

Gods, heroes, Titans and monsters from Greek mythology have been retold for more than two thousand years, in art and literature alike. The Romans adopted them and adapted them to their own religion, and since the Renaissance Western culture has returned to them again and again, alongside the Bible and the plays of Shakespeare. Today they appear in many different forms, from a passing allusion to a mythological figure who becomes the central character of a new story.

One of the earliest uses of these tales outside literature is on coins. In the Greek-speaking East under Roman rule, for instance in Phrygia, Roman authorities allowed local coins to show existing figures such as Zeus. The aim was to enhance the prestige of the region and build a civic identity without openly advertising the spread of Roman culture. In the twenty-first century, the first Greek two-euro coin showed the myth of Zeus and Europa, linking the new Europe to the older culture of Greece. Stamps have also drawn on these figures. Pegasus appears often on airmail stamps, and Greece issued a series in 1906 on the adventures of Heracles.

Military and naval institutions have also reached for these names. The United States Navy has commissioned more than a dozen ships named after figures from Greek myth, among them USS Castor, USS Nereus and USS Prometheus. The British Royal Navy did something similar with vessels such as HMS Phaeton and HMS Arethusa, a tradition the Australian and New Zealand navies continued. The Canadair CP-107 Argus of the Royal Canadian Air Force honours both Argus Panoptes, the hundred-eyed giant, and Odysseus's dog, which in Homer's account was the only one to recognise him on his return home.

Science too has borrowed mythological names, sometimes with a striking link to the story behind them. Tantalum and niobium are always found together in nature, and their names recall King Tantalus and his daughter Niobe. Titanium takes its name from the Titans, who in myth were locked far beneath the earth, an image that echoes how hard the metal is to extract from its ore. In astronomy, the constellation Scorpius recalls the scorpion that, in some versions of the story, attacked Orion, and the asteroid 1108 Demeter, discovered in 1929, is named for the goddess of fertile soil and agriculture. The Apollo space programme took its name from the god of light and the archer's skill, and the Artemis programme, launched in 2017, from the Moon goddess, Apollo's twin sister.

Psychology has left its mark on everyday language as well. The term "Oedipus complex", coined by Sigmund Freud, describes unconscious desires in childhood that he linked to the story of Oedipus. Later Freud proposed an equivalent situation for girls, which is often called the Electra complex, although Freud himself did not use that name. The "Medea complex" is sometimes used to describe parents who harm their children. These labels are modern interpretations of the myths, not concepts from the ancient tradition.

In film, television and video games, myths are reinvented with great freedom. Disney's 1997 Hercules draws on Greek myth but updates the story with jokes and anachronistic dialogue, a approach that the scholar Keith Booker has linked to a gradual loss of historical sense. The series Xena: Warrior Princess is set in a world dominated by Greek gods, though it rewrites the legends in its own way. Jean Cocteau regarded Orpheus as his own myth and returned to it in many projects, including the film Orphée. The 2001 film Moulin Rouge! also draws on the Orpheus story, setting it in 1899 with modern pop music. In video games, the God of War series is set in a world of Greek myth, and Assassin's Creed Odyssey gives Hermes a role within its plot.

Advertising and brands complete the picture. The sportswear company Nike takes its name from the Greek goddess of victory, and several firms have used Pegasus in their logos, among them TriStar Pictures, Reader's Digest and Mobil Oil. The wine Semeli is named after Semele, mother of Dionysus, the god of wine, drawing on that association to lend the product credibility. The Goodyear logo shows a winged foot, that of Hermes, god of travel.

All these uses show that Greek myths are not only a heritage of the past. They work as a shared language that can suggest ideas such as justice, victory, fertility or space exploration, and audiences recognise it immediately even if they have never read Homer or Hesiod.

Trivia

A newspaper name. In 1795, the American colonial revolutionary Thomas Greenleaf named his New York newspaper The Argus, after the mythological watchman with a hundred eyes.
A ship named after a sea nymph. The oceanographer Jacques-Yves Cousteau named his research ship, a former Royal Navy minesweeper, RV Calypso after the sea nymph. The ship later inspired the John Denver song "Calypso".
A computer Trojan. The Trojan Horse, a seemingly harmless gift that let a hostile force inside, gave its name to the malicious programs known as Trojans in computing.
Snakes in medical symbolism. The medical profession is symbolised by a staff with a snake wrapped around it, linked to Asclepius, god of medicine. The Gaia hypothesis, formulated by James Lovelock, takes its name from Gaia, the grandmother of the Greek gods.
