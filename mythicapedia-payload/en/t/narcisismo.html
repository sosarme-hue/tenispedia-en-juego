From the myth of Narcissus to a psychological concept

Narcissism

Narcissism is a self-centred personality trait named after Narcissus, the young man in Greek myth who fell in love with his own reflection in water. Today it is understood as a continuum, running from a healthy level of self-regard to pathological forms such as narcissistic personality disorder. Current psychology distinguishes several expressions of it, and the myth remains the usual starting point for discussing them.

The word narcissism comes from the myth of Narcissus, a young man of great beauty who, in the version Ovid tells in Book III of Metamorphoses, was consumed by his own reflection in a pool. Today the word names both a personality trait and a concept used in psychology, psychoanalysis and popular culture, and it helps to keep those two uses apart.

In Ovid's telling, Narcissus was born out of violence. The river god Cephissus had seized and raped the nymph Liriope, and the child was the result. When the seer Tiresias was asked whether the boy would live a long life, he replied that he would, provided he never came to know himself. Narcissus went on to stir passions in men and women, mortals and gods alike, without taking responsibility for any of them, since he was unable to love or to recognise the other person.

Among those he hurt was the nymph Echo, who had been cursed by Hera to repeat only the last words she heard. One day Narcissus called out to ask whether anyone was nearby. Echo answered him, and when he, unable to see her among the trees, shouted for her to come, she stepped out with her arms open. He turned her away. In another version of the story, one of the people Narcissus scorned complains to the gods, and Nemesis, the goddess of vengeance, punishes his pride.

Captivated by the image the water gave back to him, Narcissus could neither touch it nor look away. He withdrew from every relationship and neglected even his basic needs, until he wasted away and became the flower that bears his name. Echo, meanwhile, retreated into a cave, and all that remained of her was a voice that repeats the final words it hears.

The idea of excessive self-regard is an old one. Greek thought framed it as hubris. The term only entered psychology in the late nineteenth century. In 1889 the psychiatrists Paul Näcke and Havelock Ellis, working independently, used it to describe someone who treats his own body the way a sexual partner's body would ordinarily be treated. In 1911 Otto Rank linked it to vanity and self-admiration. Freud's 1914 essay On Narcissism: An Introduction described it as the turning of libidinal energy toward the self. He proposed a primary narcissism typical of early infancy and a secondary narcissism that appeared in pathological states.

Nowadays narcissism is usually seen as a continuum. Many psychologists regard a moderate degree as normal and even healthy. When it becomes rigid, maladaptive and persistent, it can impair functioning. The DSM-5, published in 2013, describes narcissistic personality disorder as a pattern of traits that affects several areas of life. According to Grapsas and colleagues (2020), people with narcissistic traits tend to believe they are superior and deserve special treatment, work hard to impress others, and can at the same time be combative, contemptuous or intimidating.

Research by Miller and colleagues (2021) identifies two main expressions. Grandiose narcissism, sometimes called thick-skinned, is linked to arrogance, a craving for attention, high self-regard, and risk-taking. Vulnerable narcissism, or thin-skinned narcissism, is associated with fragile self-esteem that depends on other people's approval, distrust of others, and social withdrawal. Both share antagonism, a tendency to feel entitled and to exploit others.

According to these authors, narcissism is not the same as high self-esteem. In the vulnerable type, self-esteem can be high or low depending on what others think and on the comparisons the person makes. Evolutionary psychology has proposed that grandiose narcissism is tied to the pursuit of higher social status, and that such people pay closer attention to the status cues around them.

Clinicians often describe a striking pattern: the confident front of a narcissistic person can conceal an inner emptiness. Some attribute this dynamic to an upbringing marked by parental indifference or by excessive praise. The DSM-5 notes that the disorder typically begins in adolescence or early adulthood. Lifetime prevalence in the general population is estimated at around 1%, and roughly six in ten people diagnosed are men.

Outside the clinic, the word is often used as an insult, a shorthand for vanity, conceit or self-absorption. Christopher Lasch brought it into wide circulation in 1979 with The Culture of Narcissism, and since then it has been applied to a very wide range of behaviour. Read carefully, the myth of Narcissus is less about beauty than about the difficulty of seeing another person as separate from oneself.

Trivia

A flower named after a myth. The narcissus flower takes its name from the young man of the myth, who, in Ovid's account, was transformed into it after wasting away beside the water.
A late nineteenth-century term. Although the myth is ancient, the psychological use of the word dates from 1889, when Paul Näcke and Havelock Ellis used it independently of each other.
Two faces of one trait. According to Miller and colleagues, grandiose and vulnerable narcissism share antagonism but differ in other factors, such as self-esteem and negative emotionality.
