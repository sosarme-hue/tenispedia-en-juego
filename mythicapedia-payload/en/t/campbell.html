American mythologist and scholar of the hero's journey

Joseph Campbell

Joseph Campbell (1904-1987) was a mythologist, writer and professor who spent his life comparing myths and religions from cultures around the world. His idea of the monomyth, or hero's journey, brought him fame well beyond academia. His books went on to shape psychology, literature and film.

Joseph Campbell was born in White Plains, New York, in 1904, into an upper-middle-class Irish Catholic family. As a boy he was captivated by the Native American collections at the American Museum of Natural History in New York. That early curiosity led him to study the mythology of Indigenous peoples and to search for the threads that seemed to connect cultures far apart from one another. That search would define his entire career.

His studies took him from Dartmouth, where he began with biology and mathematics, to Columbia University, where he earned a degree in English literature in 1925 and a master's in medieval literature in 1927. On a ship returning from Europe in 1924, he talked with Jiddu Krishnamurti about Indian philosophy, and the conversation sparked a lasting interest in Hindu thought. According to the sources, the trip also led him to stop practising his Catholic faith.

With a fellowship he went to Paris and Munich, where he studied Old French, Provençal and Sanskrit. There he discovered the work of James Joyce and Thomas Mann, and he became familiar with the writings of Freud and Jung. The Great Depression then interrupted his academic plans. For five years he lived on very little money in Woodstock, New York, reading intensively every day. In 1931 and 1932 he spent a year in California, where he formed a close friendship with the young writer John Steinbeck and the marine biologist Ed Ricketts, who inspired the character of Doc in Cannery Row.

In 1934 he took a post as professor of literature at Sarah Lawrence College, where he taught until his retirement in 1972. He married the dancer Jean Erdman in 1938. During the Second World War he attended a lecture by the Indologist Heinrich Zimmer, who became a good friend and a mentor. After Zimmer's death, Campbell took on the job of editing and publishing his unfinished work. In 1955 and 1956 he travelled to Asia for the first time, spending six months in the south, mostly in India, and six more in the east, mainly in Japan. The experience convinced him that comparative mythology needed to reach a non-academic audience.

Campbell drew on Jung's ideas about archetypes and the unconscious, yet he did not follow Jung into clinical practice. Following Zimmer's example, he preferred to interpret the stories of world mythology directly, treating them as a kind of psychological road map for modern life. This distinction helps explain why his influence was cultural more than therapeutic.

His central idea, the monomyth, appears in The Hero with a Thousand Faces (1949). He borrowed the term from Joyce's Finnegans Wake, and he described a narrative pattern that, in his view, runs through stories from many cultures: the call to adventure, supernatural aid, the meeting with the goddess or atonement with the father, and the return. Campbell argued that myths are not comparisons but metaphors, pointing beyond themselves toward a mystery that cannot be named directly. Some scholars have criticised him for oversimplifying very different cultures.

Campbell also proposed that mythology serves four functions: awakening a sense of wonder before the mystery of existence, explaining the shape of the cosmos, supporting the social order, and guiding the individual through the stages of life. He distinguished between myths of conformity, which reinforce the established order, and the so-called left-hand path, such as the hero's journey, which can demand that individuals go beyond social norms. His books, above all the series The Masks of God, trace how these systems changed alongside the societies that held them.

Campbell's influence reached film and popular culture. The phrase follow your bliss, attributed to him after the broadcast of the interview series The Power of Myth with Bill Moyers, derives according to the sources from the Hindu Upanishads, although Sinclair Lewis's 1922 novel Babbitt may also have played a part. George Lucas acknowledged that Campbell's work influenced his Star Wars saga. Campbell died at his home in Honolulu in 1987, at the age of 83, from complications of esophageal cancer.

Trivia

A reference point for Hollywood. George Lucas acknowledged that Campbell's work influenced his Star Wars saga, which brought his ideas about the hero's journey to a very wide audience.
An inspiring neighbour. Campbell lived next to the marine biologist Ed Ricketts on the Monterey Peninsula. Ricketts was the model for Doc in Steinbeck's Cannery Row, although Campbell never finished the novel he began about him.
No doctorate and no title. After his master's degree he left graduate studies when the faculty did not approve his proposal. Later in life he asked to be addressed as Mr. Campbell rather than Dr. Campbell.
