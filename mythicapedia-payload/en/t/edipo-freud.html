The conflict Freud borrowed from a Greek tragedy

Oedipus complex

The Oedipus complex is a central concept in psychoanalysis that describes childhood feelings in which affection and hostility toward the parents coexist. Sigmund Freud named it after the hero of Sophocles' tragedy Oedipus Rex, whose story he felt reflected a desire that he believed is shared by everyone. The idea has been widely debated ever since.

The Oedipus complex is one of the foundational concepts that Sigmund Freud developed within psychoanalysis. It describes a set of childhood emotions in which love and rivalry toward the parents exist side by side. In Freud's classic account, a boy unconsciously desires closeness with his mother and wants to push his father aside as a competitor. Freud applied the same pattern to girls with the roles reversed. The name comes from a Greek myth that most people know through Sophocles' play Oedipus Rex, which Freud used as his starting point.

The myth itself is well known. In Sophocles' version, Laius, king of Thebes, receives an oracle warning that he will be killed by his own son. To prevent this, he orders the newborn Oedipus to be left on Mount Cithaeron, but a servant hands the child to a shepherd, and he is eventually raised by Polybus and Merope, the rulers of Corinth, who treat him as their own. As a grown man, Oedipus consults the oracle at Delphi, which tells him he will kill his father and marry his mother. Believing his parents to be the rulers of Corinth, he resolves never to return there. At a crossroads he gets into a quarrel with a traveler and kills him, not knowing that the man is Laius, his real father. Later, on reaching Thebes, he marries Jocasta, the widow of Laius. When the truth comes out, Jocasta takes her own life, and Oedipus blinds himself.

Freud knew the play well and read it through a personal lens. In a letter to his friend Wilhelm Fliess, dated 15 October 1897, he interpreted his own childhood feelings of love for his mother and jealousy of his father in light of the tragedy, and suggested that such feelings were common to all children. The term Oedipus complex appears in his published work in 1910. In The Interpretation of Dreams, Freud argues that the myth moves audiences because its fate might be anyone's. He also compares it with Hamlet: in the Greek drama the childhood wish is brought into the open, while in Shakespeare it stays repressed and shows itself only through its effects.

Freud described three forms the conflict can take. In the positive complex, the child competes with the same-sex parent and feels attracted to the opposite-sex one. In the negative complex, the pattern is reversed, with affection for the same-sex parent and rejection of the other. The complete complex combines both. According to the theory, the complex emerges during the phallic stage, roughly between the ages of three and six, and it ends when the child enters the latency period. Freud believed it was revived at puberty and resolved when adult partners were chosen outside the family.

In Totem and Taboo, Freud built a speculative story about the origins of culture, which he called a scientific myth. He imagined a primitive horde ruled by a tyrannical male who kept all the females to himself, and sons who killed him and then felt remorse. In this account, the bans on incest and on killing the totem animal were responses to that crime. Freud used the tale to argue that the Oedipus complex is universal and lies at the root of culture. It should be stressed that this is a theoretical construction with no demonstrated historical basis.

The concept linked two other key ideas. The first was the castration complex: the boy would fear losing his penis as punishment for his desires, and this fear would push him to give up his attachment to his mother. For girls, Freud argued, the absence of a penis would be experienced as a lack, leading them toward their father. The conflict was resolved through identification with the same-sex parent, which gave rise to the superego, the part of the mind that forbids and judges. Freud thought the complex was not simply repressed but dissolved and cancelled.

The concept's legacy has been varied. In 1913 Carl Gustav Jung proposed the Electra complex to describe a girl's attraction to her father, but Freud rejected it, arguing that it did not reflect the differences between the sexes or the role of the phallus. Others reshaped the idea. Otto Rank stressed the mother's role in forming the superego, and Melanie Klein moved the conflict back to the first year of life. Jacques Lacan read the complex in symbolic terms, treating the father as a function representing the law rather than as a particular person.

Criticism has been extensive too. Some researchers have pointed out that the theory says little about relationships between children and same-sex parents, and that its universality has not been established with empirical evidence. Others have argued that when Freud set aside his earlier theory of childhood sexual abuse, he may have downplayed real violence. Today the Oedipus complex is studied more as part of the history of psychoanalysis than as an accepted account of child development, though it remains a key example of how an ancient tragedy could inspire a modern theory of the mind.

Trivia

A name with an etymology. According to the tradition behind the story, the name Oedipus is linked to the phrase 'swollen feet', a reference to the injuries he suffered as a baby when he was abandoned.
A letter from 1897. In October 1897 Freud wrote to Wilhelm Fliess that he had found within himself a constant love for his mother and jealousy of his father, and that he considered this a universal event in early childhood.
A rejected counterpart. In 1913 Carl Gustav Jung proposed the Electra complex as a counterpart for girls. Freud never accepted the idea and considered that it ignored the differences between the sexes.
A myth of his own. In Totem and Taboo, Freud imagined the killing of a tyrannical father by his sons as the origin of culture. He presented it as a scientific myth, a theoretical construction rather than a historical fact.
