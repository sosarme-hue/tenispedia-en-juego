Sword-and-sandal cinema

Peplum

Peplum is a subgenre of mostly Italian adventure films set in classical or biblical antiquity, and sometimes in the Middle Ages. It dominated Italian film production between 1958 and 1965, with muscular heroes fighting tyrants and monsters. Its link to myth is loose, closer to a pretext than a faithful retelling, yet it brought ancient stories to a mass audience.

Peplum, also known as sword-and-sandal, is a film genre that can broadly be understood as historical adventure cinema. Its films, mostly Italian-made, are historical, mythological or biblical epics set in Greco-Roman antiquity, in neighbouring cultures such as the Egyptian, Assyrian or Etruscan, or in the Middle Ages. They tried to rival the big-budget Hollywood spectacles of the day, such as Ben-Hur, Cleopatra, Quo Vadis, The Robe, Spartacus, Samson and Delilah or The Ten Commandments. In several countries they were often shown on television during Holy Week.

The word "peplum" comes from Latin and refers to the peplos, a sleeveless tunic of ancient Greece that appears often in the costumes. French critics introduced the term in the 1960s, and Jacques Siclier made it widely known in May 1962 in Cahiers du Cinéma, in an article titled "L'âge du péplum". Critics first used it in a condescending way, but fans later embraced it, much as they did with "spaghetti Western". Interest in antiquity was not new to cinema, since films such as Cabiria or Intolerance had already tackled it. The genre proper emerges around 1958 with Hercules, starring Steve Reeves, a former Mr. Universe who became one of the most recognisable faces of the period.

The success of Hercules and its 1959 sequel, Hercules Unchained, pushed the Italian film industry to build a real production line. Studios reused the same sets and costumes again and again to make mass-entertainment films. This set patterns that most later productions followed almost literally, until the formula became a string of clichés that flattened the genre, whatever the hero or the story. Many plots featured real historical figures such as Julius Caesar, Cleopatra or Hannibal, but with great liberties. Gladiators and slaves rebelling against tyrants, pirates and swashbucklers were also popular.

The critic Robert Rushing describes peplum, in its most stereotypical form, as stories of muscular heroes, bodybuilders, athletes or wrestlers, who fight fantastic monsters and rescue scantily clad women. The protagonists were usually men of superhuman strength, such as Hercules, Samson, Goliath, Ursus or Maciste, the Italian folk hero. The plots tended to revolve around two women: a good love interest who needed rescuing and an evil queen, a femme fatale who wanted to dominate the hero. Added to this was an ambitious ruler who seized the throne by murdering his rivals and whom only the hero could overthrow. Maria Elena D'Amelio sums up the hero's political aim as "to restore a legitimate sovereign against an evil dictator".

Many films set a civilised people against a barbaric one, often with a village or city burned by invaders. A colourful dancing sequence, meant to stress pagan decadence, was also common, along with animal fights, mass battles and some great catastrophe, whether natural or supposedly a divine punishment. The morality is very simple: the hero is spotless and the villain beyond redemption. The protagonist solves everything with physical strength, while reason and cunning belong to the villains. Sets and costumes had little connection with the periods they depicted; it was not unusual to see Minoan settings with Greek warriors dressed as Roman legionaries.

The genre's roots go back before 1958. Between 1905 and 1926 many silent films on ancient subjects were made, including Cabiria, Quo Vadis and Messalina. In 1914, Cabiria introduced Maciste, a muscular character played by Bartolomeo Pagano who was at first the hero's servile companion, but who proved so popular that Pagano returned to the role in at least two dozen films between 1914 and 1926, set in very different times and places. In the 1950s came major productions such as Quo Vadis (1951), shot in Rome by MGM, or Ulysses, with Kirk Douglas. After the success of Hercules, Maciste returned in a new sound series between 1960 and 1964, followed by films about Ursus, Samson and Goliath. Over time the genre moved away from myth and towards fantasy.

Peplum also had defenders among critics. In the early 1960s a group of French critics, mostly writing for Cahiers du Cinéma, such as Luc Moullet, began to celebrate the genre and some of its directors, among them Mario Bava, Riccardo Freda and Sergio Leone. Some screenwriters, often working in teams, slipped into these formulaic plots ideas drawn from philosophy, psychoanalysis and reflections on destiny. The director Vittorio Cottafavi used the term "neo-mythologism" to describe this free use of ancient mythology.

The genre faded in 1965, replaced by spaghetti westerns and Eurospy films. Today it is remembered mainly for its B-movie look, with clumsy dubbing and modest effects, which has given it a camp appeal that many enjoy. Its legacy lives on in later neo-peplum films, which have returned to antiquity with bigger budgets and a more serious tone.

Trivia

A name taken from a garment. The term comes from the peplos, a sleeveless Greek tunic that often appeared in the costumes of these films. It was used metonymically to name the whole genre.
A character who became popular. Maciste was created as a servile companion in Cabiria (1914), but audiences preferred him to the lead. Bartolomeo Pagano played him in at least two dozen silent films.
An unexpected commercial hit. American distributor Joseph E. Levine bought the US rights to Hercules for $120,000 and made more than five million dollars in profit, which fuelled the genre's growth.
