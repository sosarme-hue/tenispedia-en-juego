The female counterpart of the Oedipus complex

Electra complex

The Electra complex is a concept Carl Gustav Jung proposed around 1912 or 1913 to describe a girl's emotional attraction to her father, alongside a rivalry with her mother. The name comes from the Greek myth of Electra, who helped her brother Orestes avenge their father. Today it has little support in scientific psychology, although it still appears in literary criticism and the arts.

The Electra complex is a concept Carl Gustav Jung put forward around 1912 or 1913, depending on the chronology consulted, as the female counterpart of Sigmund Freud’s Oedipus complex. It describes a girl’s emotional attraction to her father, along with a rivalry with her mother that runs alongside it. Jung set out the idea in his Theory of Psychoanalysis.

The name comes from Greek myth. In the traditional story, Electra was the daughter of Agamemnon, king of Mycenae, and Clytemnestra. Agamemnon was killed either by Aegisthus, Clytemnestra’s lover, or, in other versions, by the queen herself, who was avenging the death of their other daughter, Iphigenia, sacrificed to the gods by her own father. Electra urged her brother Orestes to kill their mother and Aegisthus. Sophocles wrote one of the best-known tragedies about her. In ancient texts her devotion to her father reads as filial loyalty rather than sexual desire. The erotic reading of the character came much later, spreading mainly from the early twentieth century onward.

In Jung’s scheme, psychosexual development passes through five stages: oral, anal, phallic, latency and genital. Each is defined by the part of the body where libido finds its main pleasure. The Electra complex belongs to the phallic stage, roughly ages three to six, just as the Oedipus complex is the parallel experience in boys. Unlike Freud, Jung did not offer a wholly biological or universal account, so the concept should not be treated as a rigid rule.

For Jung, the complex is common in many girls at some point in childhood, and in some cases it lasts longer than usual. Attachment to the father can produce rivalry with the mother, which in theory can be seen from about age three and usually resolves on its own. It is less noticeable than in boys, because the bond between daughter and mother is often very close, which makes the competition harder to spot. When it resolves well, the girl accepts that her mother is the preferred figure and turns her affection toward other relationships. In pathological cases the reverse can happen: the girl rejects her father, feeling disappointed because he had rejected her first.

Classical psychoanalysis points to several differences between the two complexes. The first concerns the erogenous zone. The girl has two sexual organs, the clitoris and the vagina, and once she discovers the vaginal zone she would give up the part linked to the masculine and accept castration and a passive role. The second concerns the love object. The mother is at first the object of desire for both boys and girls, but the girl shifts toward her father, a move explained by an instinct for preserving the species. The third is that the girl leaves the Oedipus complex and enters the Electra complex, and if she had been trying to escape her relationship with her mother, she ends up close to an incestuous bond with her father.

Freud rejected the term. He saw no advantage in it and believed it implied an analogy between the sexes that did not hold. He later argued that it was mistaken, since the Oedipus complex applied strictly to the male child. Jung himself never wrote again about the Electra complex as his theory of archetypes developed. He turned instead to the anima and the animus.

Today the concept is rarely used in mental health care. There is little empirical support, because its predictions do not match what scientists observe in child development, and it is not listed in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders. It shares the criticisms made of the Oedipus complex: the lack of evidence and its poor fit with single-parent or same-sex-parent families. Some feminist authors raise a further objection. Freud’s theories were built largely around men and then extended to women, so the idea of “penis envy” seems to treat women as defective men. The social worker Florence Rush went further, arguing that these theories helped cover up the sexual abuse of children by their parents, especially by their fathers, a claim that is still debated.

The term has also entered culture. Psychoanalytic literary critics, most active in the middle of the twentieth century, searched for this rivalry in fairy tales and older stories. Sylvia Plath, who knew the work of Freud and Jung, used the symbol deliberately in her poem “Daddy” (1962), where a woman merges her dead father with her husband. Hélène Cixous’s play Portrait of Dora (1976) reconsiders the case of Ida Bauer, whom Freud called Dora, and presents her symptoms as a reasonable response to her father’s behavior. More recently, the electronic musician Arca released Electra Rex in 2021, a track that blends the Electra and Oedipus complexes into a nonbinary psychosexual narrative.

In the end, the Electra complex is most useful as an example of how myth has been used to explain the mind. The Greek story is one of revenge and family loyalty, while its psychoanalytic reading is a modern interpretation. In the classical view of psychotherapy, a patient must recognize her attraction and overcome it in order to heal.

Trivia

A chain of revenge. In the myth, Clytemnestra killed Agamemnon partly to avenge Iphigenia, the daughter her husband had sacrificed to the gods. Electra, in turn, urged Orestes to kill their mother.
A symbol in poetry. Sylvia Plath wrote the poem “Daddy” in 1962. In it, a woman merges her dead father with her husband, whom she describes as a “vampire”.
A concept Jung left behind. Jung never returned to the Electra complex in the later part of his work. He preferred to develop his theory of archetypes and the concepts of anima and animus.
