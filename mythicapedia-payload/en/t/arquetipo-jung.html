Universal images in the human psyche

Jungian archetypes

For Carl Gustav Jung, archetypes are inherited, universal patterns that shape images such as the mother, the child, or the trickster. They are predispositions that cannot be seen directly, but can be glimpsed in dreams, stories, art, and myth. The concept tries to explain why similar themes turn up in cultures that never met.

For Carl Gustav Jung, archetypes are universal and very ancient patterns and images that come from the collective unconscious, a layer of the psyche that all people share. He described them as the psychic counterpart of instinct: inherited potentials that cannot be seen directly, but can be sensed when they surface in dreams, stories, works of art, religions, and myths.

It helps to separate two levels, a distinction Jung did not always make clearly. The archetype as such is an empty form, a predisposition that cannot be observed in itself. What we do see are archetypal images, such as the mother, the child, the trickster and the universal flood, which history, culture, and each person's life story fill with concrete content.

The term itself has a history. Jung first spoke of primordial images, a phrase he borrowed from Jacob Burckhardt. In 1917 he called them dominants of the collective unconscious, and in 1919, in the essay Instinct and the Unconscious, he began using the word archetype. Its Greek roots combine arche, meaning beginning or origin, with typos, meaning impression, mould, or model.

His idea grew partly out of a rejection of the tabula rasa, the notion that the mind starts as a blank slate filled only by experience. Jung thought life shapes us, but always on an inherited foundation. By his own account, his work with psychotic patients at the Burghölzli Hospital and his self-analysis made him notice that many dreams followed patterns resembling myths, legends, and fairy tales. He also recalled a childhood dream in which an underground phallic god appeared.

Myths are where these motifs are easiest to see. The trickster, a clever figure who breaks rules and stirs up chaos, appears in stories from very different peoples. The flood recurs in traditions across the world, as do accounts of creation and of the end of the world. Jung did not think cultures copied one another. He held that these themes arise from a common structure of the psyche.

Jung listed archetypal events, such as birth, death, initiation, marriage, or the separation of parents; figures such as the great mother, the father, the child, the wise old man, the hero, or the devil; and motifs such as creation, the deluge, or the apocalypse. Among the images he considered most important were the shadow, the wise old man, the child, the mother and her counterpart, the maiden, along with the anima in men and the animus in women. The anima is the image of the feminine that a man carries within him, with its contrasexual possibilities; the animus is the analogous image of the masculine in women. The shadow gathers the aspects of ourselves we do not acknowledge. The self, meanwhile, expresses the unity of the whole personality.

Jung warned that any exhaustive list would be pointless, because archetypes blend, exchange qualities, and make it hard to say where one ends and another begins. A single archetype can also appear in very different forms. This raises an open question: are we dealing with several archetypes, or with several forms of one?

In popular and New Age usage, the concept has often been reduced to a catalogue of figures such as the hero, the goddess, or the sage. Scholars point out that such figures are better understood as archetypal images that crystallised out of archetypes, which cannot be grasped directly. Anthony Stevens proposed that an archetype is both an innate tendency to form a certain image and a readiness to recognise and respond to the real creature. This, he suggested, could explain why many city dwellers develop phobias of snakes or spiders without ever having encountered them.

For Jung, archetypes seek actualisation within the context of each life, and he used the words evocation and constellation to describe the process. The mother archetype, for instance, is activated in a child when a caregiver closely matches the child's innate expectations. That archetype is built into the personal unconscious as a mother complex. Complexes are functional units of the personal unconscious, just as archetypes are units of the collective one. This process shapes the degree of individuation, the development of each person's unique identity.

Jung also believed that the archetypal plan unfolds across life in a sequence he called the stages of life. Each stage brings its own imperatives: becoming a parent, initiation, courtship, marriage, and preparation for death.

Later thinkers drew parallels with other fields. Charles Darwin spoke of social instincts, Henri Bergson of faculties, and Wolfgang Köhler of isomorphisms. Noam Chomsky described an invariable pattern of language acquisition in children, and Jean Piaget wrote of innate schemata. In ethology, Michael Fordham compared releasing mechanisms in animals with the activation of archetypes in infancy. These comparisons remain debated.

Finally, Jung gave the archetype a dual nature, which he called psychoid: it exists in the psyche and also in the world. He used the image of the light spectrum to explain it and the term unus mundus for the unitary reality underlying phenomena. The physicist Wolfgang Pauli became deeply interested in this idea. How archetypes are inherited, and where in the organism they reside, is still an open question.

Trivia

A childhood dream. Jung recalled one of his earliest dreams as an image of an underground phallic god. He cited it as an early sign of his intuition that the psyche reaches beyond individual experience.
A borrowed term. Before speaking of archetypes, Jung used the phrase primordial images, borrowed from the historian Jacob Burckhardt. The word archetype first appears in 1919, in the essay Instinct and the Unconscious.
An interested physicist. The Nobel-winning physicist Wolfgang Pauli was drawn to the psychoid aspect of the archetype, which links it to the physical world. Jung and Pauli exchanged ideas on the question.
