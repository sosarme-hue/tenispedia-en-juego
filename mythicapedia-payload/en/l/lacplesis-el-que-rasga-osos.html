The hero of Latvia's national epic

Lāčplēsis, the Bear-Slayer

Lāčplēsis is a hero of Latvian tradition, the son of a she-bear, with superhuman strength and bear's ears. His story is told in an epic poem by Andrejs Pumpurs, published in 1888, which is regarded as Latvia's national epic. The hero remains present in national celebrations, plays and a rock opera that became a landmark of the late 1980s.

Lāčplēsis, the Bear-Slayer, is the central figure of one of the best-loved stories in Latvian tradition. According to the legend, he was the son of a she-bear and a human father, and from his mother he inherited enormous strength and a pair of bear's ears. As a child, the tale goes, he fought the bears of the surrounding forests with his bare hands and tore them apart. His name comes from lāči, meaning 'bears', and plēst, meaning 'to tear'.

The best-known version is the epic poem Lāčplēsis, written by the poet and soldier Andrejs Pumpurs between 1872 and 1887 and released in 1888. Pumpurs drew on local legends and folklore motifs. The poem is considered Latvia's national epic, although it came relatively late compared with its neighbours: the Finnish Kalevala appeared in 1835, and the Estonian Kalevipoeg was published between 1857 and 1861.

The poem opens in the sky, at the palace of the god Pērkons, where the Baltic gods gather. The Father of Destiny warns that Christian crusaders are planning to conquer the Baltic lands and enslave the Latvian people, and the gods promise to protect them. Then the goddess Staburadze arrives, having found a young man drowning in the river Daugava. Pērkons decides to save him, because he has chosen him for an important task. The story then goes back a few days. The young man is the eighteen-year-old son of the Lord of Lielvārde, who is out walking with his father when a bear attacks them. The youth seizes the animal by the jaws and tears it apart as if it were a small goat, and this is how he earns his name.

From there the hero goes through a series of adventures. On his way to the castle of Burtnieks he runs into the witch Spīdala, who serves the Devil, and the false holy man Kangars, who in fact is plotting with the crusaders to replace the old gods with Christianity. Spīdala tries to drown him in the whirlpool of Staburags, but Staburadze rescues him and takes him to her crystal palace beneath the water, where he meets and falls in love with Laimdota. He befriends Koknesis, fights the giant Kalapuisis to a draw, and then makes peace with him in order to face the German crusaders, led by the priest Dietrich. He also breaks a curse on a sunken castle, which rises back into the air.

In the later episodes, Laimdota and Koknesis are kidnapped and taken to Germany, and Spīdala persuades Lāčplēsis that they are lovers. After a long journey that includes a fight with monsters with three, six and nine heads on an enchanted island, the hero is reunited with his companions and they return to Latvia together. Kangars discovers the secret of the hero's strength, which lies in his bear's ears, and reveals it to the Germans. At a friendly tournament, a Dark Knight cuts off one ear and then the other. Lāčplēsis manages to hurl the knight into the Daugava, but he falls into the river himself, and at that moment Laimdota dies. The poem ends with a promise that the struggle is not over, and that one day the hero will win and the Latvians will be free again.

It helps to separate what the texts say from later readings. Scholars link Lāčplēsis to other strong men born of animals who appear in European folktales, classified in indexes such as ATU 650A, 'Strong John', and AT 301B, 'Jean de l'Ours' or 'John the Bear'. The poem also has a historical reading. It was written at a time when most Latvians were landless hired labourers, while the land was still largely owned by nobles of German descent who dated back to the crusades of the 13th century. The harsh portrayal of the invaders therefore had a clear resonance with the author's own time.

The hero's story has stayed alive. During the Latvian War of Independence, the fighting against the troops of Bermondt-Avalov was compared with the battle between Lāčplēsis and the Black Knight. The Latvian victory of 11 November 1919 beside the Daugava gave rise to Lāčplēsis Day, which is still held on that date each year. The writer Rainis reinterpreted the story in his play Uguns un nakts (Fire and Night, 1907), in which Lāčplēsis stands for strength and Spīdala for wisdom and change. During the Soviet period, audiences often read the crusaders as a stand-in for the Soviets. In 1986 and 1987, Zigmars Liepiņš and Māra Zālīte created the rock opera Lāčplēsis, which became a symbol of the national awakening of the late 1980s.

Author: Andrejs Pumpurs Date: 1888-

Trivia

A day named after the hero. 11 November is Lāčplēsis Day, commemorating the fighting of 1919 beside the river Daugava. The date is linked to the Latvian victory in that battle.
An Estonian echo. The giant Kalapuisis, the hero's rival, is linked to the hero of the Estonian epic Kalevipoeg, another national epic of the region.
Ears as the key. In the 1986-1987 rock opera, the hero's ears play a central role, and the character still calls on Latvians to summon him more loudly.
