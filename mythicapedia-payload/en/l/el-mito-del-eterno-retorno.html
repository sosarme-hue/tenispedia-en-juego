Archetypes, rituals and the relationship with sacred time according to Mircea Eliade

The Myth of the Eternal Return

The Myth of the Eternal Return is a 1949 work in which Mircea Eliade examines how archaic societies understood time, space and reality through myth and ritual. For traditional people, what is real is what repeats an original pattern, and ritual makes it possible to return again and again to the time of origins. The book remains a reference for thinking about the difference between sacred and historical time.

According to the myth, the world began in a primordial time, the illud tempus, when the sacred broke into reality and gave it form. For archaic societies, what truly existed was what conformed to that original model, while everything else belonged to chaos or the profane. Eliade argues that ritual does not simply recall that beginning: by repeating it, the participant re-enters that sacred time, so that imitating a mythical act amounts to the mythical act itself happening again.

Within this logic, even space carried religious meaning. Temples and settlements were built as copies of a celestial model, and the place where heaven and earth met was understood as the centre of the world. Eliade draws on beliefs from Mesopotamia and Egypt, in which rivers, mountains and cities had their archetype in the sky. Where a territory lacked such a model, it was considered chaotic, and only a rite of consecration could make it real.

The book also gives attention to the periodic renewal of time. The New Year was the moment when the past year was symbolically cancelled and cosmogony was re-enacted. In the Babylonian akitu, order was restored after a symbolic representation of chaos, and the recitation of the Enuma Elish renewed Marduk's victory over Tiamat. For these societies, life in profane time was a sequence of events without lasting value, and ritual was the way to regenerate it.

Eliade also notes that historical suffering needed an explanation in order to be bearable. In India, the doctrine of karma gave misfortune a meaning as part of a debt carried over from earlier lives. In Israel, catastrophes could be read as calls to order from Yahweh, and monotheistic revelation introduced a linear view of time, with a final event that would end history. Ideas of world destruction and renewal, such as the Greek and Roman ekpyrosis or universal conflagration, offered consolation: after the end, a better time would come.

In the final chapter, Eliade compares traditional people, who sought to neutralise history, with modern people, who see themselves as makers of history. According to the author, cyclical conceptions did not disappear entirely in the West: they persisted in popular beliefs, in certain thinkers and in readings of history that sought to repeat patterns. The work thus leaves open a question about the nostalgia for origins and the place of meaning in a time that moves forward without return.

The book is a classic introduction to the history of religions and the anthropology of myth. Its interest lies not only in describing ancient beliefs, but in showing that the way we understand time still shapes how people give meaning to their lives.

Author: Mircea Eliade Date: 1949-

Trivia

An earlier concept. The idea of a cyclical time linked to ritual had already been formulated in 1912 by the French sociologist Émile Durkheim, according to the sources consulted.
The Bagadjimbiri brothers. Among Australian Aboriginal peoples, the Bagadjimbiri brothers founded customs such as the way of urinating or of hunting, and the Karadjeri still imitate them today.
A year as a world. In some North American Indian languages, the word for world was also used to mean year, reflecting the idea of a cosmos that is born and dies each cycle.
