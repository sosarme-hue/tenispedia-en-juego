The Great Bharata epic of ancient India

Mahabharata

The Mahabharata is one of the two major Sanskrit epics of ancient India, alongside the Ramayana. It tells of a war of succession between two branches of the same royal family on the plain of Kurukshetra. Traditionally attributed to the sage Vyasa, it gathers myths, legends and philosophical teachings, including the Bhagavad Gita. Its enormous length and layered history make it one of the central works of Indian culture.

The title is usually rendered as "the great [war] of the Bharatas". Bharata is a patronymic meaning "belonging to Bharata", a legendary king associated with India, so the name does not mean that the story is about that king or about the country itself, but about the descendants of his line. For this reason the title is often followed by a word meaning "war".

Tradition ascribes the work to the sage Vyasa, who also appears as a character in the story and was the grandfather of the two rival families, the Kauravas and the Pandavas. Its central plot is the dispute over the throne of Hastinapura between the cousins Duryodhana, son of the blind king Dhritarashtra, and the five Pandava brothers, sons of Pandu. The conflict begins with a rigged game of dice that forces the Pandavas into twelve years of exile in the forest, followed by a year in disguise. On their return, sharing the kingdom proves impossible, and the quarrel ends in an eighteen-day battle on the field of Kurukshetra, in which heroes on both sides die and Krishna serves as Arjuna's charioteer and adviser.

The work is far more than a war chronicle. It is divided into eighteen books, or parvas, and within them are dozens of secondary stories: the romance of Damayanti, the legend of Shakuntala, the tale of Savitri and Satyavan, or an abridged version of the Ramayana. Many of these tales have lives of their own beyond the poem, such as Kalidasa's play about Shakuntala. The text also reflects on dharma, duty and right conduct, and on the four aims of human life, and it raises the question of when a war can be considered just.

The best-known episode is the Bhagavad Gita, which forms part of the sixth book. It is a dialogue between Arjuna, paralysed by the thought of fighting his own relatives, and Krishna, who explains his reasons regarding duty, action without attachment and devotion. For modern Hindus it is a sacred text in its own right, and many read and comment on it independently of the rest of the epic.

The textual history of the work is very complex. According to tradition, Vyasa first composed a core called Jaya or "victory", of about 8,800 verses, in the form of a dialogue between Dhritarashtra and his secretary Sanjaya, who describes the battle day by day. Vaishampayana expanded it to about 24,000 verses under the name Bharata, and Ugrashrava Sauti later extended it to more than one hundred thousand. Modern scholars agree that the text grew in layers over centuries, drawing on an oral tradition of professional reciters, and that the oldest surviving written versions are much later than the events they describe. Proposed dates for the war range from the second millennium BCE to the 10th century BCE, while Indian tradition places it in 3102 BCE.

The story of its dictation has its own myth. According to one legend, the god Ganesha agreed to write the work down on the condition that Vyasa never pause. To gain time, Vyasa recited difficult verses, and it is said that Ganesha, in his haste, broke one of his tusks and used it as a pen. It is one of the popular explanations for why his image shows a broken tusk. Scholars note that the scene may reflect the real difficulty of writing down a memorised tradition.

The work's influence has been enormous. It is still read, performed and recited in India and Southeast Asia, and it has inspired literature, theatre, dance, painting and film. For Hindus it is also a source of moral and religious teaching, which is why it is sometimes called the fifth Veda.

Author: Vyasa

Trivia

One of the longest works. With more than one hundred thousand verses, the Mahabharata is several times longer than the Iliad and the Odyssey combined.
The fifth Veda. Within Indian tradition it is sometimes called the fifth Veda, although it is not part of the Vedic canon proper.
A poem within the poem. The Bhagavad Gita is a dialogue of about seven hundred verses that forms part of the sixth book of the epic.
