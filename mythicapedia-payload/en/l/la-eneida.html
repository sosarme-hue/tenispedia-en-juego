Rome's founding epic in Virgil's hands

The Aeneid

The Aeneid is a Latin epic that Virgil wrote between 29 and 19 BC. It follows the Trojan Aeneas from the fall of Troy to Italy, where, according to tradition, he became the ancestor of the Romans. Virgil gathered scattered tales about this hero into a single founding myth.

The Aeneid (in Latin, Aeneis) is an epic poem that Virgil wrote between 29 and 19 BC. It has twelve books and about 9,896 lines in dactylic hexameter, although some sixty lines were left unfinished when the poet died. Its hero, Aeneas, is a Trojan who escapes the burning of Troy and reaches Italy, where tradition holds that he became an ancestor of the Romans. Aeneas already appeared in the Iliad, but his later adventures survived only as scattered stories. Virgil wove them into a founding myth that linked Rome to the legends of Troy.

Stories about Aeneas in Italy are much older than Virgil. From the sixth century BC, Greek colonists in southern Italy and Sicily sought to connect their new lands with their mythology, and Troy offered a prestigious past. The Etruscans also linked Aeneas with the peninsula early on. Greek vases from that century show him founding a home in Etruria, and he was said to be honoured at Lavinium, the city credited to him. After the war against Pyrrhus in 280 BC, the Romans adopted these traditions to place their own city within the Greek past. A fragment attributed to Hellanicus of Lesbos even names Aeneas and Odysseus as founders, though that attribution is disputed.

Virgil divided the poem into two halves. Books I to VI follow the model of the Odyssey and recount the voyage to Italy. Books VII to XII, centred on war, recall the Iliad. The poem opens in medias res, with the Trojan fleet already at sea. Scholars often read it as Virgil's attempt to rival Homer, which may explain why he wrote twelve books against the twenty-four songs of each Homeric epic.

The first book introduces Juno, who opposes the Trojans, and her plan to raise a storm through Aeolus. Neptune calms the waters, and Venus, disguised as a huntress, guides Aeneas to Carthage. There the queen Dido welcomes the shipwrecked Trojans. Through a scheme of Venus and Cupid, Dido falls in love with Aeneas. In books II and III, Aeneas tells the queen how Troy fell: the wooden horse, the priest Laocoön killed by serpents, the death of Priam, and the loss of Creusa, whose ghost tells him of a destiny in Hesperia. He then describes his stops across the Mediterranean, the prophecy of the Harpy, and his meeting with Andromache and Helenus.

Book IV tells of Dido's passion and its tragic end. When Aeneas leaves at the command of the gods, the queen takes her own life, cursing his descendants. This scene is read as the origin of the enmity between Carthage and Rome that would lead to the Punic Wars. In book VI, Aeneas descends into the underworld guided by the Sibyl of Cumae and meets his father Anchises, who shows him the heroes of the future Roman line.

The second half begins when King Latinus welcomes the Trojans, following an oracle that foretold a foreign son-in-law for his daughter Lavinia. Turnus, her suitor, declares war. Juno backs the Rutulians and Venus the Trojans, while Jupiter stays out of the fighting. After many deaths on both sides, Aeneas kills Turnus in the final duel.

Virgil worked on the poem at the request of Augustus, who wanted a national epic. He first considered prose but chose verse. According to tradition, he gave public readings, and the mention of Marcellus in book VI made Octavia faint. In 19 BC he travelled to Greece to revise the work, but he fell ill on the return journey and died at Brundisium on 21 September. He is said to have asked that the manuscript be burned, but Augustus refused and ordered its publication with minimal changes.

Today the Aeneid is considered Virgil's masterpiece and one of the great works of Latin literature. Its story gave Rome a Trojan origin and offered a mythic justification for the Julio-Claudian dynasty.

Trivia

Unfinished lines. About sixty lines of the Aeneid were left unfinished when Virgil died, and some do not form a complete hexameter.
Ancient Greek vases. Greek vases from the sixth century BC show Aeneas founding a home in Etruria, centuries before Virgil.
A fainting during a reading. According to tradition, Octavia fainted when she heard the mention of Marcellus during the reading of book VI before Augustus.
