The Book of Invasions of Ireland

Lebor Gabála Érenn

The Lebor Gabála Érenn is a collection of poems and prose narratives in Middle Irish that tells the story of Ireland from the creation of the world to the Middle Ages. It presents the island as a series of takeovers by different peoples, ending with the Gaels. Today scholars regard it mainly as myth, though it shaped Irish ideas of the national past for centuries.

The Lebor Gabála Érenn, whose title can be rendered as "The Book of Ireland's Taking", is known in English as The Book of Invasions. The earliest version was put together in the late 11th century by an anonymous scholar, who gathered poems composed by earlier historian-poets and set them within a prose framework. Many later versions followed, and no two are identical.

According to the narrative, Ireland was taken by six peoples: the people of Cessair, the people of Partholón, the people of Nemed, the Fir Bolg, the Tuatha Dé Danann and the Milesians. The first four are wiped out by plague or flood, or forced to leave. The Tuatha Dé Danann, treated by the tradition as gods, withdraw into the otherworld beneath the earth. The Milesians are the ancestors of the Gaels, who remain on the island. Their line goes back to Fénius Farsaid, one of the seventy-two chieftains said to have built the Tower of Babel, and to his descendant Goídel Glas, credited with shaping the Gaelic language.

The most striking feature of the work is its attempt to fit Irish history into biblical chronology. Its writers wanted a past comparable to those of Rome or Israel, so the Gaels appear as a wandering people, with a forty-year journey of four hundred and forty years and a promised land glimpsed from afar. Its Christian sources include St Augustine's City of God, Orosius's Histories, Eusebius's Chronicle and Isidore of Seville's Etymologies. Even so, pagan elements were never fully removed, such as the goddesses of the Tuatha Dé Danann who take Gaelic husbands.

The myth also had a lasting afterlife in Galicia. In the story, Breogán founds Brigantia and builds a tower from which his son Ith sights Ireland. Nineteenth-century Galician historians linked Brigantia with A Coruña and the tower with the Tower of Hercules, and the Galician anthem Os Pinos refers to the "nation of Breogán". Older sources do not agree with one another: the Historia Brittonum, written in the 9th century, describes three settler groups coming from Hispania.

Today specialists treat the Lebor Gabála chiefly as a myth rather than a history. Its importance lies in giving lasting shape to Ireland's sense of its past, and in showing how pagan oral tradition and Christian learning blended over many centuries.

Date: 1100-

Trivia

Six peoples. In the 11th-century version, the three waves of settlers found in the Historia Brittonum had grown to six.
A late translation. The first complete English translation was made by R. A. Stewart Macalister and appeared between 1937 and 1942, long after the French version of 1884.
A Franciscan scribe. The 1631 version was written by Mícheál Ó Cléirigh, a Franciscan and one of the Four Masters, in the convent at Lisgool.
