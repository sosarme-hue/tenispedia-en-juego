The Chinese novel of gods, spirits and the fall of the Shang

The Investiture of the Gods

The Investiture of the Gods (Fengshen Yanyi) is a Chinese novel from the Ming dynasty that tells the transition from the Shang to the Zhou through a war between immortals, spirits and human heroes. Its title refers to the idea that those who meet certain conditions are made gods, including many who fell on the losing side. It is one of the major works of the shenmo genre of gods and demons.

The novel draws on a long tradition that blends history, folklore, mythology and fantasy. Its setting is the decline of the Shang dynasty, which ruled China from around 1600 to 1046 BC, and the rise of the Zhou. Authorship is traditionally attributed to Xu Zhonglin, although some scholars propose Lu Xixing. The work first appeared in book form between 1567 and 1619, and it mixes Taoism, Chinese folk religion, Buddhism and Confucianism.

The plot centres on Di Xin, the last Shang ruler, who is bewitched by his concubine Daji, in reality a fox spirit. According to the story, the anger of the goddess Nüwa, offended by a poem the king had written, sent three spirits into the palace to bring down the dynasty. Di Xin grows increasingly cruel, punishing loyal ministers with horrific executions and even turning against his own sons. Opposing him is Ji Fa, the future King Wu of Zhou, aided by the sage Jiang Ziya, a Taoist sent on a heavenly mission.

The narrative unfolds as a war between two camps, each with its supernatural allies. The immortals are split into two rival schools, the Chan and the Jie, and that rivalry drives battles, traps and alliances. To organise the conflict, the gods draw up a list of 365 names, the Fengshen bang, which assigns heavenly rank to the heroes and to some of their enemies. That is why the title speaks of investiture: the story is not only about winning a war but about distributing places in heaven.

One of the best-known episodes is the birth of Nezha, son of Li Jing, a child born with magical weapons who, after a quarrel that ends in the death of a son of the Dragon King, cuts open his own belly so that his parents will not be blamed. His story shows the mix of filial piety, rebellion and redemption that runs through the whole book. Other passages, such as the fox who disguises herself as a woman or the flying bear dream, gave rise to expressions still used in China.

The novel's influence has been considerable. China has produced series, films and operas based on it, and the Ne Zha films have been major box-office hits. In Japan the story was reworked very freely, most notably in the manga Hōshin Engi. Critics were initially divided about its literary value, yet it is now regarded as one of the most important texts in Chinese mythological literature.

Author: Xu Zhonglin

Trivia

A thousand-year-old fox. In the story, the spirit posing as Daji is a thousand-year-old fox sent by Nüwa. Foxes are a recurring figure in Chinese folklore.
A list of 365 names. The Fengshen bang records 365 people, immortals, Taoists or demons who are to be raised to divine status.
A title read in Japanese. In Japan the novel is known as Hōshin, the Japanese reading of Fengshen, which gave its name to adaptations such as Battle Houshin.
