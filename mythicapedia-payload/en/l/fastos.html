Ovid's Roman calendar, told in verse

Fasti

The Fasti is a six-book Latin poem in which Ovid walks through the Roman calendar from January to June, explaining the origins of its festivals, month names and customs through myth. The work was left unfinished when Augustus exiled the poet in AD 8. Today it is counted among Ovid's major works and is a valuable source on Roman religion.

The Fasti is a poem written in elegiac couplets, made public around AD 8. Its title refers to the fasti, the Roman calendar lists that marked lawful days, festivals, rituals and anniversaries of important events. Ovid turns that dry list into a series of stories. Speaking in the first person as a vates, or poet-prophet, he meets Roman deities who explain where each observance came from, often offering more than one explanation.

The poem belongs to the tradition of didactic verse and has two clear models. One is Callimachus' Aetia, which explained the origins of rites, legends and names. The other is the fourth book of Propertius, a poet who presented himself as a Roman Callimachus and also dealt with Roman customs. Ovid added his own material: real calendars, such as the Fasti Praenestini compiled by the grammarian Verrius Flaccus, and a taste for digression that mixes myth, history and astronomy.

The plan called for twelve books, one for each month, but only the first six survive, covering January to June. Ovid tells us in the Tristia that his work was interrupted by his exile to Tomis. Scholars think he revised the text while in exile. In book one he calls Livia "Iulia Augusta", a title she did not receive until AD 14, and there is a reference to Germanicus's triumph of AD 17. Since the poem is dedicated to Germanicus, some have suggested Ovid hoped it would win him back Augustus's favour, though this remains a hypothesis.

Each book opens with an explanation of the month's name, often delivered by the god or goddess connected with it. In January, Janus answers the poet's questions about his nature and cult. February takes its name from the februa, objects used for purification. In March, Mars recounts the rape of the Sabine women and how Numa captured Jupiter. Between these frames appear Romulus and Remus, Lucretia, Ariadne, Anna Perenna, Cybele and Ceres, among others.

The poem's reputation has risen and fallen. It was widely read from the fifteenth to the eighteenth century and inspired many mythological paintings. In the twentieth century, anthropologists and scholars of Roman religion found it unreliable as a source, but since the late 1980s it has been reassessed, and it is now also studied as a politically charged work.

Author: Ovid Date: 0008-

Trivia

A calendar in verse. The Fasti records festivals that were celebrated differently in Ovid's time than in the official lists. For this reason the poem helps scholars compare the calendar with the rites actually practised.
An interrupted poem. Only six of the planned twelve books survive. No ancient author quotes a fragment from the six missing ones.
