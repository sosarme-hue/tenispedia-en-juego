A Latin handbook of Greek myth

Fabulae (Genealogiae)

Known under the titles Fabulae or Genealogiae, this Latin handbook of myth reached print in 1535. It was traditionally attributed to Gaius Julius Hyginus, though most modern scholars doubt that he wrote it. It remains one of the most comprehensive surviving sources on Greek myth from antiquity.

Known as both Fabulae and Genealogiae, this Latin handbook of myth reached print in 1535, when the humanist Jakob Micyllus published it under the title Fabulae. He credited the work to a certain Gaius Julius Hyginus, a freedman of Augustus. Today most specialists think the author was a different Hyginus, perhaps active in the latter half of the 2nd century AD, as R. Scott Smith has argued. Some scholars still defend the traditional attribution, so the question is not fully settled.

The text contains 277 sections, according to the numbering used by modern editors, and it focuses almost entirely on Greek mythology, even though it was written in Latin. The first 220 sections tell stories of gods and heroes, usually in a plain, direct style. The remaining sections are lists of names and myths. The work opens with an unusual theogony that places a figure called Mist (Caligo) before even Chaos, a being unattested elsewhere. Hesiod's Theogony treats Chaos as the first being. This opening may have been attached to the work at a later date.

The survival of the text is a matter of chance. The only known manuscript came from the abbey of Freising, and Micyllus copied it carelessly. During printing, the manuscript was taken apart, and only two small fragments remain, found as reinforcement in bookbindings. A Greek extract attributed to the grammarian Dositheus, dated to AD 207, differs from the Latin version in important ways, which suggests that the work circulated in several forms.

Critics have often judged the handbook harshly for its elementary style. H. J. Rose described its author as an ignorant, half-educated youth. Many readers, however, value it for preserving summaries of Greek tragedies that are otherwise lost. Like the Library of Apollodorus, it gives a broad picture of Greek myth as an educated Roman might have known it, and it remains a useful guide for anyone studying the myths.

Author: Gaius Julius Hyginus

Trivia

Recycled fragments. Only two small fragments of the original manuscript survive. They turned up as reinforcement in book bindings, a common fate for old manuscripts.
Mist before Chaos. The work's theogony begins with Caligo, Mist, placed before even Chaos. In Hesiod's Theogony, by contrast, Chaos is the first being.
A misread title. A translation error makes Melanippe the daughter of «Desmontes», because an adjective in the title of a Euripides tragedy was read as a patronymic.
