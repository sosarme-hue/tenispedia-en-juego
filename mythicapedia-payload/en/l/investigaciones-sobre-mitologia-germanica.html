Viktor Rydberg's study of the myths of the North

Investigations into Germanic Mythology

Undersökningar i germanisk mythologi is a two-volume work by the Swedish writer Viktor Rydberg, published in 1886 and 1889. It argues that many Germanic myths, especially those of Old Norse tradition, reach back to a shared heritage older than the division of the Indo-European peoples. Its bold, systematic method earned both admiration and sharp criticism from specialists.

Viktor Rydberg was a Swedish novelist, journalist and university lecturer who, in the late nineteenth century, turned his attention to the history of religion. His Undersökningar i germanisk mythologi appeared in two parts, the first in 1886 and the second in 1889. The critic Henrik Schück considered him the last, and poetically the most gifted, member of the mythological school founded by Jacob Grimm, which also included Adalbert Kuhn. Schück saw Rydberg's approach as strongly synthetic, searching for a general pattern behind scattered stories.

Rydberg's central claim was that many Germanic myths, and with them the Old Norse tradition, were not late inventions. In his view, some stories went back to a time before the Indo-European peoples had separated, when they had already shaped a common picture of the world in myth. Jan de Vries later noted that this idea, once a minority position, had gradually become more widely accepted.

The work has also been widely debated. Some scholars find it ingenious, while others consider it too speculative. Critics pointed to risky etymologies, the identification of mythical figures on thin evidence, and the mixing of heroic legend with myth. The most contested element was his attempt to arrange myths into a chronology. Many modern scholars distrust any systematic ordering of the material, but Rydberg believed that most Germanic myths could be fitted into a coherent sequence.

Among his best-known ideas is that of the World Mill, a vast mechanism that turns the heavens and which, he argued, belonged to Norse mythic cosmology. Much later, this theory inspired the controversial book Hamlet's Mill (1969) by Giorgio de Santillana and Hertha von Dechend. The literary researcher Brian Johnston has also suggested that Ibsen's The Master Builder (1892) draws on both Norse mythology and Zoroastrianism, and that Rydberg's book may have been an important source for the playwright.

Reception was uneven. In Germany, where the school of nature-mythology held sway, many philologists regarded him as a heretic. The lack of a major-language translation did not help: a German version planned in 1889 was cut short when its translator died in a skiing accident, and a French project from Lund in 1891 was never completed. The first English review, in 1894, praised his brilliance while noting that he sometimes stumbled in his effort to reduce chaos to order.

Trivia

An unfinished translation. A German translation was being prepared in 1889, but its translator, Phillip B. Schweitzer, died in a skiing accident shortly afterwards.
A debate about Harbard. Rydberg was one of the early writers who believed that the ferryman Harbard in the poem Hárbardsljóð was Loki rather than Odin, a view scholars abandoned by the late nineteenth century.
