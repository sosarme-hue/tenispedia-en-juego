Robert Graves's mythography

The Greek Myths

The Greek Myths is a mythography by the English poet and writer Robert Graves, first published in 1955, which gathers the scattered stories of classical mythology into continuous narratives. Each myth is followed by the author's own interpretation, strongly shaped by his belief in a prehistoric matriarchal religion. The book is admired for its prose and heavily criticised by classical scholars.

With this book, Robert Graves set himself an ambitious and unusual task: to weave together all the scattered elements of each myth into one harmonious narrative, bring in little-known variants that might clarify its meaning, and answer, in anthropological or historical terms, the questions that arise along the way. The result is a mythography, a compendium of Greek mythology, which appeared in two Penguin volumes in 1955 and was revised by the author in 1960.

Each retelling is written in the voice of a narrator working under the Antonines, such as Plutarch or Pausanias, and is supported by citations of classical sources. After each story, Graves offers his own reading of its origin and meaning. That reading rests on his conviction that a prehistoric religion centred on a goddess once existed, an idea he developed most fully in The White Goddess.

In his scheme, Bronze Age Greece moved from a matriarchal society under the Pelasgians to a patriarchal one, pressed by Greek-speaking peoples. Local kings reigned by marrying the hereditary queen, who represented the Triple Goddess, and were ritually killed by their successors after a set period. According to Graves, myths preserve traces of this process, sometimes as distorted records of real struggles between kings and moon-priestesses.

The book also includes a Pelasgian creation myth that Graves reconstructed imaginatively. In it, the goddess Eurynome rises naked from Chaos, parts sea from sky and generates the serpent Ophion. It sits alongside the Homeric, Orphic and Olympian creation myths, together with two creation accounts of a philosophical kind. Ancient authors disagree on genealogies and details, and Graves arranges these versions into a single unified story.

The reception has been divided. Many readers have praised the literary quality of his retellings, and poets such as Ted Hughes found his system congenial. Most specialists, however, reject his hypotheses and etymologies, and the classicist H. J. Rose pointed to omissions and clear errors, such as ascribing to Sophocles the plot of his Ajax. Robin Hard summed up the split: for some the notes are a major contribution, for others a farrago of eccentric nonsense.

Today the book is read chiefly as a creative work that reflects its author's vision. Its value lies both in the way it tells the myths and in the debate it has sparked about how they should be interpreted.

Trivia

Two volumes at first. The first edition of 1955 appeared in two Penguin volumes. It was later also published in a single volume.
A horse with twins. Graves conjectured that an ancient image of the Horse-Goddess, later misread by Greeks, gave rise to the myth of the Trojan Horse.
A name with meaning. Eurynome, Graves's creator goddess, means roughly "wide wandering", according to the author's own reading.
