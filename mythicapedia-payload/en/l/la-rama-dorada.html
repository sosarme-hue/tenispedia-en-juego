A study in magic and religion that shaped twentieth-century thought

The Golden Bough

The Golden Bough is a vast comparative study of myth and religion by the Scottish anthropologist James George Frazer. First published in 1890 and expanded over successive editions to twelve volumes, it argues that many ancient religions centred on the periodic sacrifice of a sacred king tied to the cycles of nature. Although academic anthropology has abandoned its central theories, its impact on modernist literature and art has been considerable.

The title comes from a episode in Virgil's Aeneid. To enter the underworld, Aeneas and the Sibyl of Cumae offer the ferryman Charon a golden bough taken from a sacred grove. The English painter J. M. W. Turner depicted this scene in his 1834 painting of the same name, and Frazer borrowed the title from it, though he mistakenly believed the painting showed Lake Nemi rather than Lake Avernus.

Frazer's starting point was in fact Nemi, a lake in the Alban Hills that the Romans called "Diana's Mirror". In antiquity, a priest of Diana known as the King of the Wood, Rex Nemorensis, held the office for as long as he could keep a branch from a sacred tree; his successor was the one who killed him in ritual combat. From this figure Frazer built a general hypothesis: the king embodied a dying and reviving god, a solar deity who underwent a mystical marriage with an earth goddess, dying at harvest and returning in spring. According to Frazer, this story of death and rebirth lay at the heart of almost all the world's mythologies.

To support that claim, he surveyed cults, legends and customs from Egypt, Mesopotamia, Greece, Rome, northern Europe and many other regions. He gathered figures such as Adonis, Attis and Osiris, and devoted whole volumes to subjects such as taboo, the scapegoat and the spirit of the corn. The work first appeared in two volumes in 1890, grew to twelve in the third edition (1906–1915), and in 1922 a single-volume abridgment appeared, the version most readers have known.

Frazer also proposed an evolutionary sequence: humanity, he argued, had moved from magic to religion and finally to science. He distinguished two kinds of sympathetic magic. Homeopathic or imitative magic works on the principle that like produces like; contagious magic holds that things once in contact remain linked at a distance. Religion, by contrast, seeks the favour of higher beings, whereas magic tries to force natural results directly. For Frazer, this was a mistake about the laws of cause and effect.

Later scholars have been sharply critical of his method. Anthropologists objected that he selected evidence to fit his theories and treated the peoples he studied as lower rungs on a ladder of progress. Edmund Leach went so far as to say that when the evidence did not fit, Frazer simply changed it. Ludwig Wittgenstein, in his posthumously published remarks, wrote that Frazer was more savage than his own "savages", since his explanations of their rites were cruder than the meaning of the rites themselves. Frazer himself acknowledged that his conclusions were speculative and that his parallels rested largely on resemblance.

Still, the book reached far beyond the academy. Modernist poets and novelists found in it a repertoire of images of death and renewal. T. S. Eliot acknowledged his debt to Frazer in the notes to The Waste Land; W. B. Yeats alludes to it in "Sailing to Byzantium"; Joyce, Hemingway, Lawrence and William Carlos Williams also engaged with it. Robert Graves drew on the figure of the sacrificed king to describe the poet who suffers for his Muse in The White Goddess. Film took notice too: in Apocalypse Now, Coppola shows Kurtz with the book in his lair, and his death is staged as a ritual sacrifice.

Today the book is read chiefly as a document in the history of ideas, and as a classic that shows how part of the late nineteenth- and early twentieth-century European intelligentsia imagined the religious past. Frazer offered a synthesis of material in a breadth few anthropologists have matched, even though his conclusions are no longer considered sustainable.

Trivia

A work that grew over time. The first edition of 1890 had two volumes; the third, published between 1906 and 1915, reached twelve, and in 1922 Frazer issued a single-volume abridgment.
A painting misidentified. Frazer believed Turner's painting showed Lake Nemi, but it actually depicts Lake Avernus.
From book to song. The Doors took the title and opening lines of their song "Not to Touch the Earth" from the book's table of contents.
