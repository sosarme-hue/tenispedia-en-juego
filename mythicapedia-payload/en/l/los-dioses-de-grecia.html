Walter F. Otto and the spiritual meaning of Greek religion

The Homeric Gods

In 1929 the philologist Walter F. Otto published Die Götter Griechenlands, a book that quickly became a reference on Greek religion. Its central claim is that Homer's gods appear within the natural world rather than through miracles. Almost a century later, it is still read and debated.

In 1929 the philologist Walter F. Otto published in Bonn Die Götter Griechenlands, a book that soon became a point of reference for the study of Greek religion. Otto (1874-1958), a professor of classical philology at the University of Frankfurt, starts from a simple premise: Greek gods should not be judged by the standards of Asian religions, which centre on miracles, but understood on their own terms.

For Otto, the difference lies in how the divine is experienced. In the Old Testament, Yahweh acts through wonders. In the Iliad and the Odyssey, by contrast, the gods almost never perform miracles. They are present in everyday experiences: a clever thought, the enthusiasm that drives a person to act, or the courage that ignites a warrior. The divine shows itself in the natural world as it is.

Otto separates the older beings, such as the Titans, the Erinyes and the Gorgons, which he sees as remnants of an earlier and darker religion, from the Homeric Olympians, who have human form and a luminous quality. Hesiod keeps more features of that older world, and the comparison helps Otto highlight what was new in Homer. The older deities hold power through magic, while the Olympians hold it because they are bound to the being of nature.

Otto gives Zeus no separate chapter, since all divinity converges in him. Athena stands for the immediate present and for clarity of action, bringing good sense, cunning and boldness to men. Apollo and Artemis, twins, express the distance between gods and humans and, with it, the limits of human life. Aphrodite embodies rapture, spring and intense longing, while Hermes unites luck, the night and travel.

Otto argues that Homeric religion is a myth of the world rather than a myth of the soul: the gods give meaning to those who act in the world. Human freedom therefore remains delicate, since there is no clear boundary between human and divine action. Moira, or fate, is an impersonal order that limits life. The gods affirm life and may intervene against what can be avoided, but they cannot overcome death.

The reception was sharply divided. Martin P. Nilsson found the mystical approach delusional, and H. J. Rose criticised its errors and rhetoric. Others, such as Walter Burkert and Edith Hamilton, recognised its force. Its influence can be seen in Károly Kerényi and in the structuralist study of ancient polytheism.

Author: Walter Friedrich Otto

Trivia

A title with a Schiller echo. The German title borrows from Friedrich Schiller's poem Die Götter Griechenlandes, published in 1788.
A heavily debated work. Martin P. Nilsson, who favoured an approach centred on cult, criticised it harshly in his 1929 review.
Translated into English in 1956. Moses Hadas translated the book into English, and the translation was reviewed in 1956 by the British scholar H. J. Rose.
