The oldest surviving epic in the world

The Epic of Gilgamesh

The Epic of Gilgamesh is a poem from ancient Mesopotamia that tells the story of a king of Uruk, two-thirds god and one-third man, and his friendship with Enkidu. Its central theme is the search for immortality after the death of a friend, and it ends with the acceptance of mortality. It is the oldest surviving example of narrative literature.

The Epic of Gilgamesh is a tale from ancient Mesopotamia that survives in several versions, the most complete in Akkadian, written on twelve clay tablets. Its roots go much further back: the earliest material consists of independent Sumerian poems, composed around 2500 to 2000 BCE, about a king of Uruk and his deeds. Over time these stories were combined into one long poem, to which episodes were added and which was reworked across centuries.

According to the myth, Gilgamesh rules Uruk with excessive force. He is two-thirds god and one-third human, and he abuses his subjects so badly that the people appeal to the gods. The divine answer is to create Enkidu, a wild and nearly invincible man who lives in the steppe among the animals. Enkidu wrecks a hunter's traps, and the hunter turns to the sun god Shamash. A plan is made to tame him through a woman, Shamhat, who civilizes him. When Enkidu reaches Uruk, he challenges Gilgamesh to a contest. The king wins, yet the two end up as inseparable friends.

Together they set out on legendary adventures. They travel far to the Cedar Forest, where they kill its guardian, Humbaba, and cut down the sacred trees. Later, the goddess Ishtar, whose advances Gilgamesh had rejected, sends the Bull of Heaven, which they also defeat. The gods, angered by these deeds, decide that Enkidu must die. He falls ill and dies, and this loss marks the turning point of the poem.

The second part deals with grief. Devastated, Gilgamesh undertakes a perilous journey to find the secret of eternal life. He reaches Utnapishtim and his wife, the only humans to survive the flood sent by the gods. The flood account, which is closely related to the older story of Atrahasis, forms part of the poem. Utnapishtim explains that eternal life is not within mortal reach, since the gods reserved death for humankind. Gilgamesh eventually obtains a plant that restores youth, but a serpent steals it while he sleeps. He returns to Uruk empty-handed.

Scholars often point to the emotional core of the poem: the grief over the loss of a friend and the awareness of human mortality set against divine immortality. For this reason some consider it one of the first literary works to reflect explicitly on death. The Standard Babylonian version, attributed to the scholar Sîn-lēqi-unninni, reorganizes the material and ends by presenting the hero as a sage who has glimpsed the mysteries of the deep.

The text reached modern times through the library of the Assyrian king Ashurbanipal at Nineveh, where some fifteen thousand fragments were found in the early 1850s. George Smith studied them at the British Museum and, in 1872, presented translated fragments that drew wide public attention. Since then the poem has been edited and translated many times, and later discoveries have filled gaps in the text.

Its influence reaches well beyond Mesopotamia. Many scholars see Gilgamesh as a forerunner of later heroes of epic tradition, such as Heracles, and link some of its motifs to the biblical flood story. Today it remains one of the essential works for understanding how ancient cultures thought about friendship, power, death and the meaning of life.

Date: -2100

Trivia

A title that begins with the deep. The standard version opens with the words «He who saw the deep». Specialists understand the phrase as referring to special knowledge that Gilgamesh gained during his meeting with Utnapishtim.
A poem without marked lines. The ancient text does not separate its lines. Its length is estimated from its rhythm, and it may have contained around 3,500 verses.
A name from another era. When the character was rediscovered in the nineteenth century, he was first called «Izdubar», because the cuneiform signs of his name could not yet be read accurately.
