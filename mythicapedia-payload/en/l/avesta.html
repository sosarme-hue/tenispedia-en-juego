The sacred corpus of Zoroastrianism

Avesta

The Avesta is the collection of sacred texts of Zoroastrianism, composed in Avestan, the largest body of literature from the Old Iranian period. Its texts began as oral compositions over several centuries, and Zoroastrian tradition links them to Zoroaster and Ahura Mazda. Only part of what was once a much larger compilation survives today.

The Avesta is the body of religious texts of Zoroastrianism. All of its texts are composed in the Avestan language and written in the Avestan alphabet, which was devised long after the texts first circulated. It is the largest literature of the Old Iranian period and contains the oldest texts in any Iranian language. The compositions took shape over several centuries in what scholars call the Avestan period, which in the broadest proposals would stretch from the 15th century BCE to the 4th century BCE.

Zoroastrian tradition has its own account of how the Avesta was transmitted. According to the Pahlavi books of the ninth to eleventh centuries, preserved in the Denkard, the twenty-one nasks, or books, were created by Ahura Mazda and brought by Zoroaster to his patron Vishtaspa. Two copies were supposedly made, one for the treasury and one for the royal archives. The story goes on to say that after Alexander's conquest the Greeks destroyed or scattered the text, having first translated its scientific passages. Centuries later, a Parthian king named Valaksh was said to have gathered the fragments, both written and oral. In the Sasanian era, the priest Tansar was credited with selecting only part of the works as authoritative, and Adurbad Mahraspandan with revising the canon, with a final revision supposedly made under Khosrow I. These are traditional legends rather than verifiable history.

Modern scholarship largely sets this account aside. There is now broad agreement that for most of its long history the various texts were handed down orally and independently of one another. Linguistic evidence has allowed specialists to distinguish several phases of composition. The large edition assembled in the Sasanian period was lost after the Islamic conquest of Iran. The manuscripts that survive are fragmentary and were kept for ritual and devotional use. The oldest surviving fragment dates to 1323 CE. Commentators of the ninth and tenth centuries cite far more verses than remain today, and only about a quarter of those appear in the surviving texts. Legal, historical and legendary texts were probably lost, while the most valuable parts, including the oldest texts, survived because priests knew them by heart.

The liturgical Avesta falls into three main collections. The Yasna, meaning roughly sacrifice or offering, is the principal one and gives its name to the ceremony in which it is recited. At its heart are the Gathas, hymns traditionally attributed to Zoroaster, composed in a metre similar to that of the Vedic hymns, along with the Yasna Haptanghaiti, which is nearly as old but in prose. The Visperad contains additional invocations to the yazatas, the divine beings who receive worship. The Vendidad is different: a mixed collection of prose texts on purity laws and on driving away the daevas, beings regarded in Persia as wicked. It is the only liturgical text not recited from memory. Its second fargard tells the legend of Yima, and its first presents a dualist creation myth followed by a destructive winter that recalls the flood story. The Yashts are twenty-one hymns to individual divinities, and together with other minor texts they make up the Khordeh Avesta, or Little Avesta, which became a common prayer book for laypeople once printed editions appeared.

The Avesta matters for the comparative study of Indo-European religion, because its language and mythology relate closely to those of Vedic India. Western scholars came to it late. Anquetil-Duperron published French translations in 1771, based on texts he had found among Parsi communities in India. The most widely used edition remains that of Karl Friedrich Geldner, and James Darmesteter's 1887 French translation and Fritz Wolff's German one are among the few reliable translations. There is no reliable translation into Spanish.

Author: Zoroaster

Trivia

Seventy-two threads. The 72 strands of lamb's wool in the kushti, the sacred cord worn by Zoroastrians, stand for the 72 sections of the Yasna.
A prayer of twenty-one words. According to the Denkard, the twenty-one nasks mirror the twenty-one words of the Ahuna Vairya prayer, arranged in three lines of seven.
A book preserved whole. Nask 19, contained in the Vendidad, is the only one of the twenty-one nasks to survive in full.
