The Cattle Raid of Cooley, Ireland's national epic

The Táin

The Táin, also called The Cattle Raid of Cooley, is a central text of the Ulster Cycle, set in a pagan heroic age and handed down orally for centuries before being written down. It tells of the war that Queen Medb of Connacht wages to seize a prodigious stud bull, while the young Cú Chulainn stands alone as Ulster's defender. It is often regarded as Ireland's national epic.

The story begins with a domestic quarrel. One night, Medb and her husband Ailill, the rulers of Connacht, compare their possessions and find that they are equal in everything except one thing: Ailill owns Finnbhennach, an extraordinarily fertile bull who had once belonged to Medb's herd. According to the tale, he refused to be owned by a woman and transferred himself to her husband. Medb resolves to even the score and looks for a bull as valuable. She finds one at Cooley, in Ulster, owned by Dáire mac Fiachna, who agrees to lend it to her for a year. However, her drunken messengers reveal that she intends to take the animal by force if refused. The deal collapses, and war follows.

Ulster is weakened by a curse, the ces noínden, which leaves its warriors prostrate for months. Another tale ascribes the curse to the goddess Macha, who was forced to race a chariot while heavily pregnant. Only Cú Chulainn, a seventeen-year-old youth, remains fit to fight, and he is away on a romantic tryst when the invaders cross the border. He harries the army with guerrilla tactics and, at the fords, challenges champion after champion to single combat. Supernatural beings from the Tuatha Dé Danann take part on both sides. The war goddess Morrígan tries to win him and, when rejected, attacks him in animal forms. Lug, who reveals himself as Cú Chulainn's father, heals him after an especially grueling fight.

The best-known episode concerns Ferdiad, Cú Chulainn's foster-brother. Medb persuades him to fight, and the duel lasts three days. Cú Chulainn wins, but at great emotional cost. When the Ulstermen recover, the final battle decides the conflict. Medb flees, and Cú Chulainn spares her and protects her retreat. Donn Cuailnge returns to Connacht and kills Finnbhennach, but is mortally wounded. According to the tradition, he wanders across Ireland, shedding pieces of his rival from his horns, which give rise to place names, before dying of exhaustion at home.

The surviving text exists in three recensions. The first is partly preserved in the Lebor na hUidre and the Yellow Book of Lecan, the second in the Book of Leinster, which is the most complete and most often translated. Scholars think the material derives from much older oral storytelling, and the tale has no clearly definable historical setting. The written versions show strong Christian influence, though pagan elements remain prominent.

The work has been translated many times since the nineteenth century, and Thomas Kinsella's 1969 translation brought it to a wide readership. Its influence on Irish literature and cultural identity has been considerable, and the story of the bulls remains a key reference for those studying Celtic myth.

Trivia

A bull with a name. The main animal is called Donn Cuailnge, and his rival Finnbhennach is almost as famous. Both are presented as animals of near-divine value.
Prose and verse. The text alternates prose with poems spoken by the characters, a mix common in early Irish literature.
