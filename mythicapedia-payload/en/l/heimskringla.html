The great chronicle of the kings of Norway

Heimskringla

Heimskringla is a collection of sagas written in Old Norse in Iceland around 1225 and traditionally attributed to Snorri Sturluson. It covers roughly four hundred years of Norwegian history, from a mythological prehistory centred on the Ynglings to the death of Eystein Meyla in 1177. Its first part draws on Norse mythology, and as the collection advances the accounts become more reliable as history.

Heimskringla means "the circle of the world", and its name comes from the opening words of the work in the first surviving manuscript, whose first page has been lost. The phrase "Kringla heimsins" began the text and gave the collection its title, which was only applied in the seventeenth century. Today it is regarded as the best known of the Old Norwegian kings' sagas, a prose genre that mixes legend, poetry and documented events.

The work consists of sixteen narratives that are usually grouped into three blocks, giving the whole the shape of a triptych. The first block opens with the Ynglinga saga, which tells the mythological prehistory of the Ynglings dynasty. According to the tradition the text records, their lineage links back to Freyr of the Vanir and to Odin of the Æsir, whose home was Asgard in the East. The stories describe figures who reappear among peoples across Europe and beyond, and a notion of rebirth and defiance of death that runs through several tales, such as the story of Örvar Odd. Snorri presents these figures as ancestors of kings, which modern readers should understand as a narrative construction rather than verified history.

From the saga of Halfdan the Black onwards, the collection focuses on individual rulers. Its central piece is the saga of Olaf II of Norway, the king later venerated as a saint. His fifteen-year reign fills about a third of the entire work, and it combines political chronicle, struggles with local chieftains and the spread of Christianity. Another major block is the saga of Harald Hardrada, which recounts his journeys to the East, his exploits in Constantinople, Syria and Sicily, his skill as a skald, and his battles in England. Harald fell at Stamford Bridge in 1066, only a few days before Harold Godwinson fell at Hastings.

The work then continues with the saga of Sigurd the Crusader, which takes the Norwegian fleet as far as Palestine, where the authors describe a fight against Arab Muslim pirates whom they call Vikings. The final sagas trace the succession of kings and civil wars down to Magnus Erlingsson, and the collection closes with the death of Eystein Meyla in 1177.

Snorri's sources were varied and included earlier kings' sagas such as the Morkinskinna and the Fagrskinna, twelfth-century Norwegian synoptic histories, oral traditions and many skaldic poems. He did not simply copy them: he rewrote them in a carefully crafted narrative style in which myth, poetry and history intertwine. Its literary quality and documentary value have made Heimskringla a key source for understanding the social and political development of northern Europe in the Middle Ages.

Authorship, however, is not attributed to Snorri Sturluson in any medieval manuscript. The attribution appears in sixteenth-century Danish translations by Peder Claussøn Friis and Laurents Hanssøn, who probably used a manuscript now lost. For this reason, scholars date the work to Iceland around 1225 to 1230 and treat the identification of its author with caution. This blend of myth, history and literature is exactly what makes Heimskringla so useful for studying how Norse societies remembered their past.

Trivia

A title born from a phrase. The name comes from the words with which the text opens in a manuscript, "Kringla heimsins". It was first used in the seventeenth century.
Vikings in Palestine. The saga of Sigurd the Crusader describes Arab Muslim pirates whom the authors call Vikings, in an episode set in Palestine.
An almost lost manuscript. The oldest surviving parchment copy is a single leaf from around 1260, part of a saga of St Olaf. The rest of the manuscript was lost in a fire in 1728.
