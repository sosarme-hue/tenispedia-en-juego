The Persian epic of Rustam's son

Faramarz-nama

The Faramarz-nama is a Persian epic poem that follows the adventures of Faramarz, son of the hero Rustam. Two very different versions bear this name, and together they show how Iranian heroic tales were passed on and reshaped after Ferdowsi's Shahnameh.

The Faramarz-nama (also spelled Faramarz-nameh) is a Persian epic centred on Faramarz, the son of the hero Rustam. It is far less famous than the Shahnameh, yet it preserves episodes that help explain how Iranian epic storytelling developed after Ferdowsi.

The earliest mention of these tales appears in the chronicle Tarikh-i Sistan, which refers to a work about Faramarz in twelve volumes, probably in prose. That text has been lost. Two poems survive. The older one is an anonymous manuscript dated between the mid-11th and 12th centuries. Its author presents himself as an admirer of Ferdowsi, says he wrote it at the age of thirty-six, and calls himself a native of Piruzabad, although scholars suspect a spelling error and propose Forsabad, near Marv. His main source was a book by Azadsarv, and his style shows the strong influence of the Shahnameh.

This first poem tells how Faramarz sails to India on the orders of King Kay Kavus, to help the Indian ruler Nowshaad Shah. There he defeats Konnās Dīv, a carrion-eating demon who had abducted the king's daughter, a talking rhinoceros, a dragon, and thirty thousand rhinoceroses. He must then complete seven labours, a pattern that recalls Rustam's seven tasks and became a model for Persian heroic epics. In the sixth, he debates with an Indian Brahman, who renounces idol worship and turns to Yazdān. The poem ends before the seventh labour is told.

The second group of manuscripts is much longer. An edition of 464 pages, containing roughly nine to ten thousand couplets, was printed in Bombay in 1906 by the Zoroastrian scholar Rostam, son of Bahrām Soruš of Taft. This version begins with Rustam, whose journey to India leads to the birth of Faramarz from his union with the Indian king's daughter. One section centres on Banu Gushasp, and another adds about six thousand couplets of new adventures, including battles with Tovorg, Rāy and the demon Tajānū, and a search for a young woman who vanishes into a spring, daughter of the king of the fairies. Their son Sām inspired Khwaju Kermani's Sām-nāma, while the exploits of his brother Āḏar-Borzīn close the Bahman-nama.

Trivia

A lost work. The chronicle Tarikh-i Sistan mentions a account of Faramarz in twelve volumes. It is thought to have been in prose and has not survived.
A Bombay edition. The longer version was printed in Bombay in 1906 by the Zoroastrian scholar Rostam, who travelled to India to gather stories about Faramarz.
