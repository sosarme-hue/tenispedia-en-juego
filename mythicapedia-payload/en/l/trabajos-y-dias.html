Hesiod's poem on farming, justice and the origins of human toil

Works and Days

Works and Days is a didactic poem attributed to Hesiod, composed around the late eighth or early seventh century BC. Presented as a farmer's almanac and a moral lecture addressed to his brother Perses, it preserves some of the best-known myths of Greek tradition, including Prometheus and Pandora and the myth of the five ages. Its central message is that work is the lot of humankind and that justice is the best way to bear it.

Works and Days (in ancient Greek Ἔργα καὶ Ἡμέραι, sometimes cited by its Latin title Opera et Dies) is a didactic poem of some 828 lines in dactylic hexameter. Tradition ascribes it to Hesiod, an early Greek poet who is generally dated to around 700 BC, although the exact date is still debated. Its starting point is a family quarrel. After the inheritance of their father's estate was divided, Perses claimed more than his fair share by winning over the 'bribe-devouring kings', the judges who could be bought. Hesiod does not offer his brother money or land, which would soon be squandered. Instead, he offers instruction in the virtues of work and the wisdom that can earn a living.

The poem opens with a short invocation to the Muses, far shorter than the one in the Theogony, and asks Zeus to set justice straight. Unlike the Theogony, which follows a genealogical line, Works and Days is arranged by theme rather than by chronology, though its farming calendars and sailing sections keep an internal sequence. Hesiod distinguishes two kinds of Strife: one is destructive and breeds wars and lawsuits, while the other spurs people to compete through honest labour.

The most famous section tells the story of Prometheus and Pandora. According to the myth, Prometheus tricked Zeus over the division of a feast between gods and humans, and afterwards stole fire. As punishment, Zeus had Pandora fashioned, and Epimetheus, Prometheus's brother, accepted her despite warnings. She carried a jar full of evils, and when she opened it they spread across the world; only Hope stayed inside. In this telling, toil and suffering are part of the human condition from the start.

The myth of the five ages comes next. In the Golden Age, under Cronus, people lived without hardship, the earth yielded its fruits unasked, and death came like sleep. The Silver Age was far inferior: people stayed as children for a long time, quarrelled and disobeyed the gods, and Zeus destroyed them, though they were granted a place of honour among mortals beneath the earth. The Bronze Age was violent and warlike, and its people perished by their own hands. The Age of Heroes was more just and noble, and its members fell in wars such as those at Thebes and Troy, yet they were carried off to the Isles of the Blessed. The fifth is the Iron Age, the time in which Hesiod himself lives: an age of toil and hardship, in which children are born grey-haired, respect and justice are forgotten, and Aidos and Nemesis abandon the earth.

Within this frame the poet includes fables and proverbs. The best known is the tale of the hawk and the nightingale: a hawk seizes a singing bird, which pleads in vain, and the hawk replies that it is held by a stronger creature. The lesson is that the powerful should not prey on the weak. Such collections of sayings, partly inherited from Near Eastern traditions such as the wisdom attributed to Ahikar, mingle with similes and practical advice.

The second half of the poem turns to country life. Hesiod advises on sowing, harvesting and pruning, on how to treat workers, and on when it is safe to sail. He also records customs and superstitions, such as when one should marry or which household objects to avoid, along with a list of days in the month that are favourable or dangerous for particular tasks, from shearing to sowing. Many of these instructions combine practical observation with religious belief and reference to the gods.

Scholars read the poem against an agrarian crisis in mainland Greece that encouraged waves of colonial expeditions in search of new land. Beyond its personal frame, then, Works and Days reflects the economic pressure of its time. Its legacy is considerable: it shaped later didactic poetry, including Virgil's Georgics, and it remains one of the main sources for the myths of Prometheus, Pandora and the ages of the world.

Trivia

Two Eris. Hesiod distinguishes in the poem between a harmful Eris, who provokes wars and lawsuits, and another who pushes people to work and compete honestly.
A jar of evils. In the myth of Pandora, she carries a jar containing all the evils; when it is opened, only Hope remains inside.
An 828-line poem. The work is written in dactylic hexameter and runs to about 828 lines, a modest length compared with Homeric epic.
