Apollonius of Rhodes's epic on Jason and the Golden Fleece

Argonautica

The Argonautica is the only complete Hellenistic epic to survive, and it tells how Jason and the Argonauts sailed to Colchis in search of the Golden Fleece. Apollonius of Rhodes reworked the myth with the scholarly interests of his age and gave a striking place to the love between Jason and Medea. The poem went on to shape Latin poetry, from Varro Atacinus to Virgil.

The Argonautica is the most important surviving work of Apollonius of Rhodes and the only Hellenistic epic poem preserved in full. Written in Greek in the third century BC, it tells the story of Jason and the Argonauts, the heroes who, according to the myth, undertook a dangerous voyage to distant Colchis to bring back the Golden Fleece. Its audience already knew the outline well, especially the figure of Medea, the Colchian princess and sorceress. Apollonius took that familiar material and built from it an epic with a strong scholarly character.

The poem is divided into four books. The first two follow the voyage from Iolcus, in Thessaly, through the Propontis and the Black Sea towards the coast of Colchis. Along the way the heroes stop at places such as Lemnos, where the women, led by Hypsipyle, have killed all their men, or the land of the Doliones, where King Cyzicus dies in a night battle caused by mistaken identity, or Mysia, where the young Hylas disappears. The third book focuses on Colchis, on the trials set by King Aeëtes and on the help Medea gives Jason. The fourth describes the return, which does not follow the outward route but crosses the Danube, the Po, the Mediterranean and the north coast of Africa.

What sets this work apart from Homer is its treatment of character. Apollonius gave generous space to feeling and to Medea's inner conflict, as she wavers between her family and the stranger who has arrived in her land. Many modern readers see in that portrait one of the earliest literary studies of love as a psychological experience. Other critics have pointed out that the heroes appear less confident and more despondent than Homer's, and that the poem seems to question the traditional ideal of heroism. The German classicist H. Fränkel highlighted these moods of despair, while D. A. Van Krevelen argued that almost all the characters, apart from Medea, lack interesting qualities.

The poem also contains many learned episodes. Apollonius adds explanations of the origins of names, customs and places, known to scholars as aitia, along with descriptions of wonders and curiosities. This feature has led to debate over whether the work is a unified narrative or whether the plot serves as a thread on which to string episodes of geography, ethnography and religion. Its relationship with the poetry of Callimachus has also been discussed. A late tradition speaks of a quarrel between the two poets, although modern scholars often treat that account with caution and prefer to focus on the similarities in their work.

The date of composition is also disputed. It has been suggested that the poem was published during the reign of Ptolemy II Philadelphus, or somewhat later. An astronomical analysis by Jackie Murray argues that the poem refers to a particular year, 238 BC, which would point to the time of Ptolemy III Euergetes, as most ancient sources claim.

The work's influence was considerable. In Latin, Varro Atacinus translated it, Valerius Flaccus imitated it, and Catullus and Ovid drew on it. Virgil used it as a model for the Aeneid, so a Greek poem written centuries earlier helped shape the great Roman epic. For anyone approaching the legend of the Argonauts today, it remains one of the most personal and thought-provoking versions of the story.

Author: Apollonius of Rhodes

Trivia

A poem in the Homeric tradition. Apollonius wrote in a style that echoed Homer, but with a more reflective tone and a close attention to geographical detail that ancient readers enjoyed.
Love in Greek literature. Medea, torn between duty and desire, is one of the most complex female characters in ancient literature.
A return by another route. The Argonauts return by a different route from the outward voyage, crossing rivers and seas that the poem describes in considerable detail.
