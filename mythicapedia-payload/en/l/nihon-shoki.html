The Chronicles of Japan and Its Foundational Myths

Nihon Shoki

The Nihon Shoki, also known as the Nihongi, is one of the two great ancient chronicles of Japan, completed in 720. It gathers stories of creation, of the gods, and of the earliest rulers, presenting them as the origin of the country and of the imperial family. Today it is studied both as a religious text and as a historical document, with the caution its chronology demands.

The Nihon Shoki was completed in 720 under the direction of Prince Toneri, with the help of Ō no Yasumaro. Ki no Kiyohito and Miyake no Fujimaro also took part, along with other compilers whose names are now lost. Tradition holds that the work was presented to Empress Genshō. Its title can be rendered roughly as “origin of the sun.” The court preferred this name to the Chinese term Wa, a choice that asserted Japan’s independence from China, even though the book drew heavily on Chinese historical models.

The text is written in classical Chinese, the usual language of official documents at the time, but it also includes phonetic transcriptions of Japanese for names, songs, and pronunciation notes. Its thirty volumes run from the age of the gods to the reign of Empress Jitō in 697. The aim was a written history that could stand beside the annals of the Chinese court, recording the merits of virtuous rulers and the failings of bad ones.

The mythological section opens with the origin of the world and the first generations of deities, beginning with Kuninotokotachi. Many of its stories overlap with those in the Kojiki, the other great chronicle, completed only a few years earlier, in 712. The Nihon Shoki, however, often gives several versions of the same episode, introducing them with phrases such as “some documents say.” For this reason its mythic material feels more fragmented, and many readers find it less vivid than the Kojiki.

Among the best-known stories is that of Izanagi and Izanami, the divine pair who form the islands of Japan by stirring the sea with a jeweled spear. Izanami dies giving birth to Kagutsuchi, the fire god. Izanagi then travels to Yomi, the land of the dead, to bring her back, but flees in horror at the sight of her decaying body and seals the entrance. The worlds of the living and the dead are separated forever. After purifying himself, he gives birth to Amaterasu, the sun goddess, Tsukuyomi, the moon god, and Susanoo, the god of the sea and storms.

Other tales follow Susanoo, whose destructive behavior drives Amaterasu into the Heavenly Rock Cave, plunging the world into darkness until the gods devise a trick to lure her out. Ōkuninushi, meanwhile, survives the trials set by Susanoo in the underworld and eventually rules the earthly realm. Later, Amaterasu sends her grandson Ninigi to govern the world below. The story of Hoori, who travels to the palace of the sea goddess and marries her daughter, leads to the line that gives rise to the imperial family, whose first ruler is traditionally identified as Kamu-Yamato-Iwarebiko, known as Jinmu.

The book matters greatly to Japanese mythology. It presents a sacred history linking the emperor to the sun goddess, supports Shinto beliefs about the relationship between nature, people, and the kami, and teaches values such as perseverance, courage, and loyalty. For centuries it also offered the people a shared story about the nation’s origins and the legitimacy of the dynasty. For Shinto practitioners these stories remain a living part of their faith, and they deserve to be read that way.

Modern historians have reexamined its chronology. The date that places the founding of Japan in 660 BCE is regarded as mythical. It is widely believed that the year was chosen because it falls on a xīn-yǒu year in the sexagenary cycle, which Taoist thought considered a suitable time for revolution. The compilers linked it to a calculation that counted from a reform in 601. Scholars also think that the reigns of the earliest rulers, along with those of Ōjin and Nintoku, were lengthened to give the dynasty an ancient pedigree that fit numerological expectations.

The eight generations of emperors from the second to the ninth are now generally considered a compilers’ invention, and the existence of Jinmu is doubted. Emperor Ōjin is regarded as the first truly historical monarch. Historians generally accept the events recorded from the year 673 onward.

The work drew on court annals kept in Yamato since the sixth century, on documents and traditions submitted by clans serving the court, and on Korean sources, especially from Baekje. Some earlier materials were lost when the residence of Soga no Umako was destroyed during the Isshi Incident in 645, so we know them only through the book itself. Today it remains indispensable for studying Japanese mythic thought and for understanding how the idea of the nation was built.

Trivia

A date chosen by the calendar. The year 660 BCE, taken as the mythical founding of Japan, is believed to have been chosen because it falls on a xīn-yǒu year in the Chinese sexagenary cycle, which Taoist thought considered favorable for revolution.
