K. Langloh Parker's collection of Aboriginal Australian stories

Australian Legendary Tales

Australian Legendary Tales is a collection of stories that K. Langloh Parker gathered from Aboriginal Australians and translated into English. First published in 1896, it was widely read and is considered one of the first substantial works of Aboriginal Australian cultural material to reach a broad public. Its value and its limitations are best understood by looking at both the stories and the context in which the book was produced.

Australian Legendary Tales gathers stories that K. Langloh Parker collected from Aboriginal Australians and translated into English. It appeared in 1896, printed in London and Melbourne, with more than thirty tales, a glossary and the first story transcribed in its original language. It sold well and was reissued several times, and it is often seen as one of the earliest substantial books of Aboriginal Australian culture to reach a wide readership.

The tales unfold in a 'no-time' where animal spirits, supernatural beings and humans live and interact. Many touch on creation. Baiame, whom Parker calls an 'All Father', appears often, along with Wurrunnah, a culture hero, and animal beings who engage with people and with each other. Parker acknowledges the Noongahburrah people in her preface and dedicates the book to Peter Hippi, whom she describes as their king.

The introduction and the editorial oversight came from Andrew Lang, a British folklorist who compared some of the tales to Ovid's Metamorphoses and treated them as the work of savage inferiors. The illustrations, later attributed to Tommy McRae from an inscription on the original drawings, also received dismissive comments from Lang. Many favourable British reviews read the book as a work of Lang's scholarship rather than Parker's, although Parker herself seems to have grown sceptical of his authority over time.

Parker had lived among Aboriginal communities since childhood. Later commentators value her familiarity with the people, her word lists and her careful translations, even though some of her methods would be unacceptable today. An 1897 review by A. G. Stephens in The Bulletin questioned the ethnological worth of the material and even suggested that Parker had invented it.

In 1953, Henrietta Drake-Brockman selected and edited a children's edition illustrated by Elizabeth Durack, which won an award from the Children's Book Council of Australia. Later retellings followed, by Vashti Farrer in 1982 and by Johanna Lambert in 1993, the latter including bark paintings by Dorothy Djukulul.

Trivia

A time without a date. Parker describes the setting of the tales as a 'no-time', a period without a specific date. It places the stories outside historical chronology.
A signature in the drawings. The illustrations were long credited to an 'untaught Australian native'. The artist was later identified as Tommy McRae through an inscription on the original drawings found among Lang's papers.
A Lang series. The book was published as part of a series overseen by Andrew Lang, alongside other works presented as folklore studies or fairy tales.
