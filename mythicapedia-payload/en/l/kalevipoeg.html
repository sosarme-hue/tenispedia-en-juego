Estonia's national epic

Kalevipoeg

Kalevipoeg is an epic poem by Friedrich Reinhold Kreutzwald, published between 1857 and 1861, and since then regarded as Estonia's national epic. It gathers oral legends about a heroic giant who shaped the landscape and who, according to tradition, ended up guarding the gates of hell.

Kalevipoeg is the epic poem that Friedrich Reinhold Kreutzwald published between 1857 and 1861, and it has since been regarded as Estonia's national epic. Its hero, a giant from Estonian oral tradition, comes from material far older than the poem itself. The stories survive in runic songs, chants passed down by word of mouth, in which the hero battles other giants and enemies of the people.

According to these traditions, Kalevipoeg is the youngest son of Kalev and Linda. He was born after his father's death and surpassed his brothers in wit and strength. The legends credit him with shaping the landscape: lakes, hills and stones are said to be traces of his deeds. The tradition holds, for instance, that the Toompea hill is Kalev's burial mound and that Lake Ülemiste was formed from Linda's tears.

The poem's own history is a long one. In 1839, Friedrich Robert Faehlmann read a paper to the Learned Estonian Society about the legends of Kalevipoeg and sketched the plot of a romantic national epic. After Faehlmann's death, Kreutzwald took up the project in 1850. He saw his task as reconstructing an oral epic that had vanished, so he gathered folk stories and wove them into a single whole. The first version, from 1853, could not be printed because of censorship. The second, heavily revised, appeared in instalments between 1857 and 1861 as a publication of the Society, with a German translation. In 1862 a third, somewhat shorter version came out for general readers. It was printed in Kuopio, in Finland.

The poem runs to about 19,000 lines across twenty cantos, written in the alliterative verse of old Estonian poetry. Only about one eighth of the lines are authentic folk material; the rest imitate that style. The story follows the hero's life: his journey to Finland to look for his mother, who has been abducted by a sorcerer; the sword forged by Ilmarinen, which he later uses in a drunken quarrel to kill the smith's eldest son; and his reign in Estonia, where he builds towns and forts. He then travels to the ends of the earth, defeats Satan in a trial of strength and rescues three maidens from hell.

According to the tale, war eventually brings destruction to Estonia. His loyal companions are killed, and Kalevipoeg hands the kingship to his brother Olev and retreats, dejected, into the forest. While crossing a river, the cursed sword, which had fallen into the water earlier, cuts off his legs. He dies and goes to heaven. Taara, whom some versions identify with Thor, consults the other gods, brings him back to life, seats him on a white horse and sends him to the gates of hell. There he must strike the rock with his fist, sealing hell inside it. He remains there as its guardian.

The poem became a symbol of identity in a country that for centuries lived under foreign rule, which is why it is read as the epic of a whole people. Its links with Finland are also strong. Kalevipoeg recalls the hero of the Finnish Kalevala, and both traditions share figures such as Ilmarinen. Scholars still debate how much of this kinship comes from a common stock of stories and how much from Kreutzwald's own shaping. Some researchers suggest that the "Caelic" named in Widsith, an Anglo-Saxon poem of the sixth or seventh century, may refer to Kaleva, the ancestor of the Finnish and Estonian heroes, though this cannot be proven. The same poem also contains the spelling wicing, the earliest known written use of the word Viking.

Author: Friedrich Reinhold Kreutzwald Date: 1857-

Trivia

A lake born of tears. According to the tradition retold in the poem, Lake Ülemiste was formed from the tears Linda shed for seven days and nights after Kalev's death. It lies in what is now the area of Tallinn.
A very early mention. Many specialists see Kalevipoeg in Widsith, an Anglo-Saxon poem from the sixth or seventh century. The same text contains the spelling wicing, the earliest known written use of the word Viking.
A censored poem. The first version, from 1853 and about 13,800 lines long, could not be printed because of censorship. The work reached readers only in later, revised and expanded versions.
