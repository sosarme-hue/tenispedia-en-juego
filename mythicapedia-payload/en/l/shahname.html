The national epic of Iran

Shahnameh

The Shahnameh, or 'Book of Kings', is a long epic poem written by the Persian poet Ferdowsi between 977 and 1010. It traces the mythical and partly historical past of Iran, from the creation of the world to the Muslim conquest in the seventh century. For Persian speakers it remains a foundational work of their cultural identity and language.

The Shahnameh, also romanized as Shahnama, is a long epic poem written by the Persian poet Ferdowsi. According to the sources, he began it in 977 and completed it on 8 March 1010, after about 33 years of work. With some 50,000 couplets, it ranks among the longest epic poems in the world and is the longest ever composed by a single author.

The poem recounts the mythical and partly historical past of Iran, from the creation of the world to the Muslim conquest in the seventh century. Its mythical section is relatively short, around 2,100 verses, and opens with the creation of the world and of humankind as the Sasanians believed it. Then come Keyumars, the first man and first king; Hushang, who by accident discovered fire and gave rise to the Sadeh festival; Tahmuras and Jamshid; the tyrant Zahhak; Kaveh; and Fereydun, who divided his realm among his three sons, Salm, Tur and Iraj.

Almost two-thirds of the work belongs to the heroic age, which runs from the reign of Manuchehr until the conquest of Alexander the Great. The best-known figure here is Rostam, described as the bravest of the brave, whose home lay in Sistan. His story includes the romance of Zal and Rudaba, his Seven Labours, his fatal duel with his son Sohrab, the tragedy of Siyavash, the love of Bijan and Manijeh, and the long wars against Afrasiab. Although these heroes are memorable, many readers see Iran itself as the true hero of the poem.

The historical section briefly mentions the Arsacid, or Parthian, dynasty and then turns to the Sasanians, whose history the poem relates with considerable accuracy. The fall of that dynasty to the Arab armies is told in a more romantic tone.

Ferdowsi did not start from nothing. He relied mainly on a prose Shahnameh compiled in his native Tus, which in turn translated the Khwadāy-Nāmag, or 'Book of Kings', a Middle Persian chronicle put together in the late Sasanian period. Other material came from the Kar-Namag i Ardashir i Pabagan, which describes how Ardashir I rose to power. The poet Daqiqi had begun to put that chronicle into verse, but he met a violent end after writing about 1,000 verses; Ferdowsi incorporated them, acknowledging their author. Some scholars also think he drew on Zoroastrian books, such as the now-lost Chihrdad. The orientalists Josef Markwart and Theodor Nöldeke argued that much Parthian material was reshaped in the poem, and Markwart traced Rostam's roots to Parthian noble houses.

The Shahnameh is also a work of deep patriotism. Traditional Iranian historiography holds that Ferdowsi wrote it to preserve the memory of Persia's golden days at a time when the country's independence was under threat. Most scholars believe his main aim was to safeguard the pre-Islamic legacy of myth and history, although some authors have challenged this reading. Lines in which the poet addresses the heavens, 'spit on your face, oh heavens, spit!', are read as a rebuke to the Muslim invaders who damaged Zoroastrianism. For this reason the epic was admired by many rulers yet viewed with suspicion by others. During the reign of Mohammad Reza Shah it was largely set aside, and Muslim reformists of the 1970s, such as Ali Shariati, also criticised parts of its content.

Its linguistic influence has been just as strong. Ferdowsi avoided Arabic vocabulary wherever he could, a point the British Iranologist E. G. Browne made in the nineteenth century, although recent scholarship, including the work of Mohammed Moinfar, has examined that claim. Many experts believe that modern Persian remains essentially the language of Ferdowsi's day, largely thanks to the poem, which later poets studied as a model. Ibn al-Athir called it the 'Persian Quran', a title not commonly used by Persian speakers but a sign of its weight for millions of people in Iran, Afghanistan, Tajikistan and beyond, and for Zoroastrians, for whom it links the origins of their faith to the defeat of the last Zoroastrian king.

Trivia

A poem of 50,000 couplets. It is one of the longest epic poems in the world and the longest ever written by a single author. Ferdowsi took about 33 years to finish it.
Fire discovered by accident. According to the story, Hushang discovered fire by chance, and the Sadeh festival was established in its honour.
A poet who did not finish. Daqiqi, who had begun turning the chronicle into verse, met a violent end after writing about a thousand lines, which Ferdowsi later included in his own work.
