Maya chronicles written after the conquest

Books of Chilam Balam

The Books of Chilam Balam are handwritten Yucatec Maya miscellanies, mostly from the 17th and 18th centuries, that bring together history, prophecy, myth, the calendar and medicine. They take their name from the legendary author attributed to them and from the towns where they were kept. They offer a rare window into Maya thought after contact with the Spaniards.

The Books of Chilam Balam are a group of handwritten manuscripts in the Yucatec Maya language, written with the Latin alphabet and composed by anonymous authors, chiefly in the 17th and 18th centuries. They are named after the small towns where they were kept, such as Chumayel, Maní and Tizimín, and they mix knowledge of history, prophecy, religion, the calendar, astronomy and medicine in which Maya and Spanish traditions meet. The texts credit a legendary figure, Chilam Balam, with having foretold the arrival of the Spaniards in Yucatán.

The name combines two words whose meaning is not certain. Chilam is linked to the title of the priests who interpreted the books and the will of the gods, while balam can mean 'sorcerer', 'magician' or 'jaguar'. This has led to renderings such as 'mouth of the jaguar' or 'priest of the jaguar god'. Some passages, written in very terse language, seem to render hieroglyphic script, which suggests that part of the content goes back to the period before the conquest.

The books are not one continuous story but a mixture of genres. There are historical chronicles arranged by the Maya calendar and its katuns of twenty years, alongside migration legends and lists of lords. There are also prophecies and collections of riddles, which in some cases were used to confirm local lords in their office. The mythic texts tell of the destruction and re-creation of the world, linked to the start of a particular katun, and describe rituals such as that of the Four Burners or the one concerning the birth of maize, regarded as a divine gift.

Other sections are more practical: classifications of the twenty named days with birds, plants, human types and occupations; treatises on astrology that follow Ptolemaic models and the European zodiac; farming almanacs; and herbal prescriptions drawn from Greek and Arab traditions rather than from Maya incantations. Catholic material also appears, such as saints' feast days and prayers.

Their importance is considerable because, after the conquest, much of the Maya religious writing was destroyed by missionaries. Some historians believe that the books preserve, through collective memory, fragments of what was lost in the auto de fe at Maní carried out under Diego de Landa. Nine books are known today, though there were probably more. The most studied are those of Chumayel, Maní and Tizimín, and translating them remains a challenge because of their archaic and metaphorical language.

Trivia

A poem that gives a story its title. A poem from the Chilam Balam, translated into Spanish by Antonio Mediz Bolio, gives its title to a short story by the US-born writer Lucia Berlin, who lived for many years in Latin America.
A prelude in a Cuban novel. Alejo Carpentier used a brief excerpt from the Chilam Balam as the prelude to Chapter 3 of his novel The Lost Steps, published in 1953.
A poem on a wall in Mexico City. The same poem appears on one of the walls of the interior courtyard of Mexico City's National Museum of Anthropology.
