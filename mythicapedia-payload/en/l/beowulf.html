The Old English epic of Grendel, his mother and the dragon

Beowulf

Beowulf is an Old English epic poem written in alliterative verse and preserved in a single manuscript. It tells how a Geatish hero rid the Danes of a monster and its mother and, decades later, fought a dragon. Its author and date of composition remain matters of debate among scholars.

Beowulf is an Old English epic of 3,182 alliterative lines. Nobody knows who wrote it or exactly when. Scholars propose very different dates for its composition, and the only firm evidence is the age of the surviving manuscript, which was copied around the year 1000. The manuscript gives the poem no title, and the name comes from its hero.

The first half of the story takes place in Denmark, at the great hall Heorot, whose name means roughly «hart». King Hrothgar built it for feasting, but for twelve years the monster Grendel, said to be descended from the biblical Cain, has been attacking it at night and devouring the king's warriors. Beowulf, a young Geat who serves his uncle, King Hygelac, sails from Geatland with fourteen companions to help. He fights Grendel without weapons, tears off one of the monster's arms, and Grendel flees to die in his lair in the marshes. Then Grendel's mother comes to avenge her son. Beowulf dives into a lake to reach her underwater cavern, fights her there, and kills her with a giant's sword he finds in the hall. He returns to Heorot with Grendel's head and is rewarded with gifts.

The second part comes some fifty years later. Beowulf is now king of the Geats, and his land is threatened by a dragon that wakes when a slave steals a golden cup from its hoard. The king decides to face the beast himself. He is mortally wounded, and only his young relative Wiglaf stays with him to the end. After his death his people cremate him and raise a burial mound by the sea. The poem begins and ends with funerals, and many critics have noted how carefully its parts are balanced against one another.

The question of how the poem came to be has produced very different answers. Some scholars think the legends about Beowulf circulated first in a pagan setting and were gathered and reshaped by a Christian copyist. Others argue that the poem took its present form in a single process, with oral composition and writing happening at nearly the same time. J. R. R. Tolkien believed that the oral tradition came before the written text, and Robert William Chambers saw popular folktale as its main source. The same uncertainty affects interpretation: if the story was composed in pagan times, its pagan elements are central, while if it was written later by a Christian, they may be a decorative echo of the past.

The events are set in Scandinavia during the fifth and sixth centuries, and many of the characters appear in Scandinavian traditions, among them Healfdene, Hrothgar and Eadgils. The raid of King Hygelac into Frisia is recorded by the sixth-century historian Gregory of Tours and dated to about 521. Parallels have been drawn with the Icelandic Grettis saga, the Norse story of Hrolf Kraki and his bear-shaped companion, and folktales such as the Bear's Son. The biblical echoes are clear too, with allusions to Genesis, Exodus and Daniel. Excavations at Lejre, where Scandinavian tradition placed the Scyldings' seat, uncovered a large hall built in the mid-sixth century, which suggests that the poem drew on a real world that legend then reshaped.

The manuscript, known as the Nowell Codex, is kept in the British Library. In 1731 a fire at Ashburnham House in London damaged it: it survived, but its margins were charred and some readings were lost. The Electronic Beowulf project has since used advanced imaging to recover parts of the lost text. The poem was first transcribed in 1786, and nine complete translations appeared in the nineteenth century, including those by John Mitchell Kemble and William Morris. Hundreds more have followed, in prose, rhyme and alliterative verse, which shows how the poem still speaks to readers beside other great European epics such as the Song of the Nibelungs and the Poem of the Cid.

Trivia

A name taken from the hero. The manuscript has no original title. The poem's name comes from its protagonist, a Geat, a people the tradition places in southern Sweden.
One voyage in two sources. The historian Gregory of Tours mentions a raid by Hygelac into Frisia around 521, a detail linked to the poem's account.
A hall at Lejre. Excavations at Lejre in Denmark uncovered a large sixth-century hall, from a period close to the one the poem describes.
