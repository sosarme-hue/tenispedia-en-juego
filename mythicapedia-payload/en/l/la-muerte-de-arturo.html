Sir Thomas Malory's great prose compilation of Arthurian legend

Le Morte d'Arthur

Le Morte d'Arthur is Sir Thomas Malory's prose retelling of the legends of King Arthur, Guinevere, Lancelot, Merlin and the Knights of the Round Table, including the quest for the Holy Grail and the story of Tristan and Iseult. Malory probably completed it around 1470, and William Caxton printed it in 1485. It remains one of the best-known works of Arthurian literature in English.

Le Morte d'Arthur gathers the Arthurian story into one continuous narrative, from the King's conception to his death. Its episodes feature knights and ladies, enchantments, rivalries and quests, all linked by the figure of Arthur and his court. Malory did not invent most of these stories. He drew them from French prose romances and from English poems, rearranging and reshaping them into a single account. He is thought to have finished the book around 1470, apparently while in prison, though the question of who he was is still debated.

Scholars have long argued about the author's identity, since at least six men named Thomas Malory lived in the late fifteenth century. The identification most widely accepted links him to a knight from Newbold Revel in Warwickshire, who inherited the family estate in 1434 and was later accused of theft, attempted murder and rape. The book itself describes its author as a knight and prisoner, and this has been read as a clue. Other researchers have proposed a Malory from Yorkshire, perhaps a religious man, or a namesake from Papworth. Most agree on one point: the author was an aristocrat who mourned the decline of chivalry.

The tradition behind the book is long. Geoffrey of Monmouth, in his Historia Regum Britanniæ (1130-1136), was among the first to give Arthur a full biography. Chrétien de Troyes expanded the cycle with verse romances, and Robert de Boron gave the Grail a central place around 1200. By about 1230, the sprawling Vulgate Cycle had gathered many of these stories, and Malory drew heavily on it and on later French and English material, sometimes referring to a single «Freynshe booke».

What sets Malory apart is his style. Where his sources often pile up mystical and moral allegory, he writes plainly, briskly and economically, letting the drama come through dialogue. He also records practical details, such as the cost of expeditions, which gives the work a grounded, almost soldierly tone. The scholar Eugène Vinaver, who edited the text in the twentieth century, saw this clean prose as the book's enduring achievement.

The early publishing history is equally interesting. Malory's own title was The hoole booke of kyng Arthur & of his noble knyghtes of the rounde table, and the name Le Morte d'Arthur applied only to its final section. When William Caxton printed the work in 1485, he divided it into twenty-one books and hundreds of chapters, added headings and a preface, and may have misread the author's intentions. The book was reprinted in 1498 and 1529 by Wynkyn de Worde, and later editions followed in 1557, 1585 and 1634, each adding changes and new errors.

In 1934, a forgotten manuscript turned up in the library of Winchester College, and it changed how scholars read the book. It is not divided into books or chapters, and it is considered closer to Malory's original than Caxton's text. Vinaver published an edition based on it in 1947 and argued that Malory had gathered separate Arthurian adventures rather than writing one unified work, a debate that has never been fully settled. Many modern editions now rely on the Winchester text.

The book's influence is wide. John Steinbeck used it as a source for his own Arthurian novel, and T. H. White's sequence The Once and Future King drew on it as well, leading to films such as Excalibur. Malory's clear prose helped shape how generations of readers picture Arthur and his knights.

Trivia

A book with a changed name. Malory intended the whole work as The hoole booke of kyng Arthur, and the title Le Morte d'Arthur applied only to its last episode. It was Caxton's printing that made the shorter name famous.
One of England's first printed books. The 1485 edition was among the first books ever printed in England, helping the story of Arthur reach a wide readership.
A manuscript found on a library shelf. The Winchester manuscript was identified in 1934 while the college library was being catalogued, and it is now considered closer to Malory's original.
