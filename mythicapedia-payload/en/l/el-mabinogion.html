Wales's great collection of medieval tales

The Mabinogion

The Mabinogion gathers eleven prose tales in Middle Welsh, written down between the twelfth and thirteenth centuries from earlier oral traditions. Its core is the Four Branches of the Mabinogi, linked by the recurring figure of Pryderi, alongside Arthurian legends and romances that engage with the work of Chrétien de Troyes.

The Mabinogion is a collection of eleven prose tales in Middle Welsh, brought together in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries from earlier oral storytelling. Most of it survives in two manuscripts: the White Book of Rhydderch, written around 1350, and the Red Book of Hergest, copied between about 1382 and 1410, with a few older fragments as well. The tales belong to what is often called the Matter of Britain, and they range widely in tone, mixing drama, philosophy, romance, tragedy, fantasy and humour.

The collective name comes from a misunderstanding. Lady Charlotte Guest, who published the complete collection in English between 1838 and 1845, found the form mabynnogyon in a manuscript and took it for the plural of mabinogi. It is now generally agreed that a medieval scribe made this error. The word mabinogi itself remains something of a puzzle. It is clearly connected with mab, meaning son, boy or young person, and as early as 1632 the lexicographer John Davies quoted a sentence from Math fab Mathonwy using that spelling. Eric P. Hamp suggested a link with Maponos, the Divine Son, a Gaulish deity. Strictly speaking, the term Mabinogi applies only to the Four Branches, which each close with the phrase thus ends this branch of the Mabinogi, and that closing line gave the collection its name.

The Four Branches are the most clearly mythological stories in the collection, and Pryderi appears in all of them, though not always at the centre. Pwyll, Prince of Dyfed, tells of Pryderi's parents, his birth, his disappearance and his recovery. Branwen, daughter of Llŷr, is mostly about Branwen's marriage to the King of Ireland, in which Pryderi plays only a minor role. Manawydan, son of Llŷr, follows Pryderi's return home with Manawydan, Branwen's brother, and the misfortunes that pursue them there. Math, son of Mathonwy, centres on Math and Gwydion, who come into conflict with Pryderi.

The other seven tales are far more varied. Culhwch and Olwen is a classic quest: Culhwch wants to marry Olwen, daughter of a giant, and to win her he must obtain magical objects and survive a series of trials, with help from King Arthur and his warriors. Lludd and Llefelys mixes legend with glimpses of a far distant age. The Dream of Rhonabwy is perhaps the most literary of the group. It is set in the reign of the historical Madog ap Maredudd and ironically measures his time against the heroic age of Arthur, a choice that has prompted very different readings. The Dream of Macsen Wledig is a romanticised story about the Roman emperor Magnus Maximus, who was proclaimed emperor in 383, was defeated in battle in 385 and was beheaded at the order of the Eastern Roman emperor. The History of Taliesin is a later survival that is absent from both the Red and White Books.

The Three Welsh Romances, known as Y Tair Rhamant, are Welsh versions of Arthurian stories that also appear in the work of Chrétien de Troyes. Owain, or the Lady of the Fountain, Peredur son of Efrawg, and Geraint and Enid are not direct translations, and they include material that Chrétien does not contain. Peredur is the Welsh version of Perceval, and in it the Grail has nothing to do with the blood of Christ. It is a dish on which rests the head of the hero's cousin, whose death he must avenge.

The date of composition has been much debated. Ifor Williams proposed a date before 1100, Saunders Lewis argued for a period between 1170 and 1190, and T. M. Charles-Edwards, in 1970, criticised both positions but concluded that the language fits the eleventh century best. In 1991 Patrick Sims-Williams proposed a span from about 1060 to 1200, which is now the most widely accepted view. The question matters because, if the tales predate Geoffrey of Monmouth's Historia Regum Britanniae, they would be a valuable witness to how the Arthurian legend developed.

For a long time, from the eighteenth century onward, scholars read the tales mainly as fragments of pre-Christian Celtic mythology or folklore. Since the 1970s, their plot structures, characterisation and language have been studied closely, and they are now understood as a sophisticated narrative tradition, both oral and written, with Anglo-French influences. Their afterlife is long: Evangeline Walton adapted them into novels from 1936 onward, J.R.R. Tolkien's The Silmarillion draws on the same deep reservoir of Celtic material, Fleetwood Mac recorded the song Rhiannon in 1975, and between 2009 and 2014 the Welsh publisher Seren Books commissioned a series of new retellings.

Trivia

A name born from an error. The word Mabinogion came from a scribe who treated a singular form as a plural. The name stuck thanks to Lady Charlotte Guest's translation.
Pryderi, the common thread. This figure appears in all four tales of the Four Branches, although in some of them he has only a minor role.
A very different Grail. In the Welsh Peredur, the Grail is a dish bearing the head of the hero's cousin, not a cup linked to the blood of Christ.
