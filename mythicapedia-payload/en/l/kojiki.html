The Record of Ancient Matters and the mythic origins of Japan

Kojiki

The Kojiki, compiled in the early eighth century, is the oldest surviving chronicle of Japan. It gathers myths of creation, gods, songs and genealogies. Its narrative presents the imperial family as descended from heavenly deities.

The Kojiki, whose title is often rendered as "Record of Ancient Matters", is the oldest literary work preserved in Japan. According to its preface, it was written down by Ō no Yasumaro at the request of Empress Genmei, who in 711 asked that what the court attendant Hieda no Are had memorized be recorded. Yasumaro presented the finished work in 712. Hieda no Are, a courtier of remarkable memory, had been asked decades earlier by Emperor Tenmu to learn the records and oral traditions of the imperial line. Nothing is known about how he developed such a memory, and the preface is the only source for this account.

The book was composed in Classical Chinese, but it includes songs and poems in Old Japanese, recorded phonetically with Chinese characters through a system called man'yōgana. Reading these songs requires specialized knowledge of that system. The language is associated with the Yamato area and is known today as Old Japanese.

The text is divided into three volumes. The first, the Kamitsumaki or "upper volume", includes the preface and focuses on the creation of the world, the deities who shaped Japan, and the births of many gods. Here appear Izanagi and Izanami, the divine pair who give birth to islands and kami, and the descent of Ninigi-no-Mikoto, grandson of Amaterasu, to Takachiho in Kyūshū. According to the myth, Ninigi becomes the ancestor of the Japanese imperial line.

The second volume, the Nakatsumaki or "middle volume", tells of Emperor Jinmu, presented as Japan's first ruler, and his conquests, and continues to the fifteenth emperor, Ōjin. Many of its stories are mythological, and its historical information is not considered reliable. Emperors two through nine are named, but very few deeds are attributed to them, for reasons that remain unknown.

The third volume, the Shimotsumaki or "lower volume", covers the sixteenth to the thirty-third emperor. It contains few scenes with the gods, unlike the earlier volumes, and information about rulers from the twenty-second to the thirty-third is scarce or absent.

Modern scholars often read the Kojiki less as history than as a work designed to justify the rule of the Yamato court. It gathers traditions from different clans and weaves them into a single mythology, linking noble families to the imperial line. A notable case is Izumo: its local traditions are absorbed into the narrative but placed beneath Yamato's claim to divine rule. The text also never mentions Buddhism, despite Emperor Tenmu's devotion to it, a silence that supports reading the book as myth rather than history.

The Nihon Shoki, completed in 720, was the official chronicle modeled on Chinese dynastic histories and meant to impress foreign envoys. The Kojiki, by contrast, looks inward and was aimed mainly at the imperial family and leading clans.

During the Heian period the Nihon Shoki was far more widely studied than the Kojiki. In the Edo period the situation changed. Scholars of kokugaku, or national learning, saw in the Kojiki the expression of a pure Japanese identity, and Motoori Norinaga devoted decades to it in his Kojiki-den, a 44-volume study written between 1764 and 1798. For Norinaga the book was a faithful account of a harmonious Japan in which the kami, the emperor and the people lived together.

In the Meiji period, Western philology and comparative mythology made it possible to compare the Kojiki with Greek and Roman myths. At the same time, State Shinto treated the text as sacred national history. Under that official ideology, the emperor's divinity and the idea of a superior national body were presented as unquestionable, and those who challenged this reading could face censorship, forced resignation or even trial. This political use of the myths served to legitimize imperial authority and nationalism, and it remains one of the most debated aspects of the work.

In 1913 Tsuda Sōkichi argued that the Kojiki, especially its earlier sections, was neither history nor myth but a document created to legitimize the imperial line. His thesis caused considerable controversy, yet it shaped much of the later research. After the Second World War, the text continued to attract editions, translations and commentaries. Today the Kojiki is studied as literature, as a source for Old Japanese, and as evidence of how the story of Japan's origins was built.

Trivia

A title with several readings. The title is usually read as Kojiki, though it also appears as Furukotofumi. It means something like "record of ancient matters".
Songs written in Chinese characters. The songs in the book use Chinese characters purely for their sound, in a system called man'yōgana that requires specialist knowledge to read.
The first printed edition. The earliest printed edition appeared in Kyoto in 1644, after centuries in which the book circulated mainly as handwritten copies.
