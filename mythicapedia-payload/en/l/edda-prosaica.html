Snorri Sturluson's poetics handbook and the greatest source for Norse mythology

The Prose Edda

The Prose Edda is an Old Norse textbook written in Iceland in the early 13th century to help poets and readers understand alliterative verse and the metaphors of skaldic poetry. Along the way, it gathers a large body of Norse myths, making it the fullest source we have on the subject. Its authorship is traditionally credited to Snorri Sturluson, although scholars still debate how much of the text he actually wrote.

The Prose Edda, also known as the Younger Edda or Snorri's Edda, is an Icelandic textbook written in the early 13th century. Scholars generally credit it to Snorri Sturluson, a historian, poet, and lawspeaker. His aim was practical. Skaldic poetry, the court verse of medieval Scandinavia, followed strict metrical rules and relied on dense metaphors known as kenningar, and a poet could only handle them with a deep knowledge of myth. Snorri wrote the book so that this art would not be lost.

The work has four parts. The Prologue presents the Norse gods as human warriors who came from Troy after its fall, a euhemerist account that echoes Virgil's Aeneid and reflects the outlook of a Christian writer looking back at pagan ancestors. Next comes Gylfaginning, whose title means roughly "the tricking of Gylfi." In it, a king named Gylfi travels to a hall where three enigmatic figures answer his questions about how the world began and how it will end. It is, in effect, a cosmogony told as a dialogue, featuring Odin, Thor, Freyja, Loki, the ash tree Yggdrasil, and Ragnarök, the final battle of gods and worlds.

The third part, Skáldskaparmál, takes the form of a conversation between Ægir, a giant associated with the sea, and Bragi, the god of poetry. Bragi tells stories about the origins of poetic art, including the theft of the mead of poetry, and then lists kennings and heiti, the poetic words that stand in for ordinary ones. Gold, for example, can be named through a tale about Andvari. This section quotes many skaldic verses, which preserve poetry that would otherwise be lost. The fourth part, Háttatal, is a catalogue of verse forms, illustrated with poems by Snorri himself.

The Prose Edda is not a fixed or single text. Seven manuscripts survive, dating from 1300 to 1600, and they differ from one another in significant ways. None of them preserves the book exactly as its author wrote it. Some passages in Skáldskaparmál are thought to be later additions, and even the Prologue has been questioned. These differences are useful, however, because they let researchers compare versions and reconstruct how the tradition changed over time.

The name "Edda" is itself uncertain. Some linked it to Oddi, the place in southern Iceland where Snorri was raised, but this idea is generally rejected. Others connected it to óðr, a word for poetry or inspiration in Old Norse, or to the Latin credo, though none of these explanations is settled. The name Prose Edda distinguishes the work from the Poetic Edda, a collection of verse poems that many of its myths draw on. In the seventeenth century, that collection was wrongly associated with Sæmundr fróði. Outside Iceland, the Prose Edda was little known until the Edda Islandorum appeared in 1665.

Today the Prose Edda is the main source for much of what we know about Norse mythology, and its influence reaches far into modern literature, art, and popular culture. Its stories about Thor, Loki, and Ragnarök have inspired writers and artists for generations. Still, it is a late and shaped account, written by a Christian author and organized for the needs of a handbook. Its versions of the myths are best read as a transmitted tradition rather than an exact record of the religion of pagan Scandinavia.

Trivia

A poetry handbook. Although it is now read mainly as a book of myths, the Prose Edda was conceived as a manual for understanding the meter and metaphorical language of skaldic poetry.
Seven manuscripts. Seven manuscripts of the work survive, dating from 1300 to 1600. They differ from one another, and none is complete.
A disputed name. The origin of the word "Edda" is uncertain, and several explanations have been proposed, including a link to Oddi or to the Old Norse word for poetry.
