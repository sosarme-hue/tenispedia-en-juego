Robert Graves and the grammar of poetic myth

The White Goddess

The White Goddess is a 1948 essay by Robert Graves that proposes a European moon goddess hidden behind the female figures of many mythologies. Graves argues that true poetry keeps the echo of an ancient cult, and the book has been hugely influential, although most specialists reject its historical claims.

Robert Graves published The White Goddess in 1948, building on a series of articles that had appeared in the Welsh magazine Wales. Revised and enlarged editions followed in 1952 and 1961. Before settling on its final title, the book went through several working names, among them The Roebuck in the Thicket and The Three-Fold Muse.

The central claim is that a great moon goddess once existed across Europe and the Middle East, one Graves calls the White Goddess of birth, love and death. In his view, she lies behind the faces of many goddesses in different mythologies, and her cult was tied to popular rites in which poetry held a magical role. Graves believed that only a wild, instinctive poet could truly sense her presence. Nineteenth-century poets, by contrast, he judged to be fragile and unreliable figures.

To reach these conclusions, Graves relied on etymology, the comparison of stories and a method he called iconotropy, which meant reducing images to their original forms. One example is a woodcut of the Judgement of Paris, which he read as showing a single triple goddess rather than Hera, Athena and Aphrodite. Another contested piece is his Celtic tree calendar, in which he linked the names of the Ogham letters to a ritual involving the sacrifice of a sacred king. This calendar has no connection to any documented Celtic calendar. Graves drew on Ogygia by the seventeenth-century Irish bard Roderick O'Flaherty. He also proposed Druantia, a tree goddess of hypothetical standing, who later attracted interest among some modern Neopagans.

The book also argues that the rise of patriarchy and a single male god pushed the goddess aside, with consequences Graves thought were lasting. Later readers have seen this as a forerunner of the matriarchal religion idea taken up by feminist theologians in the 1970s. Yet archaeologists, historians and folklorists have largely rejected it, pointing to weak philology and outdated sources. T. S. Eliot called it a prodigious, monstrous, stupefying and indescribable book, and Ronald Hutton notes that it still fuels confused ideas about the ancient Celts among non-specialist readers.

Its literary legacy is considerable. Ted Hughes and Sylvia Plath read it closely, Alan Garner drew on it for imagery in The Owl Service, and Susan Cooper counts it among the inspirations behind The Dark Is Rising. Today it is read less as history than as a striking way of imagining poetry as the inheritor of a very old rite.

Trivia

Three titles before the final one. The book began as The Roebuck in the Thicket and was called The Three-Fold Muse before becoming The White Goddess.
Eliot's mixed praise. T. S. Eliot described the book as prodigious, monstrous, stupefying and indescribable, a phrase that mixes admiration with rejection.
Names borrowed for fiction. Lloyd Alexander took the names of some characters in The Chronicles of Prydain from this book.
