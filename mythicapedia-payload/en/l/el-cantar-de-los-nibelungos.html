The medieval epic of betrayal and vengeance

The Nibelungenlied

The Nibelungenlied is an epic poem written around 1200 in Middle High German by an anonymous author, probably from the Passau region. Drawing on Germanic heroic legend with Scandinavian echoes, it tells a story of love, treachery and revenge that became the German national epic.

The Nibelungenlied, in German Nibelungenlied, is an epic poem written around 1200 in Middle High German. Its author is anonymous, though clues point to the Passau region. Behind it lies a long oral tradition of Germanic heroic legend, some of whose roots reach back to figures and events of the fifth and sixth centuries, which spread across almost all of German-speaking Europe. Parallels from Scandinavia appear especially in the heroic lays of the Poetic Edda and in the Völsunga saga.

The poem falls into two main parts. In the first, the prince Siegfried travels to Worms to ask for the hand of Kriemhild, sister of the Burgundian king Gunther. Gunther agrees on condition that Siegfried help him win Brünhild, a warrior queen of superhuman strength who kills those who lose to her in contests. Siegfried succeeds in secret, using a cloak of invisibility, and the wedding takes place. Yet Brünhild and Kriemhild quarrel over which husband ranks higher, and the dispute ends in the revelation of a betrayal. Hagen, a vassal of Gunther, persuades the kings to remove Siegfried, and he kills him from behind during a hunt. The treasure Siegfried had won in his fight with a dragon ends up sunk in the Rhine.

The second part moves to a new setting. Thirteen years later, the widowed Kriemhild marries Etzel, king of the Huns, a figure modelled on Attila. She agrees only when she realises she can use the Huns to take revenge on her husband's killers. She invites her brothers and Hagen to her court. Although Hagen warns of danger, the Burgundians come with an army, and the journey is marked by omens that he dismisses. Fighting breaks out during their stay and gradually destroys the warriors, first the lesser knights and then the greatest. Kriemhild ends up ordering Hagen's death, since he refuses to reveal where the treasure lies. The ending is a bloodbath: Etzel himself mourns the death of so brave an enemy, and the knight Hildebrand avenges Hagen by killing Kriemhild.

The work is organised into 39 cantos, or adventures, and some 2,400 stanzas. Its text survives in about 37 manuscripts and fragments, most of them incomplete, along with a Dutch version. The versions differ noticeably, although the order of events remains fairly stable. Scholars often distinguish two main families, according to whether the text ends with the word Not (destruction, calamity) or Lied (song). The Not version is often regarded as closest to the original.

The dating rests on some evidence: around 1204–1205, Wolfram von Eschenbach mentions a cook named Rumolt in his Parzival, a figure who probably comes from the Nibelungenlied, which suggests the poem already existed by then. Scholars have linked the work to Bishop Wolfger of Passau, a patron of poets, and to his interest in the figure of Bishop Pilgrim, who appears in the text. Whether an earlier version existed is debated: some think the poem is an entirely new creation, while others suspect an older oral origin.

The poem combines two worlds. On one side it keeps the heroic ethos of the Germanic tradition, with its sense of honour, vengeance and loyalty to death. On the other it adopts the form and manners of courtly romance: love, minne, and the knight's service to his lady. Many scholars see this courtly surface as a veneer over a much older core. There are also traces of Latin literature: Kriemhild's role in the opening stanzas recalls Helen of Troy, and some episodes seem to draw on Virgil's Aeneid.

Its importance grew over time. By around 1500 the poem had fallen into obscurity, but it was rediscovered in 1755 and soon dubbed the "German Iliad". It became the German national epic, and that gave it a political weight. In the nineteenth century, and above all under the Nazis, it was used in nationalist and anti-democratic propaganda that celebrated a heroic Germanic past and loyalty unto death. Today the text is studied as a literary work, and its most visible legacy is Richard Wagner's tetralogy Der Ring des Nibelungen, though that is largely based on Norse sources. In 2009 the three main manuscripts were inscribed in UNESCO's Memory of the World Register.

Date: 1200-

Trivia

A German Iliad. After its rediscovery in 1755, the poem was nicknamed the "German Iliad", a way of stressing its status as a national epic.
A poem preserved in Switzerland. The three oldest complete manuscripts are known by the letters A, B and C, and one of them is kept at the Abbey of St Gall in Switzerland.
Film adaptations. Fritz Lang adapted the story for the cinema in 1924 under the title Die Nibelungen, and Harald Reinl did so again in 1966.
