Saxo Grammaticus's history of Denmark, between myth, legend and chronicle

Gesta Danorum

The Gesta Danorum is a Latin history of Denmark written in the Middle Ages. Its early books preserve Norse myths and legends of semi-legendary kings, and it contains the oldest known version of the story of Amleth, which later inspired Shakespeare's Hamlet. Its author, Saxo Grammaticus, describes the religion of his ancestors with a notably sceptical eye.

The Gesta Danorum, usually rendered as "Deeds of the Danes", is a patriotic history of Denmark written in Latin by the cleric Saxo, known as Saxo Grammaticus, or "Saxo the Grammarian". It dates from the High Middle Ages, most likely the late twelfth or early thirteenth century, and remains the most ambitious literary project of medieval Denmark. Accounts differ on who commissioned it: some name King Valdemar I, others point to Archbishop Absalon. Its exact date of composition is debated too, since the preface mentions events of 1208 and the work was clearly not finished before that year.

The work runs to sixteen books, written in prose with the occasional excursion into verse. It is usually divided into two halves. Books I to IX deal with Norse mythology and with the legendary or semi-legendary kings of Denmark, ending with Gorm the Old, the first Danish monarch attested in documentary records. Books X to XVI cover medieval history, with particular attention to Danish campaigns against the Slavic peoples of the Baltic.

In the opening books, the nation's origins unfold through Dan, its eponymous founder, and his descendants. Gram is the first king given any real detail, and his son Hading carries the narrative for much of the early material, a story in which Denmark is lost to the Swedes and the hero tries to win it back with the help of giants and Odin. Book III contains the earliest known version of the story of Amleth, a prince who feigns madness to protect himself from an uncle who has murdered his father and married his mother. Saxo tells it with a sharp sense of cunning and revenge, and centuries later it became the source for Shakespeare's Hamlet, though the details shift considerably between the two.

The same book also tells of Balder, son of Odin, and of Hother, a prince who loves a woman named Nanna. Saxo presents the Norse gods as men with magical powers whom people mistook for deities, a rationalising approach known as euhemerism. To account for Balder's death, the story turns on a rivalry between two suitors and on an Odin who, according to the text, was temporarily driven out of Byzantium after a crime. This rationalised way of retelling myth is one of the keys to the work: Saxo is sceptical of his ancestors' religion, yet describes it in some detail.

Books VIII and IX offer other famous episodes. Book VIII recounts the battle of Brávellir between Harald Wartooth and Ringo, and follows the hero Thorkillus on journeys to the edges of the known world, where he visits the giant Utgarthilocus. Book IX centres on Ragnar Lodbrok and his sons, who are said to have governed lands stretching from Scotland to Scythia.

Books XIV to XVI are especially valuable to historians of the West Slavic peoples of the Baltic, such as the Polabian Slavs and the Pomeranians. Book XIV contains a description of the temple on the island of Rügen, one of the few written accounts of Slavic worship there. It should be remembered that Saxo wrote as a Danish chronicler defending his country's conquests, so his view of the Slavs is partial.

The original manuscripts are lost. Only four fragments survive, all held by the Royal Danish Library in Copenhagen: the Angers Fragment, the largest and the only one thought to be in Saxo's own hand, and the Lassen, Kall-Rasmussen and Plesner fragments. The text itself has nonetheless survived, through copies and above all through the printed edition by Christiern Pedersen in 1514, which became the basis for most later translations.

The work's influence has been considerable. Beyond its role as a source for Danish history, it preserves the fullest narrative versions of many Norse legends that would otherwise be barely known. Over the centuries it was also used to build stories about Denmark's past, and its legendary heroes were reinterpreted for very different purposes. Today it is read both as a historical document and as literature, and its account of Amleth remains the oldest known version of one of the most famous stories in Western literature.

Trivia

An ancestor of Hamlet. The Amleth story in Book III is the oldest known version of this tale. Shakespeare knew it through later sources and transformed it completely.
A temple on Rügen. Book XIV describes in detail the temple on the island of Rügen, one of the few written descriptions of Slavic worship in that region.
An early edition. Christiern Pedersen published the text in 1514, and that printed edition became the basis for many later translations, since the original manuscripts were lost.
