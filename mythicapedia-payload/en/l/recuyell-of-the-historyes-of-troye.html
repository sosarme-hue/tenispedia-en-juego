The first book printed in English and its retelling of the Trojan War

Recuyell of the Historyes of Troye

Recuyell of the Historyes of Troye is William Caxton's English version of a French courtly romance about the Trojan War. Printed around 1473-1474 in Bruges, it is considered the first book printed in the English language. Its story draws on the Trojan tradition, which medieval writers reshaped for their own audiences.

The title looks strange to a modern reader, but it is easy to explain. The word recuyell comes from the French recueil and means "collection". In modern English the title would read roughly as "A Collection of the Histories of Troy". It signals that the book gathers many accounts of the besieged city rather than presenting a single, unified poem.

The original was written by Raoul Lefèvre, chaplain to Philip III, Duke of Burgundy. His French text was a courtly romance that drew on the world of the Iliad and the Aeneid but recast it in a medieval style, full of knights, tournaments and courtly love. For its readers, the Trojan War was not only an ancient episode. It was the legendary origin of several European royal houses, and the tradition linking them to Trojan survivors carried real political weight.

Caxton translated the book into English and printed it in Bruges, probably with the help of Colard Mansion and Johann Veldener. The date is given as 1473 or 1474, although for a long time it was traditionally placed around 1475. Only 18 copies survive. In 2014, one of them, sold by the Duke of Northumberland, fetched more than one million pounds, a sign of its rarity.

One of the most notable copies is a presentation copy with a specially made engraving showing Caxton handing the book to Margaret of York. It is now held by the Huntington Library in California. The scene suggests royal patronage, but scholars think it may have worked mainly as advertising rather than as a faithful picture of medieval patronage.

Caxton's translation also had a later life. It served as the source for Horestes (1567), a late Tudor morality play that takes up the story of Orestes, showing how Trojan and related myths kept moving and changing in England for generations.

Author: Raoul Lefèvre
