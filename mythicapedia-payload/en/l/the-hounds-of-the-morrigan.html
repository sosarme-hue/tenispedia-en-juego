The Irish children's novel that pits two siblings against Celtic myth

The Hounds of the Morrigan

The Hounds of the Morrigan is a children's novel by Irish writer Pat O'Shea, published in 1985 after thirteen years of work. Its young heroes, a brother and sister from Galway, find themselves swept into a struggle between gods and creatures from Celtic tradition. The book blends humour, fear and figures drawn from several Irish mythological cycles.

The story follows Pidge, a ten-year-old boy, and his sister Brigit, who is five, in Galway. In a bookshop, Pidge buys a volume called A Book of Patrick's Writing and, by accident, releases the evil serpent Olc-Glas from inside it. From that moment the two children are caught in a conflict between good, represented by the Dagda, and evil, embodied by the Morrigan.

To get through it they receive help from talking animals and other figures from Celtic myth. Their journey takes them to Tír na nÓg, the land of eternal youth in Irish tradition, a place full of creatures, trials and characters drawn from many different stories.

The novel brings together many figures from Irish lore: Queen Maeve and her husband Ailill, with their seven sons, the Maines; the druid Cathbad; the goddess Brigid; Angus Og; the triple goddess the Morrigan and her two counterparts, Bodb the Scald Crow and Macha, Queen of Phantoms; Saint Patrick; and Cúchulainn. Rather than following a single ancient source, the author mixes these traditions, so the result is a personal blend rather than a reconstruction of the myths.

Pat O'Shea spent thirteen years completing the book, and according to The Irish Times the unspoilt countryside around Lough Corrib in County Galway provided its inspiration. The tone swings between comedy and unease. Critics have pointed to darker moments, such as the maze inside the Morrigan's giant thumbprint, where the walls are lined with blisters and nothing can survive.

The reception has been mixed but warm. Dave Langford, writing for White Dwarf, found it a little chaotic in its raid on Irish myth, yet fun for young and old alike. Imogen Russell Williams, writing in The Guardian almost thirty years later, called it a bravura piece of writing. Several authors, including Sarah Rees Brennan and Robyn Young, have named it among their favourite books or cited its influence on their work.

The author was working on a sequel when she died. In an obituary, David Fickling praised the few finished chapters, in which the Morrigan arrives during a Christmas scene of carol singers, candlelit shop windows and a robin.

Trivia

A book years in the making. Pat O'Shea took thirteen years to finish the novel, a sign of the research and writing behind such an intricate story.
A giant thumbprint. One of the most unsettling episodes takes place in a maze inside the Morrigan's giant thumbprint, its walls lined with sweaty blisters.
A real landscape. The inspiration came from the landscape around Lough Corrib in County Galway, according to The Irish Times.
