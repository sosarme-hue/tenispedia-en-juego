The journey of Rama, a Sanskrit epic traditionally attributed to Valmiki

Ramayana

The Ramayana is one of the two great epics of ancient India, alongside the Mahabharata. Tradition credits the sage Valmiki with its composition, and it follows Rama, the prince of Ayodhya and, in Hindu belief, the seventh avatar of the god Vishnu. Its story of exile, loyalty, love and a struggle against the demon king Ravana has shaped literature, art and religious life across much of Asia.

The Ramayana is one of the longest epic poems in world literature. It contains roughly 24,000 verses, called shlokas, arranged in seven books or kandas and about 500 chapters, known as sargas. Within the Hindu tradition it belongs to the smriti texts, which are passed down by human memory rather than directly revealed, although its cultural and religious authority is very great. The title means the journey of Rama. The name of the hero carries two meanings in early sources: the Atharvaveda links it with darkness, while the Mahabharata associates it with pleasantness and charm.

Scholars disagree about when the poem took shape. The oldest layer, transmitted orally, is usually dated between the eighth and fifth centuries BCE, or the fifth to fourth, though the legends behind it are probably older still. Robert P. Goldman proposes 750 to 500 BCE for the oral composition, and other specialists place the written redaction around the middle of the first millennium BCE. Many scholars think the first and last books, the Bala Kanda and the Uttara Kanda, were added later, since they are missing from the oldest manuscript and differ in style and content from the rest. For centuries the poem circulated orally along two regional paths, which produced a northern and a southern recension with notable differences in wording.

The story opens with King Dasaratha of Ayodhya, who has no sons. Through a sacrifice, Rama is born to his queen Kausalya, Bharata to Kaikeyi, and the twins Lakshmana and Shatrughna to Sumitra. In Mithila, Rama wins the hand of Sita by stringing a great bow, which he then breaks. When Dasaratha wishes to crown Rama, Kaikeyi, urged on by her servant Manthara, claims two old promises from the king: that Bharata should rule and that Rama should be exiled for fourteen years. Rama accepts with calm dignity, and Sita and Lakshmana go with him into the forest. There the decisive episode occurs, when Ravana abducts Sita after a trick involving a golden deer.

The search leads Rama to an alliance with the monkey king Sugriva and with Hanuman, who leaps across the sea to Lanka, allows himself to be captured, sets the city alight with his blazing tail and returns with news of Sita. After a vast battle, Rama defeats Ravana with the arrow of Brahma. Yet when he recovers Sita, he asks her to pass a test by fire to answer his subjects' doubts, and she emerges unharmed through the intervention of the god Agni. They return to Ayodhya, where Rama is crowned. Later, new doubts about Sita's purity lead Rama to send her away from the court, and she ends her life by returning to the earth that had received her.

The work strongly shaped later Sanskrit poetry, especially by establishing the shloka metre, which tradition says arose when Valmiki, moved by the death of a bird, composed his first verse. Its moral emphasis falls on virtue, duty, family loyalty and the ideal of a just ruler, captured in the image of Rama-rajya, the utopian kingdom ruled by Rama. It later inspired works such as Tulsidas's poem in Hindi and Kambar's Tamil version, as well as Buddhist and Jain adaptations.

The epic also spread across Southeast Asia, carried by kingdoms with Hindu-influenced courts, such as the Khmer, Champa and Sri Vijaya. Its episodes appear in literature, theatre and temple reliefs in Indonesia, Thailand, Cambodia, Laos, Malaysia, Burma, Vietnam and the Philippines. Today the poem is still read as a religious, moral and literary text, and also as a subject of debate. Modern readers ask about the role of Sita and about the way women and certain social groups are portrayed, particularly in the later books, whose place within the whole remains a matter of scholarly discussion.

Author: Valmiki

Trivia

Born from a slain bird. According to tradition, Valmiki created the shloka metre after witnessing the death of two birds. That verse form became the basis of Sanskrit epic poetry.
A monkey who sets a city alight. Captured by Ravana's men, Hanuman escapes with his burning tail across the rooftops and ends up setting much of Lanka on fire.
A bow no one could string. In Mithila, no suitor can even lift the bow. Rama not only strings it but breaks it, winning Sita's hand.
Traces in Southeast Asia. Its episodes appear in temples and theatre in Indonesia, Thailand and Cambodia, where the poem was adapted to local traditions over centuries.
