The Icelandic tale of Sigurd, Sigmund and the cursed ring

The Völsunga Saga

The Völsunga saga is a late thirteenth-century prose saga in Old Norse that follows several generations of the Völsung clan. Its centre is Sigurd, who kills the dragon Fáfnir and takes a cursed treasure. It is one of the most famous legendary sagas and a key link between Norse myth and the Germanic heroic tradition.

The Völsunga saga is a prose narrative written in Old Norse, most likely in Iceland, in the late thirteenth century. The oldest surviving manuscript, now held by the Royal Library of Denmark, dates to around 1400 and joins the saga to the Ragnars saga loðbrókar. Scholars class it among the fornaldarsögur, or 'sagas of ancient times', tales in which heroic memory, the supernatural and myth freely mix.

The material is much older than the text. It draws on traditional poems, several of them preserved in the Poetic Edda, and its story echoes real events in central Europe during the Migration Period, above all the destruction of the Burgundian kingdom by the Huns in the fifth century. The earliest known picture of this tradition is the Ramsund carving in Sweden.

The saga opens with Sigi, the ancestor of the line, and his quarrel with Skaði. According to the tale, Sigi was a son of Odin. Jealous of a thrall named Breði, who outdid him at hunting, he kills him and hides the body in a snowdrift. Skaði finds the corpse and names the drift after Breði, and in time every large snowdrift came to bear that name. Sigi is cast out and, after much wandering, takes over Hunaland. His brothers-in-law later overthrow him, but his son Rerir avenges him and claims the throne, so the saga builds a long lineage of kings and conquerors.

The longest episode belongs to Völsung, Sigi's grandson, who has ten sons and a daughter, the twins Sigmund and Signý. His great hall is built around a tree called Barnstokkr. At Signý's wedding, a hooded, one-eyed stranger drives a sword into the trunk and promises it to whoever can pull it out. No one succeeds until Sigmund does. Tradition identifies the stranger with Odin, because of his hood and missing eye, although the text never says so outright.

Signý marries Siggeir, king of Gautland, against her wishes, and he later turns on his father-in-law. After a grim battle Völsung dies, and Siggeir traps Signý's brothers in the forest. Sigmund survives through a scheme she arranges. The saga then recounts a revenge involving a she-wolf, a völva and an incestuous union that produces Sinfjǫtli. It ends with Siggeir's death in a fire, a trap in which Signý chose to die beside her husband.

Sigmund rules with success, but his story ends in betrayal and death within his own family. After Sinfjǫtli is poisoned by Sigmund's wife Borghild, Sigmund later dies in battle in old age. Before he falls, he gives his wife Hjördís the shards of his sword, which are to be reforged for the son she is carrying. That son is Sigurd, who grows up at the court of King Hjalprek of Denmark under the care of the smith Reginn.

Here the most famous episode begins. Reginn tells Sigurd about his brothers: Ótr, who could take the shape of an otter; Fáfnir, large and fierce; and Reginn himself, skilled in ironwork. After Ótr is killed by Odin, Loki and Hœnir, their father demands compensation in gold. The ransom is paid with the treasure of the dwarf Andvari, but Andvari curses the ring Andvaranaut, which will bring death to whoever owns it. Fáfnir kills his father, keeps the hoard and turns into a dragon. Reginn forges for Sigurd the sword Gram, which splits an anvil to its base, and urges him to slay the beast.

The saga is closely tied to the Germanic heroic tradition. Its parallels with the Nibelungenlied, a verse epic in Middle High German, lead scholars to see a more archaic form of the same material, though the Icelandic work keeps distinctly Norse elements of its own. Today it is read as one of the great works of heroic mythology and as a window onto how these stories travelled before they were written down.

Date: 1250-

Trivia

A late manuscript. The only surviving medieval manuscript is later than the original text. It dates to about 1400 and is held by the Royal Library of Denmark.
A Swedish carving. The earliest known pictorial representation of this tradition is the Ramsund carving in Sweden, which predates the Icelandic text.
A name for a snowdrift. According to the saga, Skaði named the snowdrift where Breði's body lay after him, and the name spread to any large snowdrift.
