The first major guide to Finnish mythology

Mythologia Fennica

Mythologia Fennica is a book written in Swedish by Kristfrid Ganander, a Finnish priest, gathering hundreds of entries on Finnish mythology, folk poetry and belief. Completed in 1789, it was not published until four years later, and its influence reached Elias Lönnrot and the Kalevala.

Mythologia Fennica is a book on Finnish mythology written in Swedish by Kristfrid Ganander, a priest of Finnish birth. The work was completed in 1789, but it did not appear in print until four years later, after a review by Henrik Gabriel Porthan, often regarded as the father of Finnish historical research.

Ganander conceived it as an appendix to a Swedish-Finnish dictionary that he left unfinished. Porthan encouraged him and offered his assistance, and the project gradually took on a life of its own, becoming a work in its own right rather than a supplement to the dictionary it was meant to accompany.

The book contains 430 entries arranged alphabetically, from "AARNI" to "YRJÄNÄ". Under that simple order sit very different kinds of material: myths and figures from Finnish tradition, folk poetry, spells, elements of Sámi mythology and references to the Norse gods. The reader finds a mosaic of older beliefs, gathered and explained by an author working at the end of the eighteenth century.

Its influence was considerable. Elias Lönnrot, the compiler of the Kalevala, knew and used the work, and for this reason Ganander has often been described as a kind of "Lönnrot before Lönnrot". That connection places the book near the origins of a growing interest in folk poetry and popular myth, which decades later would take shape in the great Finnish epic.

The book also had a long publishing life. In 1821 the Estonian poet Kristjan Jaak Peterson produced a German translation, and over the years it was reprinted many times, especially towards the end of the twentieth century. Today it is read as a valuable witness to how Finnish tradition was understood in the late eighteenth century, together with the limits and viewpoint of its author.

Trivia

A dictionary that never became one. The work was born as an appendix to a Swedish-Finnish dictionary that Ganander never finished. The book ended up carrying more weight than that original project.
An Estonian translator. The German translation of 1821 was made by Kristjan Jaak Peterson, an Estonian poet, showing that interest in Finnish tradition crossed linguistic and national borders.
Entries in alphabetical order. The 430 entries follow the alphabet, from "AARNI" to "YRJÄNÄ", so the book works more as a reference collection than as a continuous narrative.
