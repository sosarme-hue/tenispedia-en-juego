The Akkadian epic of the flood and the creation of humankind

Atra-Hasis

Atra-Hasis is an Akkadian epic from the 18th century BC that tells how the gods created humans to do their labour and how, once humanity multiplied, a flood nearly wiped it out. It takes its name from its protagonist, the priest Atra-Hasis, and it is one of the later versions of the flood myth in Mesopotamia.

The epic of Atra-Hasis survives in several versions written on clay tablets. Its title comes from one of its protagonists, the priest Atra-Hasis, whose name means “exceedingly wise”. The story has roots in Sumerian tradition and unfolds in four main movements: the gods who organised Mesopotamia's farming, a conflict among them, the creation of a first human couple to take over their labour, and finally a flood meant to destroy creatures the gods considered imperfect.

Among the many flood stories of Mesopotamia, this is one of the latest. Its oldest known copies date from the Ur III period, and the tale was later passed on in Akkadian, Babylonian, biblical and Qur’anic accounts. Some researchers have suggested that the relatively rapid rise of sea levels at the end of the last Ice Age may lie behind the story, since such a change would have made a strong impression on the first settled communities.

According to the myth, the gods were divided into related groups. The Anunnaki lived in the heights, the Igigi laboured on the land and dug the great canals, and Enlil, Lord Wind, led the council. After forty years of toil, the Igigi rebelled and surrounded Enlil's dwelling. To avoid a bloody battle, Enlil turned to Mami, who, with Enki's help, shaped humans from clay mixed with the blood of a slain god and assigned them the hard work.

The creation of the woman appears in a damaged section of the text, but the surviving passages show that the first couple was brought together at Mami's command. Other traditions contain similar motifs. In Hesiod, Pandora is a gift that becomes a source of conflict among the gods, and in the Gilgamesh epic the seduction of Enkidu plays a comparable role. In Genesis, by contrast, creation comes from a single God, a notable difference from the plural divine world of Atra-Hasis.

Over the centuries, humans multiplied so much that their noise disturbed the gods. Enlil sent plagues and droughts, and finally a flood. A single couple survived thanks to an act of sabotage by one of the lower gods. In the end, the quarrelling gods agreed on a method to limit the birth rate of their creatures.

The story became known largely through George Smith, who published it in the nineteenth century as The Chaldean Account of Genesis. Heinrich Zimmern corrected the hero's name in 1899, and in 1965 Wilfred G. Lambert and Alan Millard published many new texts, including the most complete Old Babylonian copy, which made possible the first nearly complete English translation.

Trivia

A revealing name. Atra-Hasis means “exceedingly wise”, a title the tradition gives to the priest who lends his name to the epic.
