Roland Barthes and the myths of modern life

Mythologies

First published in 1957, this book gathers essays in which Roland Barthes examines the myths that French society of the 1950s built around everyday objects, spectacles and images. Barthes shows that a modern myth is not an ancient story but an added meaning that appears natural. A second, theoretical part explains how these myths work.

Mythologies is a 1957 book by Roland Barthes made up of short essays written between 1954 and 1956, most of them first published in the French literary review Les Lettres nouvelles. Barthes looks at how contemporary social value systems, especially those of the French bourgeoisie, produce modern myths: stories that present human-made ideas as if they were natural.

The book has two parts. The first, also called Mythologies, collects essays on very different cultural phenomena, from Einstein's brain to soap powders, from red wine to professional wrestling and magazine photography. What links them is their status as myths, since each has been given a second-order meaning. The second part, Myth Today, asks what a myth is in the present and treats it as a kind of speech closely connected to political ideology.

In the essay on wine, Barthes describes how the drink became a French national emblem and a symbol of social equality associated with the working class. He notes that its harmful effects on health get little attention, while it is presented as life-giving and refreshing. In the case of wrestling, he argues that the audience is not interested in who wins or in admiring athletic skill, but in a staged drama of good and evil, suffering and justice. The wrestlers exaggerate stereotypes such as the traitor or the vain man, and the crowd expects to see them punished, in a theatrical version of society's idea of justice.

To explain the mechanism, Barthes builds on Ferdinand de Saussure's distinction between signifier and signified. In myth, a complete sign becomes a new signifier to which a social meaning is attached. This meaning is not arbitrary: according to Barthes, myths reinforce ideas that serve the ruling classes and the media. One example is a Paris Match cover showing a young Black soldier in French uniform saluting, an image that suggests imperial greatness rather than the life of one particular man.

Barthes also identifies recurring features of bourgeois myths: they present ideas as natural, erase the history behind them and simplify reality. This is why the book remains a useful guide to how mass culture passes on beliefs without people noticing that they are beliefs.

Trivia

A late complete edition. The first English edition, from 1972, was abridged and left out twenty-five of the fifty-three original essays. A complete edition with a new translation appeared in 2012.
An echo of McLuhan. Barthes's analyses share similarities with Marshall McLuhan's The Mechanical Bride (1951), which also examined advertisements and magazine articles of mass culture critically.
