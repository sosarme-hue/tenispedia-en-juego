Ovid's poem about bodies that change shape

Metamorphoses

Metamorphoses is a Latin poem in fifteen books that traces the history of the world from its creation to the transformation into a star of the soul of Julius Caesar. It gathers more than two hundred and fifty mythological tales held together by a single thread: the change of people, gods and objects into new forms. From the Middle Ages to the present day, it has been a constant source for literature, art and music.

Ovid completed the poem in 8 CE, shortly before the turn of the era. The title comes from the Greek word for 'transformation', and the work presents itself as a continuous journey from primordial chaos to the age of the emperor Augustus. Its tone blends epic, didactic poetry and the short narrative. Written in dactylic hexameter, it is built as a chain of nested stories, where one tale often leads to the next through a small detail of the previous one.

The work is hard to classify. It shares features with epic, through its length and ambition, but its structure is closer to a mosaic of episodes than to a single heroic story. Ovid drew on a tradition of poems about metamorphosis, yet he departed from his models in almost every respect: in tone, in the choice of myths and in the way he linked them. Many of the stories come from earlier authors such as Hesiod and Homer, though in some cases the poem is our only surviving source.

What holds the episodes together is transformation. Ovid announces it in the opening lines, where he says he intends to speak of bodies changed into new forms. The changes take many kinds. People become inanimate objects, such as a river, constellations, like Ariadne's crown, or animals, like the partridge that gives its name to Perdix. There are also reverse changes, in which animals or mushrooms become human, and others that alter sex or even colour. Often the transformation grows out of violence: a victim, pursued or punished by a god, is fixed forever in the landscape, so that nature keeps the memory of suffering. The tension between hunter and hunted returns again and again across the poem.

Ovid also plays with the idea of change itself. Some metamorphoses are told with a dry humour or a absurdity that makes the reader smile, until he slowly realises the narrator is pulling his leg. At other moments the poet questions what it really means to be transformed. Disguise and illusion are constant tools, forming part of a style in which deception and appearance become central themes.

The poem's influence on European culture has been enormous. In the Middle Ages it was among the most widely read classical texts, and some scholars argue it played a decisive part in the rise of the French, English and Italian literatures. Geoffrey Chaucer adapted several of its stories in The Canterbury Tales, and William Shakespeare drew on the tale of Pyramus and Thisbe for Romeo and Juliet, as well as on the myth of Venus and Adonis. John Milton used episodes from the poem in Paradise Lost, and Dante and Boccaccio also knew it well.

In the visual arts, the work was the great source of mythological subjects during the Renaissance and Baroque. Titian painted several scenes inspired by it, including Diana and Actaeon, Diana and Callisto and The Death of Actaeon, while Bernini sculpted Apollo and Daphne and Pieter Brueghel painted Landscape with the Fall of Icarus. The adjective 'Ovidian' came to mean mythological, although many of the myths depicted do not appear in the poem. Art theory of the period also drew on it, especially the myths of Pygmalion and Narcissus, read as reflections on the role of the artist.

After a period of waning interest following the Renaissance, the work was appreciated again toward the end of the twentieth century. Ted Hughes retold twenty-four passages in his Tales from Ovid, published in 1997, and Mary Zimmerman's stage adaptation premiered in 1998. In music, Benjamin Britten wrote Six Metamorphoses after Ovid for solo oboe, and the progressive rock group Genesis devoted a song to the myth of Salmacis and Hermaphroditus. Film and television have also turned to its stories, and in the twenty-first century the poem remains one of the main routes by which Greek and Roman myths reach modern readers.
