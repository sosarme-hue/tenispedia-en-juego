Pausanias's guide to ancient Greece

Description of Greece

Description of Greece is a travel book written by Pausanias in the second century AD. Across ten books it surveys much of mainland Greece, focusing on sanctuaries, monuments and the myths that explain each place. It is the only surviving work by its author and one of the most valuable sources on Greek religion and art.

The work is known in Greek as Ἑλλάδος περιήγησις, a term closer to a guided tour of Greece than to a description in the modern sense. Pausanias organised it into ten books that follow a single itinerary, starting at Cape Sunium in Attica and ending in the region of Locris. Along the way he passes through Corinth, Laconia, Messenia, Elis, Achaea, Arcadia, Boeotia and Phocis. Books V and VI concentrate on Olympia, with its temples and votive offerings.

Each book follows a loose pattern. Pausanias usually opens with the history and myths of the region, then moves from the border towards the main city, where he describes the agora, the acropolis and the principal streets. He then leaves by other roads and visits the settlements and notable sites. Book I, on Attica, is the exception, with no historical introduction and no clear topographical order. Some scholars suspect it was published before the rest, since later books correct points made there.

The work blends two kinds of material. On one hand are things he saw for himself, such as buildings, statues, works of art and, at times, landscapes or natural products. On the other are accounts he only passed on, drawn from oral or written sources, including myths, traditions and historical episodes. Modern scholars have praised the accuracy of what he witnessed, while noting mistakes in some of his historical narratives. For a long time he was judged unreliable, but twentieth-century excavations confirmed many of his descriptions.

Pausanias was far more drawn to the old than the new, and to the sacred rather than the secular. He hardly mentions Rome or the monuments built under Roman rule, even those commissioned by emperors such as Hadrian. He also leaves out Thessaly, Epirus and Acarnania, which lay outside the Roman province of Achaea. His Greece is a deliberately antique one, in which ruined cities matter as much as living ones, and in which myth and local identity explain every site.

The book enjoyed little circulation. It is barely cited in antiquity and survived the Middle Ages in a single manuscript, now lost. It was the Italian Renaissance that revived interest, and the first printed edition appeared in Venice in 1516, produced by the Aldine Press and edited by Marcus Musurus. Since then it has been translated many times and remains essential reading for historians of religion and art.

Trivia

A meticulous traveller. Pausanias describes the sanctuary of Olympia, home of the Olympic Games, in great detail, and devotes two full books to the region of Elis.
A surprising ending. The work closes with a dream in which a woman, thought by some to be the poet Anyte of Tegea, is told to share the text with a wider Greek audience. Scholars debate whether this ending was intentional.
A near-lost copy. The surviving manuscripts all descend from a single copy that reached Florence around 1418 and disappeared after 1500.
