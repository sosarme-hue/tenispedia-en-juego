Two small sculptures attributed to El Greco

Epimetheus and Pandora

These are two small polychrome wooden figures, dated between 1600 and 1610 and held in the Prado Museum in Madrid, showing Epimetheus and Pandora just before the opening of the box. For a long time they were taken to be Adam and Eve, until a study linked them to the Greek myth. They are now attributed to El Greco, though with some caution.

For a long time, the two figures in the Prado were catalogued as Adam and Eve. The explanation seemed reasonable: secular nudes were rare in Spain at the time, and full nudity was uncommon even in religious art, although partial male nudity was not censored. According to the historian Gregorio Marañón, several works by El Greco show an exaggerated and unnecessary nakedness, and his late painting View of Toledo is not the only one; Vision of the Apocalypse also includes several full nudes, both male and female.

The correct reading is due to Xavier de Salas, who identified the vessel held by the male figure as a pyxis, a lidded container used in antiquity. Here it would be Pandora's box. Salas reached this conclusion after consulting Pandora's Box by Dora and Erwin Panofsky, a study of how the myth evolved in art.

The story the sculptures depict is the myth of Epimetheus and Pandora, as told by Hesiod in his Theogony and Works and Days. According to that tradition, Pandora, the first woman created at the command of Zeus, came to the home of Epimetheus, brother of Prometheus, who had accepted a gift from the gods without foreseeing its consequences. The box held the evils that would spread among humankind. The two figures seem to converse in the moment before it is opened, and notably, here Epimetheus holds the box rather than Pandora, as is usually shown.

The statuettes measure 44 cm (Epimetheus) and 43 cm (Pandora). Both are nude, standing, with elongated bodies in proportion to their heads. Epimetheus has his head turned toward his companion, almost entirely covered by a yellow cap with a white border; Pandora wears her hair gathered up with a crimson diadem and turns her face to the right. Her polychrome is lighter than his.

The attribution to El Greco rests on the resemblance of these figures to his recognised works. Epimetheus's twisted, supple body recalls the risen Christ on the tabernacle of the Hospital de Tavera, the male nudes of the Laocoön, and some figures in the Vision of the Apocalypse. Pandora is harder to link to him, since he painted few female nudes, though her face and pose recall the Virgin in the Adoration of the Shepherds in Valencia.

As for their history, the pieces came from the Madrid art market. They were acquired in 1945 by Joaquín Pérez del Pulgar y Campos, 5th Count of Las Infantas, and donated to the Prado in 1962 by his widow, Dolores Andrada y Pérez de Herrasti.

Date: 1605-

Trivia

A pyxis, not a box. The Greek word pyxis designated a lidded container, usually of wood or ivory. That is why identifying the vessel as Pandora's box required a detailed iconographic reading.
A myth with different versions. In Hesiod, Pandora opens a jar containing evils; the image of a box became popular later, in subsequent tradition and Renaissance art.
