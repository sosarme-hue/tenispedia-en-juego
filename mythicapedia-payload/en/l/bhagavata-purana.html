A Purana of devotion to Krishna and the story of the cosmos

Bhagavata Purana

The Bhagavata Purana is one of Hinduism's best-known Puranas and a central text of Vaishnavism. Divided into twelve cantos, it is traditionally attributed to Vyasa. Its main theme is bhakti, or loving devotion, to Krishna, whom it presents as the source of all the gods.

The Bhagavata Purana, known in Sanskrit as Bhāgavata-purāṇa and also as the Srimad Bhagavatam, is one of the most popular Puranas in Hinduism. The Puranas are collections of ancient stories that mix myths, royal and divine genealogies, descriptions of the cosmos and religious teaching. Composed in Sanskrit, this work is traditionally attributed to Vyasa, the sage credited with arranging the Vedas. Its central message is bhakti, a devoted and affectionate love for Krishna, whom the text portrays as the supreme personality of God, with Vishnu understood as one of his manifestations.

The book has twelve cantos, called skandhas, containing 335 chapters and around 18,000 verses. The tenth canto, with some 4,000 verses, is the most read and studied part. The narrative is built like a set of nested boxes rather than a straight line, through overlapping dialogues. According to the text, the story is recited by Suka, Vyasa's son, to King Parikshit. The frame is dramatic: Parikshit, a grandson of the Pandavas, has been cursed by the son of a sage to die from a snakebite within seven days. In the time left to him, he chooses to hear teachings about the divine rather than fight his fate.

The early cantos gather a wide range of material. There are stories of Vishnu's devotees, such as Dhruva, a child who, in the tradition, wins liberation through severe austerities, and accounts of Vishnu's avatars. Among them is Varaha, the boar who lifts the Earth from the depths of the cosmic ocean and defeats the demon Hiranyaksa, and Kapila, who expounds the samkhya philosophy to his mother. There are also curses, royal genealogies, and descriptions of creation and of cyclical time. Like other Puranas, the work covers subjects ranging from cosmology and geography to music, dance and yoga.

The tenth canto is the best known. It tells of Krishna's birth, his childhood among the cowherds of Vrindavan, and his play with the gopis, the cowherd women who adore him. These episodes have become the basis of much popular devotion to Krishna and have inspired dance, theatre, painting and music across India. The text insists that Krishna is not merely an avatar but the original divine personality, so that other gods are understood as expressions of him.

Vaishnava tradition often points out that the Bhagavata Purana goes beyond the usual scope of a Purana. According to the Matsya Purana, a Purana should treat at least five topics, known as pancha-lakshana. The Bhagavata speaks of ten: the creation of the universe, secondary creation, the planetary systems, the protection of the Lord, the creative impulses, the changes of the Manus, the science of God, the return to the divine home, liberation, and the highest goal. Commentators explain that these subjects are spread across all twelve cantos.

When the text was composed remains debated. Puranas were transmitted orally for centuries and reworked by successive narrators, so a single date is hard to fix. The scholarly consensus places the basic form of the work between the 5th and 10th centuries CE. Some researchers argue that it had nearly reached its final form in the Gupta period. Others favour southern India, pointing to possible references to the Alvar saints and to the text's emphasis on bhakti. A significant objection is that southern Vaishnava theologians such as Yamuna and Ramanuja do not cite it in their commentaries, a puzzle that scholars still try to explain.

The influence of the Bhagavata Purana has been immense. Vaishnava schools treat it as a key reference, and in Bengal the Gaudiya Vaishnavas regard it as a natural commentary on the Vedanta Sutras. It has been interpreted by theologians such as Madhva, Vallabha and Sridhara Swami, and the movement of Chaitanya made it a foundation of its devotion. It was also one of the first Puranas translated into a European language: a French version of a Tamil rendering appeared in 1788 and introduced many Europeans to Hindu culture during the colonial era.

Trivia

A much-translated Purana. A French translation of a Tamil version appeared in 1788, and it is considered the first Purana to be rendered into a European language. It reached many European readers during the colonial era.
The oldest surviving manuscript. According to scholars, the oldest surviving manuscript dates to around 1124-25 and is held at Sampurnananda Sanskrit University in Varanasi.
Ten subjects instead of five. While lesser Puranas cover five topics, the Bhagavata lists ten, spread across its twelve cantos. This breadth is seen as one of its distinctive features.
