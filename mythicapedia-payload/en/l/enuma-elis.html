The Babylonian creation poem and the rise of Marduk

Enūma Eliš

The Enūma Eliš is a Babylonian poem that tells how the universe came into being and how the god Marduk defeated the goddess Tiamat. Its title, meaning «When on High», comes from its opening words. It survives on seven clay tablets written in Akkadian cuneiform and is one of the fullest sources on Mesopotamian cosmology.

The Enūma Eliš is a poem written in Akkadian, the language of ancient Mesopotamia. Its title comes from its opening words, usually rendered as «When on High». It survives on seven clay tablets, each holding between 115 and 170 lines of cuneiform, and runs to roughly a thousand lines in total. It does not rhyme and has no fixed meter. Instead it works in couplets, where the second line sharpens the first through contrast, a device similar to the parallelism found in biblical poetry.

According to the myth, there was once no heaven and no earth, only the primordial waters: Apsû, the sweet water, and Tiamat, the salt water. From their union came the first gods, among them Anshar, Kishar, Anu and Ea. The noise of this new generation irritated Apsû, who resolved to destroy it with the help of his counsellor Mummu. Ea struck first with a spell that put Apsû to sleep, killed him, and imprisoned Mummu. He then built his own palace over Apsû's remains, and it was there that Marduk was born.

Tiamat, enraged at her consort's death, gathered an army of eleven monsters and gave her son Kingu the tablets of destiny, granting him authority over all the gods. Ea and Anu tried to stop her, but both failed. Marduk then offered to face her, on condition that he be recognised as supreme. Once proclaimed king, he armed himself with a bow, a net, a club and the winds. In battle he trapped Tiamat in his net, sent the winds into her body until her belly swelled, and killed her with an arrow.

After his victory, Marduk split Tiamat's corpse like a shell: one half became the sky and the other the earth. He set the dwellings of the gods in place, created the stars, the moon and the sun, and, according to the text, the rivers Tigris and Euphrates flowed from the goddess's eyes. From the rest of her body he shaped mountains and the inhabited world. Later, Ea made human beings from Kingu's blood mixed with clay, so that they would serve the gods and let them rest.

The gods then built Babylon, with the Esagila temple and the Etemenanki ziggurat, a copy of Apsû's heavenly sanctuary. After a feast, they gave Marduk the supreme throne and recited his fifty names in a long litany that closes the poem.

Scholars still debate when it was written. The best-known copy comes from the library of Ashurbanipal at Nineveh, dating to the seventh century BCE, but many specialists place its composition in the Old Babylonian period or at the end of the second millennium. Marduk's rise to kingship of the gods is linked to the Second Dynasty of Isin and to the return of his statue from Elam. Some later Assyrian versions replace Marduk with Ashur.

The poem reached modern readers in fragments. Austen Henry Layard recovered pieces in 1849, and George Smith published a version in 1876. Centuries earlier, the Babylonian priest Berossus had written in Greek in the third century BCE about a similar cosmogony, with a fish-like sage who taught civilisation and with a female figure divided to form heaven and earth. Scholars continue to discuss how closely these accounts are related. Today the Enūma Eliš remains a key text for understanding Mesopotamian religion and its legacy.

Trivia

A find at Nineveh. Austen Henry Layard recovered fragments of the poem in 1849 among the ruins of Ashurbanipal's library at Nineveh, near modern Mosul.
A Babylonian historian. Berossus, a priest of Bel-Marduk in the third century BCE, wrote a history of Babylonia in Greek that includes a creation account similar to the poem.
Fifty names. Marduk receives fifty names in the poem. In the god list An = Anum, the number fifty was traditionally assigned to Enlil, showing how prestige passed between gods.
