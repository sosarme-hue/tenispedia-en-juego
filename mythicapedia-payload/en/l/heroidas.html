Ovid's letters of heroines

Heroides

The Heroides is a collection of verse letters by Ovid, written in elegiac couplets and voiced by women from myth and literature who lament the absence, neglect or abandonment of their lovers. Composed before the poet's exile, they combine Greek mythic tradition with a new emotional sensitivity. Ovid himself claimed to have invented the form, and its influence reached as far as the European novel.

The Heroides, or Epistulae heroidum (Letters of Heroines), is a group of elegiac poems that Ovid wrote before his exile. It contains twenty-one letters: fifteen single epistles from women to absent lovers, followed by six double ones in which heroes and heroines answer each other. The characters come from several mythical cycles, mainly the Homeric poems, Greek tragedy, Greek lyric and the Aeneid, and one of them, the poet Sappho, was a real person.

Each letter starts from a wound in love: waiting, betrayal, distance or loss. Penelope writes to Odysseus after years of waiting in Ithaca, weaving and unweaving her loom while suitors surround her. Dido reproaches Aeneas for sailing towards Italy, Ariadne laments her abandonment on Naxos by Theseus, and Medea protests against Jason's wedding. Other letters are darker: Hermione begs Orestes to rescue her from a forced marriage, and Canace writes before taking her own life after the tragic fate of her child.

What stands out is how Ovid reshapes these figures. In Penelope's letter, the loyalty that in the Odyssey seems rooted mostly in honour becomes a refined, intimate feeling, voiced with reflective tenderness. This focus on inner emotion is one reason the collection is regarded as innovative.

Ovid claims in the Art of Love that he invented this kind of poem, though scholars still debate how much he owed to the poet Propertius. The title is also uncertain. Ovid himself calls them Epistulae, while the grammarian Priscian uses Heroides, and from manuscript evidence scholars infer that the original title was Epistulae heroidum.

The authorship of some letters has been questioned. Some manuscripts omit the letter of Sappho, yet Ovid mentions a letter by Sappho in his Amores, which supports its authenticity. The last six double letters have also raised doubts, usually explained by the fact that they were written years after the first fifteen. Most scholars now accept them as Ovid's work.

For a long time the Heroides were little admired by critics, until late in the twentieth century opinion shifted. Their influence is wide: Baudri of Bourgueil popularised them in the eleventh century, Héloïse used them as models in her letters to Abelard, and in the Renaissance they inspired translations and new letters in the heroidean manner. Some critics see in Helen's reply to Paris an early form of the psychological novel.

Author: Ovid

Trivia

A letter from Sappho. The fifteenth letter is voiced by Sappho, who writes to Phaon after he has left her. Some manuscripts omit it, although Ovid mentions a letter by Sappho in his Amores.
A genre Ovid claimed. In the third book of the Art of Love, Ovid boasts of having created this kind of verse letter, although scholars debate how much he owed to Propertius.
From the loom to the novel. The critic W. M. Spackman argued that Helen's letter to Paris contains in embryo the psychological novel, from La Princesse de Clèves to Proust.
