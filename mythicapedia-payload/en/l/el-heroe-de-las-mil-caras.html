The mythic journey that runs through the world's cultures

The Hero with a Thousand Faces

In 1949 Joseph Campbell proposed that stories from very different cultures share a common underlying structure, which he called the monomyth or the hero's adventure. His book analyzes that pattern and its stages, and it continues to shape film, literature and other media.

In 1949 American mythologist, writer and professor Joseph Campbell published The Hero with a Thousand Faces, a work of comparative mythology arguing that stories from very different cultures often share an underlying structure. After comparing traditions from around the world, Campbell described what he called the monomyth, or the hero's adventure, a narrative pattern he found across many myths.

Campbell divided that journey into three broad phases: Departure, sometimes called Separation, Initiation, and Return. The hero leaves the ordinary world after a call to adventure and, often with a mentor's help, crosses a guarded threshold into a supernatural realm where familiar laws no longer apply. There he faces a series of trials, sometimes aided by allies, and confronts the ordeal, the greatest challenge of the journey. Having met it, he receives a boon. The Return brings a symbolic death and rebirth, further trials on the road home, and the application of the boon to the ordinary world. Campbell acknowledged that myths do not always follow the same sequence or include every stage.

To build his model, Campbell drew on early twentieth-century thinkers. From Freud he took the Oedipus complex, from Jung archetypal figures and the collective unconscious, and from Arnold van Gennep his study of rites of passage, which Campbell recast as his three phases. He borrowed the word "monomyth" from James Joyce's Finnegans Wake, and he was a noted Joyce scholar. His book compares stories such as those of Osiris, Prometheus, the Buddha, Moses, Muhammad and Jesus, pointing out their parallels.

The idea has had a lasting effect on popular culture. George Lucas acknowledged that he deliberately drew on Campbell's theory when making the Star Wars films, and the two discussed it in the conversations with Bill Moyers broadcast in 1988 as The Power of Myth. Hollywood producer Christopher Vogler wrote a memo for Disney Studios presenting the book as a guide for screenwriters, a memo linked to films such as Beauty and the Beast and The Lion King. Novelist Richard Adams used extracts from the book as chapter epigraphs in Watership Down.

The book has passed through several publishers and revisions. Campbell revised it in 1968, and after the release of Star Wars, some reprints showed Mark Hamill as Luke Skywalker on the cover. A 2008 edition appeared as part of the Collected Works of Joseph Campbell. It has been translated into more than twenty languages and has sold over a million copies. Not every reader enjoys its scholarly tone; some find it dry, yet it remains a standard reference for studying myth.
