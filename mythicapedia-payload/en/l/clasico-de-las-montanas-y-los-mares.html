Classic of Mountains and Seas

The Shan Hai Jing is a Chinese text that combines descriptions of mountains, rivers and distant lands with myths and strange creatures. Its present form took shape in the early Han dynasty, although its earliest versions date back to the 4th century BCE. For centuries it was read as a geography book, and today it is one of the most important sources for the study of Chinese mythology.

The Shan Hai Jing, or Classic of Mountains and Seas, is one of the most unusual texts in Chinese literature. It is a collection of descriptions of mountains, rivers, remote regions and extraordinary beings, in which geographical detail is woven together with mythic stories. Its present form took shape in the early Han dynasty, although the first versions go back to the 4th century BCE. It is divided into eighteen chapters and describes over 550 mountains and 300 channels of water.

For centuries it was attributed to legendary figures such as Yu the Great and Boyi. From the Tang dynasty onward, however, many scholars doubted this and placed the work in the Warring States period, possibly the product of several hands. Some modern researchers argue that it grew out of different materials, gathered and expanded over time. Names such as Zou Yan and Sui Chaozi have been proposed, but there is no consensus. The earliest mention of the book appears in Sima Qian's Records of the Grand Historian, in the chapter on Dayuan. Guo Pu, a scholar of the Western Jin dynasty, was not its author. He wrote commentaries that survive today and shaped how generations read it.

The book is not a continuous narrative. Each chapter describes places according to the cardinal directions: mountains, regions beyond the seas, regions within the seas, and wild lands. The entries usually detail medicinal plants, animals and features of the terrain. Some are quite mundane, while others are clearly fantastic. The formula repeats from chapter to chapter, which can make the reading monotonous, yet short myths, rarely longer than a paragraph, appear among the lists.

One of the best-known stories is that of Yu the Great, who according to tradition spent years trying to control the flood. He appears in the last chapter, and his portrait is far more fanciful than in the Book of Documents. Other figures familiar in Chinese culture, such as Kua Fu, Nüwa, Houyi and the Yellow Emperor, also appear in its pages. Many of the animals described are birds, quadrupeds, dragons and serpents with magical powers. Scholars link them to ancient totemic beliefs. In the chapter known as the Regions Beyond the Seas, the state of Wuxian is associated with a serpent, which suggests a symbolic bond between a people and an animal.

Chinese scholars long regarded it as a geography book and listed it that way in official catalogues. In the Ming and Qing periods it came to be seen more as fiction, and in the twentieth century it was studied through anthropology and folklore. Today it is read as a source for mythology, religion, zoology, medicine and ancient geography. Its mythic thinking has been connected with the wider tradition of Chinese romantic literature. Its value lies in preserving material that might otherwise have been lost.

Trivia

A book full of creatures. The text records hundreds of different animals. For centuries many scholars read it as a catalogue of real fauna, although today it is understood to mix observation with fantasy.
Yu and the flood. The account of Yu in the Classic is far more fanciful than his portrait in the Book of Documents, where he appears as a sober and methodical ruler.
A text with many versions. Later editions added commentaries and rearrangements, so the form read today is the result of centuries of transmission and editing.
