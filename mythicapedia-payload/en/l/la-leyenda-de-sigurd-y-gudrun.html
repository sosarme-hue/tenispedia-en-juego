J. R. R. Tolkien's verse retelling of Norse heroes and cursed treasure

The Legend of Sigurd and Gudrún

Written in verse by J. R. R. Tolkien and published posthumously in 2009, this work retells the Norse story of Sigurd, the cursed hoard of the Niflungs and the fates of Brynhild and Gudrún. His son Christopher edited it and added extensive notes. It offers a window into the old myths that shaped much of Tolkien's imagination.

The Legend of Sigurd and Gudrún is a work by J. R. R. Tolkien that appeared in 2009, long after his death, thanks largely to the editorial work of his son Christopher, who prepared it and added an introduction by the author along with copious notes. Its poems are written in alliterative verse inspired by the traditional poetry of the North, especially the Poetic Edda, a collection of mythological and heroic poems compiled in the thirteenth century.

The story is tied to the Völsung family, descended from Odin. According to the myth, the gold of Andvari, which Loki seizes by force, carries a curse: whoever possesses it will meet death because of it. The treasure ends up with Fafnir, son of Hreidmar, who turns into a dragon to guard it.

Sigurd, son of the hero Sigmund, is raised by Regin, Fafnir's brother, who urges him to kill the dragon. After several failed attempts, Regin forges for him the sword Gram, made from the fragments of the blade that Odin, disguised as Grimnir, had driven into the oak of a hall. Sigurd wounds Fafnir in the heart from a hiding place. While roasting that heart, he burns a finger, puts it in his mouth and suddenly understands the speech of birds. When Regin approaches with a drawn blade, Sigurd kills him.

The second part tells of Sigurd's meeting with Brynhild, a valkyrie asleep within a ring of fire, whom he awakens. She would only agree to marry a man who had won a kingdom. Later, at the court of the Niflungs, a love potion makes Sigurd forget Brynhild and marry Gudrún. Through a spell, he takes the likeness of Gunnar to ride through the fire and spend the night beside Brynhild with a sword between them. When the deception comes to light, Sigurd is killed by Gutthorm, and Brynhild takes her own life to be burned with him on the pyre.

The new lay of Gudrún returns to the widow, who wanders the forest, and to Atli, king of the Huns, who covets the treasure and Gudrún herself. Through these poems Tolkien explores Norse poetry and the weight of curses within a story that remains intense and mysterious.

Author: J. R. R. Tolkien Date: 2009-

Trivia

A posthumous book. Tolkien wrote these poems in the 1930s, but they were not published until 2009, decades after his death. His son Christopher took charge of editing them.
Sigurd's horse. According to the myth, Grani, Sigurd's horse, is sired by Sleipnir, Odin's eight-legged steed. He carries the hoard alongside his rider.
A shared ending. According to the tale, Sigurd and Brynhild are carried to Valhalla in the flames of a Viking funeral, where they await Ragnarök.
