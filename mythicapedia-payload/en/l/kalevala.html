Elias Lönnrot's Finnish epic

Kalevala

The Kalevala is a long epic in verse that gathers the oral traditions of Finland and Karelia about the creation of the world, heroes and gods. Elias Lönnrot assembled it from songs passed down from singer to singer over generations. Its first publication in 1835 and the expanded edition of 1849 made it a landmark of Finnish culture.

The Kalevala is a long poem that brings together much of the oral tradition of Finland and Karelia, a region straddling the border between the two countries, in the form of an epic. It was never written in one go by a single author. Instead, it is a patchwork of folk songs that circulated in villages and homes, recited by men and women who knew them by heart.

Its compiler was Elias Lönnrot (1802–1884), a physician who was also a linguist and poet. Between 1828 and 1844 he made eleven field trips to listen to folk singers. Two of the most important were Arhippa Perttunen (1769–1840) and Ontrei Malinen (1780–1855). Perttunen is said to have recited for him over two days, providing around 4,000 verses. The songs were often sung to the kantele, a stringed instrument, and were frequently performed by two singers who traded verses back and forth, a kind of singing match known as antiphonic performance.

The first version, known as the Old Kalevala, appeared in 1835 and was considerably shorter. The 1849 edition expanded the work to fifty poems, or runes, with roughly 22,800 verses. An abridged edition with about 9,700 verses followed in 1862. In 1840 Lönnrot also published the Kanteletar, a more lyrical collection of poems often regarded as a sister work to the Kalevala.

When arranging his material, Lönnrot joined songs from different regions, reduced the number of characters and concentrated the settings so that the story would have a clearer shape. The result tells of the creation of the earth and of the disputes and retaliatory journeys between Väinölä, the land of Kalevala, and Pohjola, the northern land. At the heart of the story is the Sampo, a magical device that produces wealth, and its theft sets off new conflicts.

The characters are both human and eccentric. Väinämöinen is a wise and quirky singer, hopelessly in love with the maiden of Pohjola. Lemminkäinen is a reckless suitor who meets his challenges with more daring than good sense. Kullervo, a herdsman, embodies the tragic hero, with enormous vitality weighed down by a fate of perpetual defeat. Ilmarinen is a smith who forges the Sampo, and his birth is so unusual that within hours he has gone from newborn to skilled craftsman. Joukahainen challenges Väinämöinen to a contest of wisdom he is bound to lose. Above them all stands Ukko, the supreme god, whose celestial work is complicated by lesser powers such as Tapio, lord of the forests, and Tuoni, death.

The work has had a notable influence beyond Finland. J. R. R. Tolkien, who read it with interest, is the most often cited example, and traces of the Kalevala have been pointed out in his legendarium, especially in the story of The Children of Húrin. At home, the poem fed the national romanticism of the nineteenth century, in which Karelianism, the enthusiasm for collecting songs from Karelia, played a prominent role. The Kalevala became a symbol of a distinct Finnish identity and was tied to the language dispute between Finnish and Swedish, which accompanied the country's path to independence from Russia in 1917.

Modern scholars have qualified the idea of a uniform ancient past. The folklorist Kaarle Krohn proposed that part of the material had Estonian origins or dealt with motifs of Estonian origin. The age of the themes also varies widely: stories about the origin of the earth may reach very far back, while others, such as the arrival of Christianity, are much more recent. For this reason, the Kalevala is now read as a modern work that preserves a living tradition built up over centuries.

Author: Elias Lönnrot Date: 1835-

Trivia

A work of duets. Many songs were performed by two people trading verses, in a style of singing contest known as antiphonic performance.
Eleven field trips. Lönnrot travelled the region on eleven expeditions over about fifteen years, partly while working as a doctor.
A thesis lost in a fire. Lönnrot's thesis was about Väinämöinen. Its second volume was destroyed in the Great Fire of Turku in the same year.
