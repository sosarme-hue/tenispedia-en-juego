The oldest collection of Vedic hymns

Rigveda

The Rigveda is the oldest of the four Vedas and one of the earliest surviving texts in any Indo-European language. It gathers 1,028 hymns in Vedic Sanskrit, mostly addressed to Agni, Indra and Soma. For centuries it was passed down orally with remarkable precision.

The Rigveda, whose name combines ṛc, meaning praise or sacred verse, and veda, meaning knowledge, is a collection of hymns in Vedic Sanskrit addressed to the gods. It is the oldest of the four Vedas and is counted among the most ancient texts in any Indo-European language. According to tradition, the hymns were composed by sages called rishis, and most are linked to a particular author.

The collection is arranged in ten books known as mandalas, containing about 1,028 hymns and roughly ten thousand verses. Mandalas two to seven, known as the family books, are the oldest and make up around 38 per cent of the text. Each is associated with a family of sages and centres on a principal deity, beginning with Agni. The first and tenth mandalas are the youngest and longest, together accounting for about 37 per cent. They include more reflective passages, such as the well-known creation hymn (10.129).

Among the leading deities is Agni, the sacred fire that links humans with the gods. Indra, a warrior hero, is praised for slaying the serpent Vritra. Soma is both a ritual drink and the plant from which it was pressed. Mitra and Varuna watch over cosmic order, Ushas is the dawn, and the twin Ashvins appear frequently. Dyaus Pita, 'Father Sky', shares an Indo-European root with the Greek Zeus and the Latin Jupiter, a link that scholars have studied since the nineteenth century.

Dating remains debated. Most scholars place the composition of its core in the later second millennium BCE, in the region of the Sapta Sindhu, the 'seven rivers' of the northwestern Indian subcontinent. Some point to a reference to the Mitanni documents of northern Syria and Iraq, around 1350 BCE, which mention Vedic gods. Other proposals differ, and many dates are still provisional.

The text was preserved through an exacting oral discipline. It survives in two principal forms: the samhitapatha, in which words are joined according to the rules of sandhi and which is used for recitation, and the padapatha, in which each word stands alone to aid memorisation. Thanks to this fidelity, the Rigveda preserves an archaic language and metre that are valuable for reconstructing Proto-Indo-European.

Some of its verses are still recited in Hindu prayer and at rites of passage such as weddings. For practising Hindus it is sacred revelation, known as shruti, and its study is part of a living tradition. Since the nineteenth century it has attracted sustained attention from Western scholars, beginning with Max Müller, and it has been translated many times, including Karl Friedrich Geldner's German version of 1923.

Trivia

A hymn to speech. The Rigveda contains a hymn devoted to Vach, speech, which is presented as a divine force present in the universe.
A verse still in use. Verse 3.62.10 is the Gayatri mantra, one of the most recited verses in the Hindu tradition.
No iron. Iron is not mentioned in the text, a detail scholars have used to place its composition before 1000 BCE.
