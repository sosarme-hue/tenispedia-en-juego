A Greek compendium of myths and heroic lineages

Bibliotheca

The Bibliotheca, also known as the Library of Greek Mythology, is a collection of myths, heroic legends and genealogies arranged by lineage, chronology and geography. It is generally dated to the first or second century AD, and its author is usually called Pseudo-Apollodorus. Today it is one of the most consulted sources for how Greek myths were told in antiquity.

The Bibliotheca walks through Greek mythology from the earliest gods and the origin of the world to the stories surrounding the Trojan War. It is not a continuous poem by a single poet but an ordered summary of a great many traditions. Its pages follow the major mythic dynasties, the lines of kings and heroes, and the best-known episodes involving figures such as Perseus, Jason and Heracles. For this reason it has become a standard reference for anyone who wants to understand how Greek myths connected with one another.

The work survives only in part. It was originally arranged in several volumes, and the division most widely used today, proposed by the humanist Benedetto Egio, places the text into three books. The surviving manuscripts break off in the middle of the account of Theseus. The patriarch Photius of Constantinople, who read the complete text in the ninth century, reports that the lost section dealt with heroes of the Trojan War. Later epitomes, or condensed versions, preserve a summary of that final part and allow scholars to reconstruct its general content.

The transmission of the text has a history of its own. Photius is the first reader we know of, and he recorded a review of it in his famous collection of notes on books he had read. In the twelfth century the Byzantine writer John Tzetzes cited it often. It came close to being lost in the thirteenth century and survived in a single manuscript, now incomplete, which was copied for Cardinal Bessarion in the fifteenth century. All surviving copies descend from a fourteenth-century manuscript kept in Paris. The first printed edition appeared in Rome in 1555. Later editions improved on it, among them Christian Gottlob Heyne's text of 1782 to 1783, the first to be based on the comparison of several manuscripts, and Richard Wagner's edition of 1894, long regarded as the standard reference.

The question of authorship has been debated for centuries. Some manuscripts name an author called Apollodorus, and for a long time he was identified with Apollodorus of Athens, a scholar of the late second century BC who studied under Aristarchus of Samothrace. That scholar is known, from references in the minor scholia on Homer, to have written a comprehensive mythological repertory in verse. However, the Bibliotheca cites Castor of Rhodes, a contemporary of Cicero, which makes it impossible for the Athenian scholar to have written it. Carl Robert demonstrated this chronological problem in 1873, and that is why scholars now speak of Pseudo-Apollodorus.

Its value for the study of myth lies in the way it handles variation. The text rarely offers a single version of a story. When ancient sources disagree about a genealogy, the author often leaves the disagreement visible. Homer and Hesiod are the authors named most often, alongside epic poets, Hellenistic mythographers and the tragedians. This habit of recording variants makes the work a valuable witness to how the Greeks themselves understood their traditions. Its influence reaches into modern writing about the classical world, and in the twentieth century the writer Robert Graves drew on it as a source.

Trivia

An epigram by Photius. The patriarch Photius opened his review with an epigram inviting readers to draw their knowledge of the past from this work rather than from other poems or tragedies.
A division into three books. The three-book division still in use comes from the humanist Benedetto Egio, whose printed edition appeared in Rome in 1555.
An early English translation. In 1921 James George Frazer published an English version that combined two manuscript summaries and included the lost section of the text.
