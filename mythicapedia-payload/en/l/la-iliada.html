The wrath of Achilles and the Trojan War

The Iliad

The Iliad is an ancient Greek epic traditionally attributed to Homer. It tells the final weeks of the Trojan War and focuses on the wrath of Achilles, from his quarrel with Agamemnon to the death of Patroclus and his revenge on Hector. It is among the oldest works of literature still widely read today.

The Iliad was composed in dactylic hexameter and runs to about 15,693 lines, divided into 24 books. Its title comes from Ilion, another name for Troy, yet the poem does not narrate the city's fall. Its real subject is the wrath of Achilles, the mēnis that drives the whole story. The action covers 51 days in the tenth and final year of the war, a long siege carried out by a coalition of Greek states against Troy.

The story opens with a quarrel between Agamemnon, leader of the Greek forces, and Achilles, their greatest warrior. Agamemnon refuses to give back Chryseis, the daughter of a priest of Apollo, and the god sends a plague on the camp. To make up for the loss, Agamemnon takes Briseis, the captive who had been awarded to Achilles. Achilles withdraws from battle and asks his mother, the sea nymph Thetis, to persuade Zeus to favour the Trojans until the Greeks realise how much they need him.

From there the poem alternates between massed fighting and quieter scenes. Duels, assemblies and embassies sent to win Achilles back follow one another, while the gods openly take sides. Athena, Hera and Poseidon support the Greeks, whereas Apollo, Ares and Aphrodite favour the Trojans. According to the tradition the poem preserves, gods and mortals share the same world, and this closeness gave ancient audiences a vivid picture of their religious heritage.

The turning point comes with the death of Patroclus, Achilles's friend, at the hands of Hector, the Trojan prince. Patroclus had gone into battle wearing Achilles's armour, and his death awakens a grief that brings the hero back to the fighting. Thetis obtains new weapons forged by Hephaestus, and Achilles, driven by a desire for revenge, kills Hector and drags his body around the walls.

The poem does not end with slaughter but with an act of compassion. Priam, Hector's father, goes alone to Achilles's tent to ask for his son's body, and the hero, thinking of his own father, agrees to return it. The funeral of Hector closes the work, and with it the anger of Achilles comes to an end. The Iliad does not recount the fall of Troy, nor does it mention the Trojan Horse, episodes that belong to later sources.

The date and authorship of the poem are still debated. Most scholars place its composition in the late eighth or early seventh century BCE, though some propose earlier or later dates. It is also disputed whether Homer existed and whether the Iliad and the Odyssey were composed by the same person. This Homeric Question goes back to antiquity and remains open.

In classical Greece the poem was a core part of education. Rhapsodes recited it at festivals such as the Panathenaia, and its blend of regional dialects helped people across the Greek world feel it belonged to them all. Themes such as glory (kleos), pride, fate and wrath remain central to its reading, alongside its detailed picture of ancient warfare and of life both inside and outside the walls of Troy.

Trivia

A title that does not name the ending. Although the poem takes its name from Ilion, the city, the Iliad does not narrate its fall. The story stops during the final weeks of the war.
No horse, no ending. The Trojan Horse does not appear in the Iliad. That episode comes from later sources that complete the story of the war.
