The ancient Egyptian guide to the afterlife

Book of the Dead

The Book of the Dead is the modern name for a collection of magical spells that the ancient Egyptians placed with the deceased, from the New Kingdom until the 1st century BC. Its purpose was to help the dead person pass the judgement of Osiris and reach the paradise of Aaru. Although the spells were credited to the wisdom of Thoth, the collection grew and changed over centuries, copy by copy.

The Book of the Dead is the modern name for a group of funerary texts from ancient Egypt. Most were written on papyrus, in hieroglyphs or hieratic script, and the tradition ran from the beginning of the New Kingdom, around 1550 BC, to about 50 BC. It was never a book with a fixed author or order. It was a loose collection of spells that scribes copied, selected and adapted for each person. The original Egyptian name, rw nw prt m hrw, is usually rendered as Spells of Coming Forth by Day.

Its aim was to guide the deceased through the Duat, the underworld, and to help them through the judgement before Osiris, so that they could reach the paradise of Aaru. According to the tradition, the dead person needed exact words to answer the dangers along the way and to identify themselves to the beings who guarded it. That is why the book was placed in the coffin or the burial chamber, close to the body, rather than displayed for the living.

Its roots are very old. Around 2400 BC, in the pyramid of King Unas, the Pyramid Texts were carved on the walls of the burial chamber. They were reserved for the king and, from the 6th Dynasty, for the queen. Many signs showing humans and animals were left incomplete or damaged, probably so that they could not harm the ruler. Their goal was to reunite the king with his divine father Ra, in an afterlife imagined in the sky. Towards the end of the Old Kingdom, regional governors and other officials began to use them too.

In the Middle Kingdom came the Coffin Texts, painted on the inside of coffins, with new spells and, for the first time, illustrations. Wealthy private people could now afford them, a shift that scholars have described as the democratisation of the afterlife. The Book of the Dead took shape in Thebes at the start of the Second Intermediate Period. The earliest known example comes from the coffin of Queen Mentuhotep of the 16th Dynasty. Some spells claimed a more ancient origin than they really had. The rubric to spell 30B says it was discovered by Prince Hordjedef in the reign of Menkaure, many centuries before there is archaeological evidence for it.

During the New Kingdom the book spread and grew richer. Spell 125, the famous Weighing of the Heart, first appears in the reigns of Hatshepsut and Thutmose III, around 1475 BC. Vignettes became more elaborate, especially in the 19th Dynasty, sometimes at the expense of the text. In the Third Intermediate Period a cheaper hieratic version appeared, written on smaller papyri and usually with a single illustration.

In the 25th and 26th Dynasties the book was revised and standardised, and its spells were ordered and numbered for the first time. Scholars call this the Saite recension. In the Late and Ptolemaic periods it continued in a shorter form, and new funerary texts were created alongside it. Its use faded in the 1st century BC, although some artistic motifs survived into Roman times.

Around 192 spells are known, but no single manuscript contains them all. Many begin with the word ro, which can mean mouth, speech, spell or chapter, reflecting the Egyptian idea that ritual speech carried power. Some spells gave the dead mystical knowledge or identified them with gods, such as spell 17, a long and obscure description of the god Atum. Others protected the body, reunited its parts or gave control over the surrounding world. Spells 26 to 30 relate to the heart and were often inscribed on scarabs.

For the Egyptians, magic and religion were not separate worlds. The concept of heka linked the power of spoken and written words. Hieroglyphs were thought to have been invented by the god Thoth, and they kept their force even when a text was shortened or left out. Knowing the name of something gave power over it, so the book provided the deceased with the secret names of many beings in the afterlife.

The book was organised quite late. Until Paul Barguet's pioneering study of 1967, many Egyptologists believed it had no internal structure at all. From the Saite period onwards, however, copies tend to group the chapters into four blocks: entering the tomb and regaining movement and speech, the mythic origins of gods and places, the nightly voyage in the sun barque, and the vindication of the deceased as one of the gods.

The book also reveals how Egyptians understood death. Death was seen as the breakdown of the kheperu, the various modes of being a person possessed, which rituals had to bring back together. Mummification preserved the body and transformed it into a sah, an idealised and divine form. For that reason some spells were recited during embalming, and the heart, seen as the seat of intelligence and memory, received particular protection.

Trivia

A German name. The modern name scholars use comes from German. In 1842, the Egyptologist Karl Richard Lepsius called these texts Todtenbuch, which was rendered in English as Book of the Dead.
A famous papyrus. The so-called Papyrus of Ani is the finest surviving copy. It was found at Luxor in 1888 by traders in antiquities and is now kept in the British Museum in London.
An invented past. The rubric to spell 30B claims that Prince Dyedefhor discovered it in the reign of Menkaure. This places the text centuries before any archaeological evidence for it, a way of lending the spell prestige.
No complete copy. Around 192 spells are known, yet no single manuscript contains all of them. Each copy was a different selection, sometimes tailored to the wishes of whoever commissioned it.
