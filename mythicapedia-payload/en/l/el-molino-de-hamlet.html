An essay on myth and the frame of time

Hamlet's Mill

Hamlet's Mill is an essay by Giorgio de Santillana and Hertha von Dechend, first published in 1969, that gathers myths from many cultures to argue that knowledge of the precession of the equinoxes may have survived for millennia in the form of stories. The book sits between the history of science and comparative mythology, and it has drawn both admiration and sharp academic criticism.

The title comes from Amlóða kvern, a poetic term recorded in the Old Icelandic Skáldskaparmál, which refers to a mill linked to Amleth, the hero of the legend that inspired Shakespeare. For the authors, that mill is the key to a wider myth: a great celestial wheel turning around the pole, which appears in varying forms across very different traditions.

Santillana was a professor of the history of science at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, while von Dechend held a professorship at Johann Wolfgang Goethe University in Frankfurt. They met at a symposium in Frankfurt in 1958 and began working together in 1959. Von Dechend had trained with Leo Frobenius at his museum of ethnology, and her ethnological interests shaped much of the book. Santillana had previously written on the earliest roots of rational thought. The work took about a decade to write, and some reviewers felt that it was largely von Dechend's achievement, since Santillana became seriously ill in the mid-1960s.

The book makes three main claims: that the precession of the equinoxes was discovered in the late Neolithic or earlier, that a megalith-building civilization made the observations needed for that discovery, and that this knowledge was encoded in myths about mills, maelstroms, the Milky Way and celestial succession. The authors draw on African myths collected by Marcel Griaule, the Persian Shahnameh, Plato, Pindar and Plutarch, the Finnish Kalevala, the Norse Eddas, the Mahabharata, the Vedas and Upanishads, Babylonian astrology and the Sumerian Gilgamesh. Their method relies on parallels as well as coincidences, and this is where much of the controversy lies.

Reception was divided. Critics such as Jaan Puhvel and Edmund Leach questioned arguments built on outdated or incorrect linguistic information, an excess of analogy, and the lack of solid evidence for a far-reaching civilization. Others were more generous. The astrophysicist Philip Morrison, a friend of Santillana, called it a book "for the wise", and the Swedish astronomer Peter Nilson credited it as an inspiration for his own study of night-sky mythologies.

The authors also reflect on method. They distrusted modern interpretations of folklore, which they found shallow, and preferred to rely on earlier scholars and on the mythologists of past centuries. That stance, together with their interest in how knowledge survives the loss of writing, keeps the book a point of reference for anyone exploring the relationship between myth and astronomy.

Trivia

A title with history. The name comes from an Old Icelandic term, Amlóða kvern, which refers to a mill linked to Amleth, the same figure who inspired Shakespeare.
Two authors, two origins. Santillana worked at MIT and von Dechend at the University of Frankfurt; they met at a symposium in 1958 and began collaborating the following year.
An expanded edition. The 2000 Italian edition grew from 552 to 630 pages, showing that the work kept expanding after its first publication.
