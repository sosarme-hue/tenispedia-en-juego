The lost poem about the war between the Olympians and the Titans

Titanomachy

The Titanomachy was an epic poem from ancient Greece, now lost, that told of the struggle between Zeus and his siblings against their father Cronus and the Titan generation. Today it survives only through quotations and fragments preserved by other authors. It is valuable for showing how ancient poets could vary the same story.

The Titanomachy was an epic poem of Greek tradition that has been lost. It survives through quotations and fragments transmitted by other writers. Its subject is the war between Zeus and his siblings, the Olympian gods, and their father Cronus along with the generation of the Titans. According to the myth, that conflict gave rise to the cosmic order the Greeks knew, with the victors ruling from Olympus.

Tradition attributed the poem to Eumelus of Corinth, a semi-legendary bard of the Bacchiad ruling family in archaic Corinth. He was also credited as the composer of the Prosodion, a processional anthem performed on Delos. Yet many ancient authors cited the work without naming any poet. The scholar M. L. West argues that the epic was likely composed in the late seventh or early sixth century BC, far too late for Eumelus, and that his name was attached simply because it was the only one available.

The fragments suggest that Eumelus's account departed from Hesiod's Theogony at important points. In Hesiod, Aegaeon, also called Briareus or Gyges, was a son of Uranus and Gaia. Eumelus instead makes him a son of Gaia and Pontus, a sea-dwelling figure who fought alongside the Titans. The same figure is also said to own the pillars of Heracles. In another fragment, Cronus joins with Philyra in the form of a horse, and from this origin comes the mixed nature of Chiron, the wise centaur who taught mortals the oaths and sacrifices owed to the gods of Olympus.

The poem also contained cosmic and narrative scenes. The horses of Helios and the Horae bore names such as Bronte, meaning thunder, and Sterope, meaning lightning. One fragment shows Zeus dancing among the gods in the manner of the Curetes' war dance, and another shows him about to strike a Titan, held back when Phoebus comes to help. The work is also credited with the first account of Helios traveling along the horizon in a golden cauldron.

Its place in tradition is perhaps its greatest interest today. According to ancient readers, the poem was preceded by a theogony, and the Byzantine writer Lydus noted that its author placed the birth of Zeus not on Crete but in Lydia, on Mount Sipylus. Like much early Greek literature, it survives only in fragments, yet they are enough to show how far the retellings of a single story could diverge.

Author: Eumelus of Corinth

Trivia

A different birthplace. According to the Byzantine writer Lydus, the author of the Titanomachy placed the birth of Zeus in Lydia, on Mount Sipylus, rather than on Crete as was common.
A poet older than the poem. Eumelus's name became attached to the poem, but scholar M. L. West dates the work to well after the period in which the poet is placed.
Helios in a cauldron. One fragment credits the Titanomachy with the first mention of Helios traveling across the horizon in a golden cauldron.
