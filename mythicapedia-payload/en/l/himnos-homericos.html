A collection of ancient Greek poems honouring the gods

Homeric Hymns

The Homeric Hymns are a group of short poems in dactylic hexameter that praise deities of the Greek pantheon and retell episodes of their birth, their place on Olympus or the founding of their cults. Although antiquity attributed them to Homer, modern scholars regard them as the work of several poets, mostly composed between the seventh and sixth centuries BCE. Their language and style link them closely to the Iliad and the Odyssey.

The Homeric Hymns are a collection of thirty-three hymns and one epigram in ancient Greek, all devoted to the gods of Olympus. Their length varies enormously: some run to only three or four lines, while the Hymn to Demeter and the Hymn to Hermes are far longer. The title comes from the ancient belief that Homer, the poet of the Iliad and the Odyssey, had composed them. Many Greeks accepted this, though not everyone did. The writer Athenaeus voiced doubts around 200 CE, and some ancient biographies of Homer denied he wrote them. Today scholars agree that several poets, working at different times, were responsible.

The links with Homeric epic are clear. The hymns share the same artificial literary language, a blend of the Aeolic and Ionic dialects that nobody spoke in everyday life, and the same dactylic hexameter. They also rely on formulae, short and stable phrases a singer could repeat to compose quickly. Yet their storytelling differs. In epic, the gods often help explain the fates of heroes; in the hymns the gods themselves stand at the centre, and mortals appear mainly as witnesses to their deeds. Scholars have also noted that the hymns focus on single events and cover a shorter span of time, giving their narration a more measured pace.

These poems had a practical purpose. They were sung as a prelude, or proem, before a longer piece, perhaps a heroic epic, and many end by announcing that another song will follow. Over time some may have been performed on their own. The short hymns we still have may be abridged versions of longer works, keeping the opening and closing but losing the central story. Singers accompanied themselves on a lyre or a similar stringed instrument, and performances may have taken place at banquets, religious festivals and royal courts. Some hymns ask the god for support in a contest, which suggests they were also sung at competitions.

In content, the hymns usually recount a deity's birth, arrival on Olympus and dealings with humans. Several explain the origin of a cult, the foundation of an important sanctuary or particular rituals. Claude Calame has described them as a kind of contract: the praise offered to the god seeks favour or protection in return for the singer or the community. This role as an offering helps explain why so many focus on a specific place of worship.

Their influence on Greek literature was modest until the third century BCE, when Alexandrian poets such as Callimachus, Theocritus and Apollonius of Rhodes drew on them. Roman writers, among them Lucretius, Catullus, Virgil, Horace and Ovid, also read them. In late antiquity they shaped both pagan and Christian writing. In the Byzantine period they were somewhat neglected, though they continued to be copied alongside the epics. In fifteenth-century Italy they were read with enthusiasm, and their indirect echoes are thought to appear in Botticelli's The Birth of Venus. The first printed edition came out in 1488–1489, and textual scholarship advanced greatly in the nineteenth century, particularly in Germany.

The hymns also inspired English Romantic poets such as Shelley and Leigh Hunt, and later writers including Tennyson, Cavafy and James Joyce. Their influence has also been traced in Hitchcock's film Rear Window and in Neil Gaiman's novel Coraline. A collection of hymns with no clear author has thus remained a point of reference for poetry and fiction across many periods.

Author: Homer

Trivia

An inherited name. They are called Homeric because antiquity attributed them to Homer, although today they are known not to have been written by him.
Rediscovered in 1777. The Hymn to Demeter sparked renewed European interest when it was rediscovered in 1777, encouraging new studies of the collection.
Inspiration for Goethe. Johann Wolfgang von Goethe drew on the Hymn to Demeter for his 1778 melodrama Proserpina.
