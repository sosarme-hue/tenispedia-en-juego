Hesiod's account of the origin of the gods

Theogony

The Theogony is a poem attributed to Hesiod that tells how the cosmos and the generations of Greek gods came into being. It combines cosmogony, genealogies and succession stories, such as the overthrow of Uranus by Cronus and of Cronus by Zeus. It remains one of the oldest and most complete sources for Greek mythology.

The Theogony (from the Greek Theogonía, roughly 'the origin of the gods') is a poem of about a thousand lines attributed to Hesiod, a Greek poet usually dated to the late eighth or early seventh century BC. It is one of the oldest surviving accounts of how the world began in Greek tradition, and for that reason it is a constant reference point for anyone studying Greek myth.

The poem opens with a prelude addressed to the Muses. Hesiod explains that they taught him to sing while he was tending sheep at the foot of Mount Helicon, and he asks them to guide his words about the gods. The structure resembles that of the Homeric Hymns: a statement of theme, the account of an episode, and a closing appeal. In the poem the Muses also give him a scepter, a sign of authority, so the poet's voice takes on the kind of standing that kings held in other societies.

The cosmogony follows. In the beginning, according to the account, there were Chaos, Gaia (Earth), the gloomy Tartarus and Eros. From Chaos came Erebus and Nyx (Night), and from Nyx, without a father, came figures such as Sleep, Death, Fate and Strife. Gaia bore Uranus (Sky), with whom she had the Titans, the Cyclopes and the Hecatoncheires, the beings with a hundred hands.

Uranus hated his children and hid them inside Gaia. Distressed, she made a sickle of adamant and asked her offspring to punish their father. Only Cronus, the youngest of the Titans, agreed. When Uranus came to lie with Gaia, Cronus castrated him with the sickle. From the blood came the Erinyes, the Giants and the Meliae, and the severed genitals, thrown into the sea, gave rise to Aphrodite, who in the myth emerged from the foam near Cyprus.

Cronus took power, but he had been warned that one of his children would overthrow him. To prevent this, he swallowed each child Rhea bore. When Zeus was born, Rhea hid him on Crete and gave Cronus a stone wrapped in swaddling clothes, which he swallowed, believing it was the baby. Once grown, Zeus forced his father to release his brothers and sisters. With the help of the Cyclopes, who had been kept imprisoned, he received the thunderbolt, and the Titanomachy began, the war between the new gods and the Titans. After ten years of fighting, and with the intervention of the Hundred-Handers, Zeus won and hurled the Titans into Tartarus.

There was still Typhon, a son of Gaia, a monster who threatened the newly established order, but he too was defeated and imprisoned. Zeus then married Metis, yet when he learned that a son born to her might overthrow him, he swallowed her. According to the poem, Athena was later born from his head. With Zeus, the poem presents the end of violent succession and the establishment of a stable order.

The rest of the work unfolds long genealogies: the Nereids, daughters of Nereus and Doris; Iris, daughter of Thaumas and Electra; the Graeae and the Gorgons, children of Phorcys and Ceto; and Echidna, half woman and half serpent, mother of creatures such as Cerberus, the Hydra and the Chimera. Helios, Selene and Eos appear, along with the winds and the rivers. There are notable digressions, including a hymn to Hecate, a description of Tartarus, and the story of Prometheus, who tricks Zeus at a sacrifice and, as punishment, sees the god withhold fire from humans until he secretly brings it back.

The poem ends with Zeus's unions with goddesses and mortal women, which produce figures such as Hermes, Dionysus and Heracles, followed by a catalogue of heroes and women. Many scholars think the ending is less secure than the rest and may have been expanded later.

The Theogony should not be seen as a closed source for Greek mythology, but as a snapshot of a living tradition that kept changing. Some researchers compare it with texts from the Near East, such as the Hittite myth of Kumarbi, which contains a castration motif resembling the Uranus and Cronus story. Whatever its origins, the poem left a lasting mark on how the Greeks, and later Western culture, imagined the origin of the world and the power of the gods.

Author: Hesiod Date: -0700

Trivia

A scepter from the Muses. In the prelude, the Muses give Hesiod a scepter, a symbol usually linked to royal authority. The poet thus claims a standing similar to that of kings.
A Hittite parallel. The decipherment of Hittite myths, including the text known as Kingship in Heaven, reveals a castration motif similar to the Uranus and Cronus story, through the figure of Kumarbi.
A poem of over a thousand lines. The work has 1,022 lines in the tradition that has survived, making it a substantial yet manageable poem within archaic epic.
