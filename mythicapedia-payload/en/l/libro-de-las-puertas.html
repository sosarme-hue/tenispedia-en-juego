The night journey of the deceased with the sun god Ra

Book of Gates

The Book of Gates is a New Kingdom funerary text that follows the nocturnal journey of the soul through the Duat, the Egyptian underworld. At each hour, the deceased must pass a gate guarded by a serpent and a goddess, and must know their names. It is known for its scene of four groups of humanity entering the otherworld.

The Book of Gates is an ancient Egyptian funerary text dating from the New Kingdom. The title did not come from the Egyptians themselves. It was given by the French Egyptologist Gaston Maspero, who called it Livre des Portes. The book describes the nightly voyage of the deceased through the Duat, the underworld, travelling alongside the sun god Ra during the twelve hours of the night until his rebirth at dawn.

According to the story, the spirit of the dead must pass through a gate at the end of each hour. Every gate is guarded by a serpent deity linked to a different goddess, and to get through, the deceased must know each guardian's name and character. The goddesses look much alike: they differ in title and in the colour of their garments, but all wear stars on their heads. Because most of them appear nowhere else in Egyptian texts, some researchers have suggested that the system may have served to mark the night cycle, with a leading star for each hour.

The text hints at different fates. Some souls, the blessed, continue unharmed, while others, the damned, are destroyed by serpents or suffer in a lake of fire. The final hours include scenes of judgement, presided over by Osiris, in which the soul is weighed against evil.

One of the most famous scenes appears in the fifth hour. Horus presides over men from four groups, Egyptians, Asiatics, Libyans and Nubians, shown in procession into the otherworld. It has often been read as an image of universal welcome, although its precise meaning remains debated.

Its influence was considerable. Scenes from the book decorate many New Kingdom tombs, including those of the pharaohs from Horemheb to Ramesses VII. Horemheb was the first to place it on the walls of his burial chamber, breaking the earlier custom of using the Amduat there. It also appears in the tomb of a worker from Deir el-Medina, the village of artisans who built the royal tombs, and in that of a priest of Amun. Outside royal circles it was less common, and it often accompanied the Book of the Dead.

It is closely related to the Amduat, which also follows Ra through the underworld. According to Mohamed Ragheb Dardir, the Book of Gates focuses on the dangers the soul will meet and on what must be done to pass each gate and be resurrected. The two works were often painted together, since neither was considered sufficient on its own. One striking difference is the crew of the solar barque: the Amduat lists a larger one, while here only Heka and Sia travel with Ra for the entire journey.

Trivia

One hundred scenes. The book is made up of about one hundred images arranged in three registers for each hour, each with an explanatory text.
A modern name. The Egyptians did not give it this title. The Egyptologist Gaston Maspero named it in French.
A small crew. Only Heka and Sia travel with Ra in the barque for the whole journey, unlike in the Amduat.
