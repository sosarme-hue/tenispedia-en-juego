The great epic of Dionysus in Late Antiquity

Dionysiaca

The Dionysiaca is a long Greek poem written by Nonnus of Panopolis around the middle of the fifth century AD. It follows the life of Dionysus, with particular attention to his expedition to India. It is one of the largest surviving collections of myth from antiquity.

Nonnus of Panopolis, an Egyptian poet who wrote in Greek, composed this epic of forty-eight books, usually dated to around 450 to 470 AD. With more than twenty-one thousand lines, it is the longest surviving poem from Greco-Roman antiquity. Its story moves from the birth of Dionysus through his childhood to his great campaign in India and his triumphant return.

The tale begins long before the god is born. According to the tradition Nonnus follows, Zeus carried off Europa, and her father sent Cadmus to search for her. The story then links up with Typhon, the monster who stole Zeus' thunderbolts, and with the founding of Thebes. Cadmus and Harmonia have children, among them Semele, the mother of Dionysus, so the poem weaves together several genealogies of the Theban family.

Another central episode is that of Zagreus, an earlier Dionysus, son of Zeus and Persephone, who was killed by the Titans. After Semele's tragic end, Zeus sewed the infant into his own thigh until he was ready to be born. Nonnus lingers over such stories with a fondness for detail that many earlier sources do not share.

The Indian war fills most of the poem, from Book 13 to Book 40. Dionysus advances with his retinue, and the campaign is told through a mix of battles, landscapes and love affairs. The god wins less by brute force than through wine, dance and music.

As a witness to its time, the poem matters greatly to historians of ideas. Nonnus lived when polytheism still coexisted with Christianity, and his verses show Neoplatonic traces, solar imagery and Christian allusions. He also wrote a verse paraphrase of the Gospel of John, which most scholars now place earlier than the Dionysiaca.

In style, Nonnus keeps the Homeric hexameter but handles it with strict metrical discipline and a vocabulary full of coinages. He mixes epic, hymns, pastoral passages and short tales, and arranges his episodes by variety rather than strict chronology, much like Ovid's Metamorphoses. His influence reached Byzantium and the Renaissance, and today he remains a valuable source for lost traditions.

Trivia

A title with history. The poem is also known as the Bassarica, a name that refers to the bassarids, the Maenads of Dionysus. The title reflects the wide range of Dionysiac traditions gathered in it.
Forty-eight books. The number of books alludes to Homer, since the Iliad and the Odyssey have twenty-four each, adding up to forty-eight.
A poet from Panopolis. Panopolis, today Akhmim, was one of the strongholds of polytheism in Upper Egypt in Nonnus' time, and it produced several notable philosophers and poets.
