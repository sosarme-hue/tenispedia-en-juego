The Chinese novel of the monk, the monkey and the pilgrimage to India

Journey to the West

Journey to the West is one of the great classic Chinese novels, published during the Ming dynasty and attributed to Wu Cheng'en. It follows the pilgrimage of the monk Tang Sanzang and his disciples as they seek Buddhist scriptures in India, blending adventure, humour and spiritual allegory. Its best-loved character, the Monkey King, has become an icon of Chinese culture.

Journey to the West is rooted in a real journey. In the seventh century, the monk Xuanzang travelled through Central Asia and India in search of Buddhist texts, and returned to China after more than a decade on the road. His account, the Great Tang Records on the Western Regions, provides the broad outline of the novel. Its author, or authors, filled that framework with folk tales, fantastic creatures and comic episodes. The story we know today is therefore a blend of travel chronicle, mythology and satire.

The hundred-chapter version printed in 1592 in Nanjing is the earliest complete edition that survives. Oral storytelling about Xuanzang's journey circulated for centuries before that, and earlier printed versions existed, though only fragments remain. The novel is traditionally attributed to Wu Cheng'en, but scholars debate how far that attribution can be trusted. Some see the work as the product of a long collective process, while others question whether the documentary evidence is as firm as was once believed.

The novel falls into four unequal parts. The first, seven chapters long, works as a self-contained prologue. It tells how the monkey Sun Wukong is born from a stone animated by the Five Elements, learns the Tao, the seventy-two transformations and the secrets of immortality, and wins the title Qitian Dasheng, the Great Sage Equal to Heaven. His pride brings him into conflict with the celestial bureaucracy, and he is finally trapped beneath a mountain by the Buddha and sealed there for five hundred years.

The second part introduces Tang Sanzang, a monk whose past life was bound up with the Buddha through a disciple known as the Golden Cicada. Dismayed by the greed and sin of the world, the Buddha asks Guanyin to find someone who can bring the sutras east. Emperor Taizong sends the monk on the pilgrimage, after a backstory in which the emperor was saved from death with the help of an official of the underworld.

The third part, the longest, covers chapters 13 to 99. Here Tang Sanzang gathers his disciples. Sun Wukong is impulsive and powerful, and can be controlled only by a golden ring that tightens around his head when the monk chants a mantra. Zhu Bajie is a former celestial marshal banished for his appetites and clumsiness. Sha Wujing is a calm, dependable river ogre. The dragon prince, transformed into a white horse, carries the monk. The dangers are constant: demons who want to eat the monk, since his flesh was believed to confer immortality; animal spirits in human guise; and fantastical kingdoms. Many of these monsters turn out to be heavenly beings who have strayed from their posts, and the text hints that many of the trials were arranged by fate or by the Buddha himself.

The ending brings the travellers to Vulture Peak, where they receive the scriptures. In the final chapter each of them is rewarded according to the path they have followed. Sun Wukong and Tang Sanzang attain Buddhahood, Sha Wujing becomes an arhat, and Zhu Bajie is named a cleaner of altars.

Beyond the adventure, the work is an allegory. The Monkey King stands for the restless mind and impulse, Zhu Bajie for basic appetites, and Tang Sanzang for the heart that seeks truth. Many readers therefore see it as a metaphor for spiritual transformation. It has also been read as a satire of imperial bureaucracy, though that interpretation remains debated.

The novel has had a long afterlife. It has been adapted for film, television and animation in China and abroad, and it has inspired manhwa, manga and anime series. Its pantheon of immortals and bodhisattvas, with their wordplay on names, remains a model for fantasy literature in East Asia.

Author: Wu Cheng'en Date: 1592-

Trivia

A real journey. The monk Xuanzang spent about thirteen years travelling in India in the seventh century and left an account of his journey that forms the basis of the novel.
Monkey in the West. The British scholar Arthur Waley published an abridged translation titled Monkey in 1942, which introduced the story to English-speaking readers.
A monkey with seventy-two transformations. In the novel Sun Wukong learns seventy-two transformations, a figure that recurs as a symbol of his magical power.
