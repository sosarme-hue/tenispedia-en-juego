A modern Breton lay between chivalry and Christian faith

The Lay of Aotrou and Itroun

Tolkien wrote this 508-line poem in 1930, using the metre of the Breton lay, and it was published in 1945. It tells of a noble couple who pay dearly for a bargain with a korrigan, and it sets the code of chivalry against Christian values. The Lay of Aotrou and Itroun is a poem of 508 lines written by J. R. R. Tolkien in 1930. It appeared in December 1945 in The Welsh Review, a magazine published in Cardiff. The two names in the title are Breton words meaning “lord” and “lady”. Tolkien shaped the poem on the Breton lay, a narrative form that was popular in Middle English literature.

The story begins with a childless noble couple. Aotrou seeks help from a witch, and a white hind leads him to her. Later, just after Itroun has given birth to twins, the witch returns and reveals herself as a korrigan, a creature of Breton folklore. Her price is Aotrou’s love. He agrees, then breaks his word, placing Christian duty above the code of chivalry. The korrigan curses him to die within three days. Aotrou accepts the consequences and places his hope in Providence. He dies when the three days are over, Itroun dies of grief, and the two are buried together, never living to see their children grow up.

The poem explores the clash between heroic chivalry and Christianity, and how both relate to marriage. Its setting can be estimated from line 429, where “the white priests” sing the Dirige. These are probably the White Monks, the Cistercians, an order founded in 1098 that expanded rapidly in the early twelfth century under Bernard of Clairvaux and reached Brittany at that time. This background fits the metre Tolkien chose.

The poem invites comparison with “The Franklin’s Tale” by Geoffrey Chaucer, one of The Canterbury Tales, written some five centuries earlier. Both share a similar starting point, but their endings point in opposite directions. Chaucer’s Dorigen sets aside her Christian scruples to meet the demands of chivalric honour, and keeps her happiness. Aotrou refuses the code so that he will not sin again, and gives up his earthly happiness, along with that of his wife and sons, to save his immortal soul.

Trivia

Fifteen years in waiting. Tolkien wrote the poem in 1930, but it was not published until December 1945, in The Welsh Review.
A white hind as guide. Aotrou reaches the witch guided by a white hind, an image that opens the story with the tone of a fable.
The white monks. Line 429 mentions the Dirige sung by the white priests, probably the Cistercians, an order founded in 1098.
