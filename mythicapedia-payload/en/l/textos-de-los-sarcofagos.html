The funerary spells that opened the afterlife to more Egyptians

Coffin Texts

The Coffin Texts are some 1,185 funerary spells, mostly inscribed on Middle Kingdom coffins from around 2100 BCE. They draw partly on the older Pyramid Texts, but adapt them for a wider audience and focus on the underworld ruled by Osiris.

The Coffin Texts are a collection of some 1,185 funerary spells, written mostly on coffins of the Middle Kingdom. The modern name comes from that surface, although the same spells also appeared on tomb walls, stelae, canopic chests, papyri and mummy masks. Their beginnings are dated to around 2100 BCE, during the First Intermediate Period, when Egyptian funerary tradition was changing.

To understand where they came from, we have to look back to the Pyramid Texts, carved around 2350 BCE in the burial chambers of Old Kingdom pharaohs. Those formulas were reserved for royalty, the only people who, according to that view, could hope for life after death. The Coffin Texts took over much of that material, but they were reworked for a broader public: the nobility and, to some extent, ordinary people who could afford a coffin.

The shift in emphasis is one of their most striking features. The Pyramid Texts looked mainly towards the sky and the journey of kings among the gods, with the sun god Ra at the centre. The Coffin Texts give more weight to the subterranean world ruled by Osiris, known as the Duat. There the deceased faces threatening beings, traps and snares, and the spells help them protect themselves and avoid what the texts call a second death. In this way Osiris becomes a lord and judge of the afterlife, a figure who would later sit at the heart of Egyptian funerary religion.

Another novelty is the notion that all people will be judged by Osiris and his council according to their deeds in life. The texts refer to a balance, an image that would later become the decisive moment of judgement in the Book of the Dead. Alongside this, the spells address everyday worries. Some aim to spare the deceased from manual labour in the next world, a concern that makes sense in a society where most people worked the land.

The texts also describe the land of the dead and its inhabitants. They mention the Sekhet Hotep, the Field of Offerings or of Peace, the paths of Rostau and the abode of Osiris. Some spells mix protective ritual actions, wishes for a blessed existence and references to the transformations of the ba and the akh, two aspects of the person that can travel and change form. These are texts in which magic, prayer and geographical description live side by side.

Among the spells there are passages spoken by the god Ra, such as number 1130, and others in which the deceased replies, such as number 1031. This alternation of voices suggests that the spells were understood as a kind of ritual dialogue that the dead person had to know how to recite at the right moment.

A few coffins from the Middle Egyptian necropolis of el-Bersheh add something unusual: graphic representations of the realm of the dead, accompanied by spells about the journey through the Duat. This collection is known as the Book of Two Ways and is regarded as the first known map of the Egyptian underworld. It shows two routes, one by land and one by water, separated by a lake of fire, both leading to Rostau and the abode of Osiris. The oldest known copy belonged to a woman named Ankh, who lived during the reign of the nomarch Ahanakht I.

The influence of this material was long lasting. The Book of Two Ways is a precursor of the underworld books of the New Kingdom, and some of the Coffin Texts were later copied into the Book of the Dead, where descriptions of routes through the afterlife remain a constant theme. For scholars, these texts show how the hope of an existence after death ceased to be a royal privilege and became a prospect open to a much wider part of Egyptian society.

Date: -2100

Trivia

A name taken from the coffin. The modern name comes from the object on which most of the spells were written, Middle Kingdom coffins. The same spells also appear on papyri and mummy masks.
The first map of the underworld. The Book of Two Ways, preserved on coffins from el-Bersheh, is regarded as the first known map of the Egyptian underworld, with land and water routes divided by a lake of fire.
A balance before the famous judgement. The Coffin Texts already refer to a balance in the judgement of the dead. That image would later play a central role in the Book of the Dead.
