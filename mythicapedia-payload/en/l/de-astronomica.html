A book of myths behind the constellations

De Astronomica

De Astronomica, also known as Poeticon Astronomicon, is a collection of stories explaining the mythological origins of nearly all the constellations of the northern sky and some of the southern ones. It is attributed to "Hyginus", although its authorship has been debated since the Renaissance. Its main value today lies in preserving Greek and Roman tales that might otherwise be lost.

De Astronomica, or De astronomia in Latin, is a book of tales that links each constellation to the myth that, according to tradition, placed its figure in the sky. It circulates under the name "Hyginus", but the real author is uncertain. During the Renaissance it was credited to the Roman historian Gaius Julius Hyginus, who oversaw the Palatine library under Augustus. Other scholars have pointed out that the work's rough style makes that attribution unlikely, and that its star lists follow exactly the same order as Ptolemy's Almagest, written in the 2nd century. For this reason many consider it a later compilation, perhaps by an author called Hyginus or Pseudo-Hyginus.

The text covers 47 of the 48 constellations listed by Ptolemy. Much of the material draws on the Catasterismi, a lost work traditionally attributed to Eratosthenes of Cyrene, although the author of De Astronomica abridges and reorganises it in his own way. The stories centre mainly on Greek and Roman mythology: the origin of Andromeda, the adventures of Perseus, or the fate of the figures that tradition turned into stars. Although the work mentions some star positions, it is not a reliable guide to the sky.

The first printed edition appeared in 1475, credited to "Ferrara", and the first illustrated edition was published in Venice in 1482 by Erhard Ratdolt. It carried the full title Clarissimi Viri Hyginii Poeticon Astronomicon Opus Utilissimum. Ratdolt commissioned woodcuts of the constellations, with the positions of various stars overlaid on each figure. These images, however, bear little resemblance either to the text's descriptions or to the real sky. Even so, they served as templates for the constellation figures in later star atlases.

The book's real value lies in its text. In some cases it is the only surviving source for obscure Greek myths, which makes it an important document for scholars of ancient mythology and of the history of astronomy.

Author: Gaius Julius Hyginus

Trivia

A disputed authorship. For centuries the author was thought to be the Roman scholar Gaius Julius Hyginus, but the work's style and its star order, identical to the Almagest, lead many to think it is a later compilation.
Stars that do not match. The 1482 woodcuts placed the stars in positions very different from the real sky, so the book is of little use as a guide for stargazing.
Myths preserved only here. For some obscure Greek stories, De Astronomica is the only source that has survived.
