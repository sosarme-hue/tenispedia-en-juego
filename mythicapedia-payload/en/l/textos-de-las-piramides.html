The oldest spells for reaching the stars

Pyramid Texts

The Pyramid Texts are the oldest known corpus of ancient Egyptian religious writing. They are spells, incantations and pleas carved inside the royal burial chambers of the late Old Kingdom to help the pharaoh in the Duat, the realm of the afterlife, and to secure his resurrection. Their content mixes solar and star-based myths with rituals that show how the Egyptians imagined death.

The Pyramid Texts are a collection of religious spells carved on the corridors, antechambers and burial chambers of the pyramids at Saqqara. They were inscribed from the end of the Fifth Dynasty and throughout the Sixth, and some examples reach into the Eighth Dynasty of the First Intermediate Period. Their purpose was to accompany the pharaoh on his journey through the Duat and to guarantee his resurrection and eternal life.

They do not form an orderly story. They are closer to a patchwork of fragments drawn from many sources: accounts of creation, episodes from the struggle between Horus and Seth, legends and, above all, formulas that let the king be reborn, rise to the sky and be identified with the gods. The texts are written in Old Egyptian, and their roots go further back than the pyramids themselves. Some appear already on stelae and mastabas of the first two dynasties, and they were probably circulating on papyrus long before they were cut into stone. The earliest surviving durable copy is in the pyramid of Unas, the last king of the Fifth Dynasty, whose pyramid the Egyptians called “Perfect are the places of Death”.

Two cosmologies sit side by side in these spells. The first is solar: the king is guided towards Ra, the sun god. The second is astral and much older: the path leads to the circumpolar stars, which never set and therefore seemed immortal. The texts describe the many ways the pharaoh could move, by ramps, stairs and ladders, or by flying. They also include formulas to avoid dangerous animals and hazards along the way, and to become the most powerful akh among those who dwelt in the Duat. Some spells even threaten the gods if they refuse to cooperate. Many were written in the first person, although some were later changed to the third person, depending on who recited them and for whom.

Another major group is ritual. According to the tradition, the ceremony of the Opening of the Mouth and Eyes restored to the deceased the power to eat, speak, breathe and see in the next world. The chief lector priest, the Kher-heb, recited the spells with his assistants, while mourners were encouraged to wail and special tools were used. Offering texts listed food, drink and clothing. Some demand that the king take control of his own food supply and claim nourishment from the gods, as in the pyramid of Unas. There are also protective spells against threats to the body and the tomb. These are the most archaic and the hardest to interpret.

The pyramid of Unas is the best-preserved example. Its subterranean walls carry 283 spells, the smallest and best-preserved corpus of the Old Kingdom. Its layout includes a sloping corridor, granite portcullises, an antechamber, a serdab for statues and the burial chamber with the sarcophagus. The causeway that linked it to the valley temple ran about 750 metres. The recitation order often began in the valley temple, which held a sanctuary for offerings, and ended in the coffin or the main chamber of the pyramid. Copies of almost all these spells later appeared in Middle Kingdom tombs, including the near-complete replica in the tomb of the High Priest Senwosretankh at El-Lisht.

The modern rediscovery began in 1880, when the French Egyptologist Gaston Maspero, who directed the French Institute for Oriental Archaeology in Cairo, excavated at Saqqara. He found hieroglyphic inscriptions in the pyramid of Pepi I, but Auguste Mariette, the director of excavations, concluded that the building must be a mastaba, since no writing had ever been found in a pyramid. Maspero then found the same texts in the pyramid of Merenre I. He published the first corpus in French in 1894 as Les inscriptions des pyramides de Saqqarah. Kurt Sethe translated the texts into German between 1908 and 1910, and his concordance became the standard reference. Raymond O. Faulkner offered an English version in 1969. Today around 759 spells are known.

Over time these formulas changed. During the First Intermediate Period they began to appear on the coffins of nobles and officials, which gave rise to the Coffin Texts. In the New Kingdom they were written on papyri placed beside the dead, and from these came the Book of the Dead. Immortality, once the privilege of the pharaoh, became available to the elite and to the righteous Egyptians who could recite these formulas and pay for embalming and burial.

Trivia

A pyramid without writing. Mariette, the excavation director, thought Pepi I's structure was a mastaba, because no writing had ever been found inside a pyramid.
From kings to nobles. The Coffin Texts began to be written during the First Intermediate Period on the coffins of nobles and officials.
